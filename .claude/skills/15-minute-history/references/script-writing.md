# Writing the script

The script is the video. Everything on screen is timed to its words, so get it right before voicing: a change
after voicing means re-voicing that chapter and re-checking its anchors.

## 1. Length and shape

- **Target 15:00–15:30 finished.** The narration runs about 185 words a minute at the locked pace, and the intro,
  title card and logo breaks add about 35 seconds. So aim for **2,700–2,800 words** of narration.
  *King Andrew*: 2,757 words → 14:53 of narration → 15:30 finished.
- **10–11 chapters**, one idea each, 30 seconds to 2:20 long. Short chapters (30–45 s) are fine for a single beat.
  A chapter is the unit of re-voicing and re-rendering, so keep each one self-contained.
- **The first 10 seconds decide the video** (the channel's own data, Oct 2026: *Good Luck* lost half its viewers by 0:08,
  mostly promoted traffic; *King Andrew*, mostly organic, kept 66% at 0:30 and about half to 13:00, with no cliff at the
  channel intro). So for every cold open: **the first sentence is the hook itself, never a date or a place** ("The most
  fashionable color of the 1860s was made with arsenic. And people knew it." not "November 1874. Lansing, Michigan.");
  **what the thumbnail shows is on screen at frame 0** (no fade from black, camera already moving); the question lands by
  about 0:40; and the cold open ends on the question plus **one or two promises** that pay off later ("We'll come back to
  that girl."). Chapter 2 starts in the action, not with backstory: background comes in as a short flashback. Write two or
  three versions of the first 30 seconds and pick the strongest before voicing. Judge retention by organic traffic (Studio
  → Advanced mode → Traffic source), not the blended graph.
- **Chapter 1 is a cold open, under a minute.** Open on a concrete artifact: a cartoon, a pamphlet, a portrait,
  a document. Describe it in short sentences. Then pose the **driving question** the whole video answers:
  "So here's the question I want to answer today: How did the People's President end up being called a king?"
  End with a hook into chapter 2 ("And it starts with a teenager, a British officer, and a pair of dirty boots.").
  Also plant the video's long loop here (see "Story loops" below).
  **After the cold open's last line, hold a one-second beat** (the user's standing direction, Oct 2026): about 30
  frames of near-silence, the last image held, only the music's tail, no graphic, before the next chapter starts.
  It lets the hook land. Build it as the next chapter's lead, not as script text.
- **The last chapter answers the question out loud** ("So let's go back to the question."), pulls the threads
  together through callbacks, and ends on a short line for a general audience that lands the title. *King
  Andrew* ended on one word: "King." *Fix Everything* ended on a one-line tease of what comes next ("It split
  the country in two. But that's a story for next time."). Either works.
- **Two subscribe reminders, every video** (the user's standing request). (1) **Mid-video**, about a third of the way
  in, at a chapter's end: two or three sentences, tied to the topic, with a wry turn. "Quick thing before we go on. If
  you're enjoying this, hit subscribe. It's free, it really helps the channel, and unlike William Wansley, nobody's
  paying me to say it." (Black Sox) / "the board would like you to subscribe... That's my hand on the pointer" (Ouija).
  (2) **At the end**, its own last paragraph *after* the closing line, voiced over the end screen: "Thanks for
  watching. If you want more stories like this one, subscribe, and I'll see you next time." Never let the plug step
  on the closing line, and never put it in a heavy chapter. On screen: a small teal Note ("subscribe ↓"), nothing
  louder.
- **Early life gets color.** One or two vivid, human details (the boots, the duel, the bullet he carried) make the
  rest of the story land.
- When the user asks for something to be cut "to get to 15 minutes", give a cut list with word counts per cut and
  the new total, then apply it.

- **Pace since the West series (eleven_v4, one take):** about **165 words a minute including pauses**, slower than the old locked
  pace. *Not in the Picture* (American West Ep 1) had 2,450 words: 14:56 of narration and **15:59 finished** (the Chunk intro, the
  title card, eight 2-second mark-snap breaks, short chapter tails and a 20-second end screen add about a minute). **For 15:00
  finished, write about 2,300 words.**

### The story arc (the channel's repeatable shape, from the user's notes, Oct 2026)

A repeatable structure saves a blank page on script day, and viewers learn the rhythm. For a 10–15 minute story video:

| Part | Time (15 min / 10 min) | Job | The YouTube reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | 0:00–0:45 | The most dramatic, bizarre or high-stakes moment, taken from the middle or end of the story. Drop the viewer into the action, name the core mystery, then pull back. Never "In the year 1872…" | Viewers decide in the first 15–30 seconds |
| **Act I: the setup** | 1–3 / 1–2 | Who, where, when, fast. Introduce the people (heroes, scoundrels, ordinary people caught up in it). Tie the context to their motives | Retention dips if background drags |
| **Act II: the escalation** | 3–7 / 2–5 | Stakes rise, the plan moves, the mystery deepens. **A midpoint twist or complication around the 4–5 minute mark** (about 25–35% in): just when the viewer thinks they know where it's going, throw a wrench in it | A fresh complication instead of a summary flattens the drop-off |
| **Act III: the climax** | 7–11 / 5–8 | The con is exposed, the battle turns, the truth comes out. The highest-energy stretch: quicker cutting, the music shifts, the payoff lands | Peak attention |
| **Act IV: the fallout** | 11–13 / 8–10 | What happened to everyone. End on a takeaway (why this odd story matters, what it says about people), never a fade to black or "and that's the story" | Session time; the ending decides the next click |

It sits on top of the rules above: the cold open is the hook, chapter 2 starts Act I in the action, the long loop pays off in Act III,
the mid-video plug goes after the escalation's big build, and the last chapter answers the driving question out loud. Mark the four acts
and the midpoint twist in SCRIPT.md's beat table.

## 2. The voice

Plain, quick and a little wry: a smart friend explaining history, not a textbook and not a comedian.

- **Short sentences.** Fragments are fine. "A man in royal robes. A crown on his head."
- **Plain words.** Define a hard word the moment it appears, in an appositive:
  "That's called expanding suffrage, the right to vote." / "a tariff, a tax on imported goods like British cloth"
  / "nullify, or cancel, a federal law".
- **Dry one-line asides**, one every minute or so, never two in a row:
  - "Okay. Dramatic." (after Jackson's "Judas of the West" letter)
  - "Which is not normally what cabinet meetings are for."
  - "Which is basically your vice president running a burner account against you."
  - "Subtle." (the Whigs naming themselves after the anti-king party)
  - "Translation: I'm the people. You're just Congress."
  - "Not a great start." (his father died weeks before he was born)
  - "So, basically, the skirt scandal."
  Modern comparisons are welcome when they are instantly clear and true (see "Write for someone who knows
  nothing" below); the rule is one clear connection per chapter, not a stream of them.
- **Humanize the people.** Loyal? Absolutely. Stubborn? Also absolutely. Grief, temper and friendship explain
  choices; use them.
- **Heavy material gets no jokes.** Slavery, removal, deaths: plain statements, numbers, a primary source, and
  space. The heavy chapter is voiced slower (see voice-and-audio.md).
- **Numbers as people say them**: "about 23 million acres", "about one in ten", "perhaps as many as one in four".
  The voice tool spells out years and numbers itself, so write digits.

### Write for someone who knows nothing (the user's standing direction, Oct 2026)

The viewer may have arrived from a Short, half-watching on a phone. Every line has to work for someone with no
background, no memory of earlier chapters and no reason to stay. Three habits, checked on every script:

**1. No context burden.** Nothing the viewer has to already know.
- **Names only when they matter.** If the story works with "one English painter", use that and put the name in the
  source tag. Name someone when the viewer will meet them again, or when the name *is* the point. Never introduce
  two new names in one sentence.
- **One date per idea, at most.** Keep the date that makes the point (often the one closest to today: "into the
  1960s"); drop the rest. Prefer "eighty years ago" or "your great-grandparents' war" when the exact year isn't the point.
- **No jargon without the plain word first.** "Paint", not "pigment". "A unit whose weapons were inflatable tanks",
  then the official name if needed. The appositive rule above still applies.
- **No "as we saw" and no callbacks that require memory** in Shorts. In long videos a callback must re-state what it
  calls back to in a few words ("that fake tank from earlier").
- **Every chapter opens like a cold open**: one concrete image or fact that makes sense on its own.

**2. Connect to today.** Each chapter gets one bridge from then to the viewer's life now.
- **Put it near them**: "a museum near you", "the sky over your town", "the same air your flight rides home on".
- **Make the time short**: "your grandparents could have bought it", "a few of them were still alive when you were born".
- **Give them something to do or notice**: "next time you're in a museum, look at the shadows."
- **Modern echoes must be real and sourced** (an inflatable decoy in a current war, a balloon found in 2014). A
  present-day event involving governments is described in neutral, factual terms, with no politicians named
  (objectivity rules below).

**3. Sound like a person talking.**
- **Write it the way you'd say it out loud to one person.** Contractions ("it's", "they'd"), "you", plain verbs.
- **Read every line aloud before voicing.** If you'd never say it at a dinner table, rewrite it. Cut "notably",
  "indeed", "it is worth noting", "in fact", "furthermore", "a testament to".
- **Vary rhythm**: a long sentence, then a short one. Then a fragment.
- **Let the punchline be the last word** of its sentence ("Then they ran out of mummies.").
- **Questions the viewer is already asking** are good transitions ("So why didn't anyone warn them?").

**Before → after** (Short 6, *When Europe Ate Mummies*):
- Before: "In 1881, the British painter Edward Burne-Jones found out what was in the tube." (a name, a date, an assumed
  "the tube") → After: "One English painter found out over Sunday lunch."
- Before: "In 1888, a Philadelphia art supply catalog still listed it, right there with the other browns."
  → After: "One London shop kept making it into the 1960s. Then they ran out of mummies."
- Before: "…somewhere, on a gallery wall, in a gilded frame, there may be a person." → After: "There may be a painting
  in a museum near you with a person in it. Not painted on it. In it." … "So next time you're in a museum, look
  closely at the shadows. And maybe say hi."

**Checklist pass** (do it on every chapter before the user sees it): circle every name, date and term. For each
one ask: does the viewer need it to follow the story? If not, cut it or move it to a source tag. Then mark the one
line in the chapter that connects to today. If there isn't one, write it.

### Story loops (the user's standing direction, Oct 2026)

Keep the viewer holding at least one unanswered question at every moment. A loop is **open** (a question the viewer
now needs answered), **build** (context and rising stakes while they wait), **close** (the payoff, ideally with a turn).
Open the next loop *before or as* the current one closes, so there is never a natural place to leave.

- **The driving question is the outer loop**: opened in the cold open, closed in the last chapter.
- **Plant one long loop in the cold open** that pays off two-thirds of the way through (the strongest mystery in the
  video, teased honestly). It carries the middle, where viewers usually drop.
- **Every chapter ends by opening the next loop, never by summarizing.** "And while those balloons protected an army,
  another unit was using balloons to invent one." Not: "So that's how barrage balloons worked."
- **Mid-chapter mini-loops**: a question the viewer is already asking, asked out loud and answered a beat later
  ("But could a rubber tank actually fool anyone?").
- **But / therefore, not and then.** Beats connect by cause or contradiction. If two beats join with "and then", cut one
  or find the "but".
- **Honest teases only.** The payoff must be at least as big as the promise. "Almost nobody was told" is fine if true;
  "a cover-up" is not unless the evidence says so. Bait the payoff can't support costs more viewers than it gains.
- **Map the loops in SCRIPT.md** (a table: loop, where it opens, where it closes) and check that no stretch longer than
  about 45 seconds has every loop closed.
- **Shorts**: one loop. Open on the question in the first second, close it on the payoff line, and loop the last frame
  to the first.

## 3. Objectivity rules

These came from the user directly; follow them in every video.

- **Let's look at the evidence.** Present what supporters and critics each saw ("To Jackson's supporters, he had
  saved the Union. To South Carolina, he was a tyrant..."). The ending explains why both readings existed; it
  doesn't hand down a verdict.
- **No historian names** in the narration or on screen. "Historians still argue about this one" is fine.
- **Don't imply false continuity with today.** If a party, institution or word shares a modern name, say plainly
  it was different: "Same name as today's Democrats, but a very different party, with very different ideas."
- **No modern politicians**, even by implication. The closer can gesture at "ever since" without naming anyone.
- **Modern companies and living people** (*Say It Ain't So*: Polymarket, two indicted pitchers): describe a company in its
  own and the official terms ("names an official partner", "where people trade on what's going to happen"), don't name
  people who haven't been convicted ("charged", "pleaded not guilty", "when this video was made"), keep a scandal and a
  company in separate sentences unless they're connected, and attribute criticism to critics. Re-read every present-day
  line with this in mind before voicing.
- **Hedge what's uncertain, in plain words**: "reportedly", "probably never said that", "There's no proof of an
  actual deal." Myths get named and corrected ("You may have heard that he said...").
- **Say who was left out** when "the people" or "democracy" is the theme: women, enslaved people, Native nations,
  and in most states free Black men.

## 4. Teaching moves

- **Vocab** (a highlighter title plus a definition bar on screen): list 10–20 per video in SCRIPT.md. Mark the
  term in the script with `{braces}` where it is defined.
- **Key concepts** the user names (e.g. "spoils system and Kitchen Cabinet need to be key concepts") get their own
  beat: the term, a definition in one sentence, an example, and why it mattered.
- **Primary-source quotes**: short, exact, dated, attributed on screen (not in the narration unless natural).
  Shorten as spoken and note the full wording in the fact-check flags.
- **Callbacks** make 15 minutes feel like one story: "Hold that thought." / "Remember that kid." / "Hold onto
  that. It's about to cause a scandal." List them in SCRIPT.md so the scenes can reuse the same image.
- **Emphasis graphics**: when the user wants a point hit hard ("expanded suffrage is why he won"), plan a
  multi-beat graphic in SCRIPT.md's production notes and make the narration give it room (three short
  paragraphs, each with one number).

## 5. File format and markup

One file per chapter: `script/chNN_slug.txt` (`ch01_cold_open.txt`, `ch05_the_people.txt`). The audio and the
scene import use the same stem.

- Paragraphs are separated by a blank line. Each paragraph is voiced as one take, with the paragraphs either side
  as context, so a paragraph is a breath group: 1–4 sentences.
- `*key idea*` marks emphasis; `{vocab term}` marks a defined term. Both may span words; the voice tool strips
  them and records them per word (`k: 'key' | 'vocab'` in words.json).
- Write digits for numbers and years; write names normally (the pronunciation table handles respellings).
- **v4 delivery cues** (from the West series; the user asked for them in every script): `[tag]` in square brackets before the line
  it colours. `tools/voice.py` sends tags to ElevenLabs (`eleven_v4` follows them and does not speak them; checked with
  speech-to-text) and strips them from the word timings and the screen. Keep them sparing, one every few paragraphs, from a small
  consistent set: `[curious]` (the driving question), `[wry]` / `[dry]` / `[deadpan]` (asides), `[thoughtful]` (the bigger promise,
  "So let's go back to the question"), `[serious]` / `[somber]` / `[grim]` (heavy material), `[warmly]` (the plug, the
  thank-you), `[light]` (hand-offs), `[pause]` (a beat before a turn). A tag never carries a joke the words don't already make. **Avoid `[quietly]`**: v4 drops to a near-whisper that the user
  found creepy (Ep 1's "You can't find them"); `[firmly]` or `[plainly]` keeps a quiet line at speaking level.
- Quotes in straight or curly double quotes are fine.

## 6. SCRIPT.md: the master document

`script/SCRIPT.md` is what the user reads and approves. Rebuild it from the chapter files whenever they change.
Layout used for *King Andrew*:

```
# <Title>: <Subtitle>
**15 Minute History** · narration script · <N> words · about <mm:ss>
**Driving question:** ...   **Answer:** ...
## 0:00 | <Chapter 1 name>
<narration>
---
## 0:58 | <Chapter 2 name>
...
## Production notes      (heavy chapter, callbacks, loop map, vocab cards, quote cards, key concepts, emphasis graphics, ending)
## Pronunciation (for ElevenLabs)   (table: Word | Say it)
## Fact-check flags      (each claim that could be challenged, with the evidence and how the script hedges)
## Sources
```

Re-time the headings from the real chapter lengths after voicing (tools/render.sh prints chapter start times).

## 7. Fact-check flags

For every date, number, quote and contested claim, write a flag: what the script says, what the record says,
and why the wording is safe. Also flag places where class readings or popular memory differ from the script
(wrong year in a textbook, a famous misquote). Teachers use this, and it is what lets the user trust the script.
Re-check anything time-sensitive before publishing (e.g. "Jackson is still on the $20").

## 8. Pronunciation check (before voicing)

Always do this before voicing a whole video; the user asked for it explicitly.

1. List every name and term the voice could misread: Native nations and leaders, French and Spanish names, place
   names (Worcester, Waxhaws), abbreviations (IOUs), anything the user flags.
2. Put the risky ones in `PRONOUNCE` in `tools/voice.py` as respellings ("Worcester": "Wooster",
   "Sequoyah": "Sih-kwoy-uh", "Tocqueville": "Toke-vill", "Floride": "Flor-id", "IOUs": "I-O-U's").
   A respelling must keep the same number of words, or the timing alignment breaks.
3. Voice a short test file (`script/tests/pronunciation.txt`, one sentence per term, in context) and send the
   user the MP3 to listen to. Fix and repeat before voicing chapters.

## 9. The review loop with the user

Run the checklist in `explainer-craft.md` §1 on the outline, on the full draft, and before voicing.

The user steers content in short messages. Expect, and invite, these passes:

1. **Beat outline** (chapter list with one line each, the driving question and the answer). Get approval.
2. **Full draft** in SCRIPT.md. Ask: "Are we missing anything important, content-wise?" Suggest additions with
   their cost in seconds.
3. **Coverage checks** the user names (a required concept, a topic to touch lightly and save for another video,
   a favorite moment from an earlier draft to keep). Answer each directly, then edit.
4. **Trim to time** with a cut list.
5. **Confusion pass** (`explainer-craft.md` rule 14): read each chapter as someone new to the story; fix every
   "wait, what?", every quote left untranslated, every callback that isn't said out loud. List the fixes in SCRIPT.md.
6. **Fact-check flags and pronunciation check**, then voice.

## 10. Writing a series (one arc every episode follows)

From the American West series (`projects/American_West/SERIES.md`, four episodes); the Reconstruction series (`projects/Reconstruction/`)
works the same way with its collage board. Plan the whole series first (one `SERIES.md` + one outline per episode), then write
each episode on the same skeleton, so the parts feel like one thing.

**Frame the series with two questions.** A **unit question** every episode answers one piece of out loud (West: *"To what extent
did the United States fulfill its promises of freedom and opportunity after the Civil War?"*) and a **series closer** the last
episode asks and stops on. Give each episode **one promise being tested** (Ep 1: progress ties the nation together; Ep 2: anyone
with nerve can get rich; Ep 3: free land; Ep 4: the promises made *to* Native nations). Order the episodes by cause.

**The arc every episode follows** (the channel's methods, applied identically):

| Beat | What it does |
|---|---|
| 1. Look again (0:00–0:20) | Open on a real artifact already on screen at frame 0 (and in the thumbnail); describe what anyone sees, then turn on one detail. The first sentence is the hook, never a date. |
| 2. Question + bigger promise (by ~0:40) | A one-breath driving question, then a promise about what it reveals; one or two "we'll come back to…" promises. |
| 3. Start in the action (ch 2) | No backstory before the question; background enters just in time as a short flashback. |
| 4. One example carries each chapter | Person or object → the general point → why → result → back to the person. |
| 5. Stack, then "That means" | A one-sentence recap before each payoff. |
| 6. Myth beat | One myth per episode, named and corrected kindly (West Ep 1: the golden spike). |
| 7. Both readings | Supporters and critics each get their evidence; no verdict (a two-views card that fills across chapters). |
| 8. Plug at about two-thirds | After the big build, never in a heavy chapter. |
| 9. The quiet chapter | One heavy chapter per episode: slower voice, no jokes, teal-only quiet mode, a primary source and space. |
| 10. "So let's go back to the question." | Answer the driving question through callbacks, fill this episode's row of the series' recurring card, end on a short reversal over the opening image. |
| 11. The handoff | One line that tees up the next episode, then the end-screen thank-you. |

**Recurring devices that hold a series together:**
- **One recurring card** that each episode adds to and the last one completes: the West's **Promise Ledger** (*The promise · Who
  got it · Who didn't*, one row per episode, stamped in Ep 4; it doubles as the students' graphic organizer), Reconstruction's
  collage **board** (`techniques/collage_board.md`).
- **One drawn map that fills in** across episodes (same base map and camera clamp in all of them).
- **Hinge years**, each a dated stamp the first time and a callback after (West: 1862, 1868, 1874, 1890), and numbers that
  return (160 acres → the Dawes allotment).
- **A cross-episode callback table** in `SERIES.md` (planted in · paid off in · what), checked when each episode is written.
- **Required test content**: if the series serves a unit, list where every tested item lives so none is missed.
