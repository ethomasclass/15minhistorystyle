# Gilded Age Episode 4: research

**Working title:** *The Machine* (or *Helper or Thief?*, or *Who Fixes the City?*)
**Status:** research pass 1 and a proposed outline, Nov 15, 2026. Not a script yet.
**Length:** about 1,650 words of narration (10 to 11 minutes).
**Covers:** urban growth 1870 to 1900 and why; how cities grew up and out (elevators, iron and steel frames, the Home Insurance
Building, the els, Sprague's trolley, the Brooklyn Bridge, streetcar suburbs); what city governments could not handle (tenements,
water, sewage, horses, disease, fire, crime, garbage) and Jacob Riis; how a machine worked (captain, district leader, boss; the
clubhouse and saloon; favors for votes; naturalization; repeaters); Tammany Hall and the Tweed Ring (the courthouse, the July 1871
*Times* exposé, Nast, the escape and the capture in Vigo, Spain); Plunkitt and "honest graft"; Brooklyn's own machine (Hugh
McLaughlin); what machines built; the 1880s reform response (Pendleton Act, Seth Low).
**Family thread:** the Kellys of Williamsburg, in the City of Brooklyn, mid-1880s. The younger Kelly brother is offered a job through
the machine and becomes a ward heeler: neither villain nor saint. The Kellys experience history, they do not cause it. Composite
disclaimer on screen.
**Unit rule:** the script never names the unit's compelling question. This episode quietly fills the "government" column with the
machine version of it: an unofficial government that did help people the official one ignored, and charged for it.

Confidence key used below: **[primary]** = checked against an 1870s to 1900s document in this pass; **[official]** = a government
statistical table; **[museum]** = a museum, park, library or archive page; **[secondary]** = reputable secondary source;
**[unverified]** = from the brief, a weak source or memory, not yet checked; do not narrate until checked.

## 1. What a student should be able to explain after watching

1. Between 1870 and 1900 American cities grew far faster than the country. The urban share rose from about a quarter to about two
   fifths, and the number of cities over 100,000 went from 14 to 38. People came from farms (machines needed fewer hands), from
   abroad (immigration), and, in smaller numbers before 1910, from the South.
2. New technology let cities grow up (elevators, iron and steel frames) and out (elevated trains, electric trolleys, the Brooklyn
   Bridge). Out-of-town "streetcar suburbs" sorted people by money: those who could pay the fare moved out; the poor stayed near the
   factories and docks.
3. City governments could not keep up. Crowded tenements, dirty water, sewage, horse manure, disease, fire and crime were everyday
   dangers, and there was almost no public safety net.
4. A **political machine** was an organization that ran a city through a chain of loyal workers (precinct captain, district or ward
   leader, boss). It gave jobs, coal, bail, rent money and help with citizenship papers in exchange for votes, and paid for itself
   with public jobs, contracts, kickbacks and fraud.
5. The Tweed Ring stole tens of millions of dollars from New York, most famously through a courthouse that cost many times its
   budget. Newspapers (*The New York Times*) and cartoons (Thomas Nast in *Harper's Weekly*) helped bring it down in 1871.
6. Historians still argue whether machines were helpers or thieves. They were both: they gave immigrants a voice and a ladder into
   politics, and they robbed the same taxpayers and tenants they claimed to serve. Reformers answered with civil service rules
   (the Pendleton Act, 1883) and "good government" mayors.

## 4. Verified facts, by section

### 4.1 The city explodes: urban growth 1870 to 1900

City populations [official: U.S. Census Bureau, Campbell Gibson, "Population of the 100 Largest Cities and Other Urban Places in
the United States: 1790 to 1990," Working Paper 27, 1998, Tables 10 to 13]:

| City | 1870 | 1880 | 1890 | 1900 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New York (Manhattan; consolidated "Greater New York" in 1900) | 942,292 | 1,206,299 | 1,515,301 | 3,437,202 |
| Brooklyn (separate city until Jan 1, 1898) | 396,099 | 566,663 | 806,343 | (in New York) |
| Chicago | 298,977 | 503,185 | 1,099,850 | 1,698,575 |
| Philadelphia | 674,022 | 847,170 | 1,046,964 | 1,293,697 |

- **Brooklyn's rank:** third-largest U.S. city in 1870 and 1880, fourth in 1890 (behind New York, Chicago, Philadelphia). [official:
  Gibson Tables 10 to 12] Narration: "Brooklyn, on its own, was one of the biggest cities in America."
- **Cities of 100,000 or more:** **14** (1870), **20** (1880), **28** (1890), **38** (1900). [official: counted in this pass from
  Gibson Tables 10 to 13; Orth 1919 independently gives 28 for 1890] So "the number of big cities nearly tripled."
- **Urban share of all Americans** (places of 2,500 or more, the Census's later consistent series): **25.7%** (1870), 28.2% (1880),
  35.1% (1890), **39.6%** (1900). [secondary: Wikipedia "Urbanization in the United States," citing Census CPH-2-5, 2012; the
  Census table itself was not reachable this pass] Urban headcount: about 9.9 million (1870) to about 30.2 million (1900).
  [unverified as counts; consistent with the percentages times the census totals of 38.6 million and 76.0 million] Safe: "the number
  of Americans living in cities roughly tripled, from about ten million to about thirty million."
- **Chicago caveat:** part of the 1880 to 1890 jump came from **annexing** neighboring towns in 1889 (Lake View, Hyde Park, Jefferson,
  Lake and others). [unverified this pass; standard; check Encyclopedia of Chicago "Annexation" before narrating] The Great Fire of
  October 1871 makes the growth more striking: the city burned and still grew almost sixfold in thirty years. [Fire: standard;
  Chicago History Museum; numbers not checked this pass] Safe: "Chicago grew from about 300,000 to about 1.7 million."
- **New York caveat:** the 1900 figure is the new consolidated city (five boroughs). Compare like with like, or say "by 1900 Greater
  New York held 3.4 million people." Manhattan alone was about 1.85 million in 1900 [unverified this pass; standard borough figure].
- **Why people came:**
  - *Immigration:* 2.8 million (1870s), 5.2 million (1880s), 3.7 million (1890s). [official: DHS Yearbook Table 1, as summed in
    ep3/RESEARCH.md 4.4]
  - *Farm machines:* reapers, binders and threshers meant fewer hands per acre; farm work fell from about half of all American
    workers in 1870 to under two fifths by 1900. [unverified: standard figure (about 53% to about 38 to 40%, Lebergott in *Historical
    Statistics of the United States*), not checked this pass] Safe: "farms needed fewer hands; factories needed more."
  - *Factories:* in ten major cities the number of teamsters (wagon drivers) rose 328% from 1870 to 1900 while population rose 105%:
    the city ran on hauling. [secondary: Eric Morris, "From Horse Power to Horsepower," *ACCESS* 30, Spring 2007]
  - *Black migration from the South:* real but small before the Great Migration (from about 1910). [unverified this pass; standard;
    check Schomburg Center "In Motion" before narrating] Brooklyn had an old free Black community (Weeksville). Recommendation: one
    clause at most, or leave out; the Kelly story does not need it.

### 4.2 Up and out: how cities grew

- **Elevators.** Elisha Otis's safety elevator (brake that holds the car if the rope breaks) made tall buildings practical; the first
  commercial passenger elevator went into the E. V. Haughwout Building, Broadway, New York, in 1857. [unverified this pass; standard;
  check NYC LPC or Otis before narrating the year]
- **Home Insurance Building, Chicago.** Designed 1884 by **William Le Baron Jenney**; completed **1885**; **10 stories, 138 feet**;
  two floors added later (1890 or 1891; sources differ); demolished **1931** for the Field Building. Its load-bearing metal frame
  ("skeleton") is why it is usually called the first skyscraper. The frame was mostly cast and wrought iron, with some steel in the
  upper floors. [museum: Chicago Architecture Center; secondary: History.com, Wikipedia] Safe: "often called the first skyscraper:
  ten stories held up by a metal skeleton instead of thick walls." Avoid "the first steel skyscraper."
- **Elevated railways, New York.** Charles T. Harvey's cable-pulled line on Greenwich Street, the first elevated railway in New York,
  ran a trial on July 1 to 3, 1868; passenger service from 1870; it switched to steam locomotives in 1871. By 1878 the Ninth Avenue
  line reached from South Ferry to 61st Street; by about 1880 Manhattan had four el lines (Second, Third, Sixth, Ninth Avenues).
  [museum: Museum of the City of New York, "Charles T. Harvey: Elevating Transit"; secondary: Wikipedia "IRT Ninth Avenue Line";
  four-line count unverified] Brooklyn's first el opened in **1885** [unverified this pass]. The els were steam-powered, noisy,
  dripping oil and cinders on the street below until electrified about 1902.
- **Electric trolley.** Frank J. Sprague's system for the **Richmond Union Passenger Railway**, Richmond, Virginia, began regular
  service in **February 1888** (February 2 is the usual date; about ten cars at first [unverified]) and became the model for most
  electric street railways after it; Boston copied it after an inspection on September 7, 1888. IEEE Milestone, dedicated 1992.
  [museum/official: IEEE Engineering and Technology History Wiki, "Milestones: Richmond Union Passenger Railway, 1888"] Electric cars ran about twice as fast as horsecars [unverified; standard]. Safe: "the first electric trolley system
  that really worked."
- **The Brooklyn Bridge** opened **May 24, 1883** (Ep 1 covers it). Machine fingerprints: Tweed reportedly demanded $60,000 for the
  aldermen's approval and took a share of the bridge company's stock; Hugh McLaughlin's Brooklyn ring also had a hand in it.
  [secondary: Burrows and Wallace, *Gotham* (1999), pp. 934 to 935, via Wikipedia "William M. Tweed"; Wikipedia "Hugh McLaughlin"]
  Narrate only "even the bridge had the machine's fingerprints on it," if at all.
- **Streetcar suburbs and sorting by class.** The term comes from Sam Bass Warner Jr., *Streetcar Suburbs: The Process of Growth in
  Boston, 1870 to 1900* (Harvard, 1962): cheap transit let the middle class live miles from work, so neighborhoods sorted by income;
  the poor, who could not afford a daily fare or needed to be near docks and factories, stayed packed downtown. Riis (1890) on
  New York: the reformers' "one way out he saw--rapid transit to the suburbs--has brought no relief." [primary: Riis, Introduction;
  secondary: Warner] Riis's quote is a nice irony line.

### 4.3 Too many, too fast: urban problems

All [primary] from Jacob A. Riis, *How the Other Half Lives* (Scribner's, 1890), Project Gutenberg #45502, unless marked. Riis's
statistics come from the city Registrar of Vital Statistics, Dr. Roger S. Tracy, whom he thanks in the Preface.

- **Tenements.** "To-day three-fourths of its people live in the tenements." In 1869, 14,872 tenements held 468,492 people; at the end of
  1888, 32,390 held 1,093,701; in 1890, "37,316 tenements, including 2,630 rear houses, and their population is over 1,250,000."
  (Riis, Introduction; ch. XXIV, "What Has Been Done"; appendix "Tenements.") Note: a "tenement" was legally a house with four or
  more families in 1869 and three or more in 1888 (Riis's footnotes 25 and 26).
- **Crowding.** Persons per dwelling, 1880 census: New York 16.37, Brooklyn 9.11, London 7.9, Philadelphia 5.79. Density in 1890:
  Eleventh Ward 246,040 and Thirteenth Ward 274,432 people per square mile. (Riis appendix) So Brooklyn was crowded but much less so
  than Manhattan's Lower East Side: useful for the Kellys.
- **Death rates.** City of New York 26.47 per 1,000 (1880), 25.19 (1889); Brooklyn 23.33 (1880), 22.5 (1889). (Riis appendix) On
  the Mulberry and Baxter Street block (the Bend) in 1888, children under five died at a rate of **139.83 per 1,000**, against a city
  general rate of 26.27; Riis: "There were five baby funerals in that house the same year." (table from Dr. Tracy; ch. VI, "The Bend") Safe: "on one Manhattan block, about one small child in seven died in a single year."
- **Water and sewage.** New York's Croton Aqueduct (1842) and Brooklyn's Ridgewood system (1850s) brought water to hydrants and
  buildings, but in tenements it often stopped at a sink in the hall or a yard pump; privies ("school sinks") in the yards drained into
  sewers that emptied raw into the rivers. [unverified this pass; standard; check Tenement Museum before narrating specifics] Safe:
  "one sink for a whole floor, a privy in the yard, sewage straight into the river."
- **Horses.** "For New York and Brooklyn, which had a combined horse population of between 150,000 and 175,000 in 1880 ... between
  three and four million pounds of manure were deposited on city streets and in city stables every day," at 15 to 30 pounds per horse,
  plus about 40,000 gallons of urine; "In 1880, New York carted away nearly 15,000 dead equines from its streets"; the average
  streetcar horse lived "barely two years"; in 1900, 200 people were killed by horses and horse-drawn vehicles in New York.
  [secondary: Morris, *ACCESS* 2007, citing McShane and Tarr] Caution: the "1894 Great Horse Manure Crisis" and the claim that the
  1898 planning conference was "mostly about manure" are popular retellings with weak sourcing [unverified]; do not use them.
- **Disease.** Cholera (1832, 1849, 1866), typhoid, tuberculosis, diphtheria and summer diarrhea killed most in the crowded wards; flies
  breeding in manure spread infant diarrhea. [secondary: Morris 2007 on flies; cholera years standard, unverified this pass] Safe:
  "a cut, a cough, or a bad summer could kill a baby."
- **Fire.** The **Brooklyn Theatre fire**, December 5, 1876, killed about 278 (official count) to 300 or more people; the official 278
  is used by Green-Wood Cemetery, where many were buried in a common grave. [secondary: Brownstoner; Green-Wood; History.com says
  "nearly 300"] It is a Brooklyn event eight years before the episode: good for Ma Kelly's memory. Tenement fires were common because
  of wooden stairs, kerosene lamps and coal stoves [standard].
- **Crime.** New York police made 62,274 arrests of men and 19,926 of women in 1889; the city had 7,884 saloons and found 2,968 lost
  children in the streets that year. (Riis appendix, "Police Statistics")
- **Garbage.** Street cleaning was a patronage job and badly done until Col. George E. Waring Jr. reorganized it (1895 to 1898, the
  "White Wings"). [secondary: Morris 2007 (1890s); Waring dates standard, unverified] It is after our setting: use only as a look ahead.
- **No safety net.** Brooklyn **abolished public "outdoor relief"** (coal, groceries or cash to poor people living at home) in 1878.
  "In Brooklyn, during 1877, 46,350 people were relieved at a cost of $141,207. In 1878 no money was given." Seth Low, quoted
  approvingly, said the almshouse did not fill up as feared. The same 1888 article advised visiting poor families in summer so they
  would save for winter coal, and if "they are less sensible than the squirrel, they must in all fairness to themselves be allowed
  to suffer discomfiture in winter and be taught by bitter experience." [primary: "Altruism Economically Considered," *Popular
  Science Monthly* vol. 34 (November 1888), p. 64, Wikisource transcription; author not checked] Seth Low founded the Brooklyn Bureau
  of Charities in 1878 [secondary, via search; unverified]. New York City's charity reformers also fought outdoor relief from 1874
  [secondary: *Journal of Policy History*, Cambridge]. **This is the gap the machine filled: the official answer to a cold family in
  1880s Brooklyn was "no coal," and the machine's answer was "here's coal."**
- **Jacob Riis (1849 to 1914).** Danish immigrant (arrived 1870), police reporter, who photographed tenements at night with magnesium
  flash powder; the book grew from his December 1889 *Scribner's Magazine* article. [secondary/standard; dates unverified this pass;
  his "flash-light" photographs are mentioned in the book itself] **Framing and prejudice:** Riis sorts people by nationality and
  ranks them; e.g., "The Italian and the poor Jew rise only by compulsion. The Chinaman does not rise at all," while praising "the
  Teuton." On the Irish: "The Irishman's genius runs to public affairs rather than domestic life; wherever he is mustered in force
  the saloon is the gorgeous centre of political activity." He blames "the rumshop," which "turns the political crank in New York."
  Chapter titles include "Jewtown." (ch. III, "The Mixed Crowd"; ch. XVIII, "The Reign of Rum") Use his numbers and photos; name him as a reformer with
  blind spots; never quote his ethnic rankings without framing.

### 4.4 How a machine worked

- **The ladder** (Tammany, as described by Orth, 1919, and Riordon, 1905):
  - **Election district (precinct) captain:** each assembly district "is in turn divided into election districts of about 400 voters,
    each with a precinct captain who is acquainted with every voter in his precinct and keeps track, as far as possible, of his
    affairs." [primary/period: Orth, *The Boss and the Machine*, 1919, ch. on Tammany; note this describes the 1910s structure]
  - **District leader** (older term: **ward boss**; the city was divided into wards, later assembly districts): "the district leader is
    a very important wheel in the machine"; the leaders together form "the potent oligarchy of Tammany." Every assembly district has
    "headquarters and a club house, where the voters can go in the evening and enjoy a smoke, a bottle, and a more or less quiet game."
    [primary/period: Orth 1919] In 1905 Tammany had "thirty-six district leaders." [primary: Plunkitt, ch. 2]
  - **Boss:** the leader of the whole organization (Tweed to 1871; "Honest John" Kelly, a stonemason, from 1874 to his death in
    1886; then Richard Croker). [primary/period: Orth 1919 on Kelly; Kelly's dates unverified this pass] Note the coincidence: the
    1880s Tammany boss was named **Kelly**. Either avoid confusion or make a light joke of it (see 8).
  - **Ward heeler:** a low-level worker who did the leader's errands and brought out the vote. The term appears in Orth ("naturalized
    under the guidance of wardheelers") and Riordon ("daily foot races and fights between the two heelers"). [primary]
- **The daily favors (Riordon's record of a district leader's day, 1905):** 2 a.m., bailing out a saloonkeeper; 6 a.m., a fire (see
  4.7); 8:30 a.m., police court, "Found six 'drunks.' Secured the discharge of four by a timely word with the judge, and paid the
  fines of two"; 9 a.m., a district captain acts "as counsel for a widow" facing eviction, and the leader "Paid the rent of a poor
  family about to be dispossessed and gave them a dollar for food"; 11 a.m., four men looking for work, all placed; afternoon, an
  Italian funeral and a "Hebrew" one; evening, a meeting where "Each captain submitted a list of all the voters in his district,
  reported on their attitude toward Tammany, suggested who might be won over and how they could be won, told who were in need";
  then a church fair, the clubhouse, a wedding. [primary: Riordon, ch. 23, "Strenuous Life of the Tammany District Leader," which
  Riordon says is "based on extracts from Plunkitt's Diary and on my daily observation"]
- **Coal (the turkey's documented cousin):** in a rival leaders' contest, Tom Foley "impressed the Italian voters by paying the widow's
  rent for a month, and sending her half a ton of coal and a barrel of flour." [primary: Riordon, ch. 23] The **turkey** is famous
  from Tim Sullivan's Christmas dinners and later Tammany lore [unverified this pass]; the coal and flour line is the safer detail.
- **Jobs:** "I can always get a job for a deservin' man ... I know every big employer in the district and in the whole city, for that
  matter, and they ain't in the habit of sayin' no to me when I ask them for a job." [primary: Plunkitt, ch. 6] On immigrants:
  "his friends here often have a good place in one of the city departments picked out for him while he is still in the old country."
  [primary: Plunkitt, ch. 7]
- **How a young man joined:** Plunkitt's first "marketable commodity" was his cousin Tommy's vote plus his own; then the flat, the
  building, the block: "Before long I had sixty men back of me, and formed the George Washington Plunkitt Association." His advice:
  "Get a followin', if it's only one man." [primary: Plunkitt, ch. 2] **This is the younger Kelly brother's path.**
- **Naturalization help:** Orth (1919): immigrants "were herded into the courts just before election and admitted to citizenship. In
  New York they were naturalized under the guidance of wardheelers, not infrequently at the rate of one a minute!" Tammany's bargain:
  "Every poor immigrant is welcomed, introduced to the district headquarters, given work, or food, or shelter ... and in return he is
  merely to become naturalized as quickly as possible under the wardship of a Tammany captain and by the grace of a Tammany judge,
  and then to vote the Tammany ticket." "In 1868, from 25,000 to 30,000 foreigners were naturalized in New York in six weeks."
  [primary/period: Orth 1919; he is a reform-minded writer, so the tone is hostile] See 4.10 for the legal rules.
- **Repeaters and fraud:** in 1868 "Of 156,288 votes cast in the city, 25,000 were afterwards shown to be fraudulent." [primary/period:
  Orth 1919; source of the figure not given by Orth] Plunkitt's joke about a hired **repeater** (a man paid to vote more than once
  under other men's names) who claimed at the polls to be the local bishop: "'Come off. You ain't Bishop Doane,' said the poll clerk."
  [primary: Plunkitt, ch. 11; the full punchline contains a censored oath, so paraphrase] "Vote early and often" is a much older
  American joke with no reliable first speaker. [unverified; do not attribute it]
- **Where the money came from:** "three sources of the necessary lubricant: offices, jobs, the sale of favors." [primary/period: Orth
  1919] Plus kickbacks: in the Tweed years contractors were told to pad their bills "at first 10%, later 66%, and finally 85%." [Orth;
  compare the 1878 aldermen's report, 4.5]

### 4.5 Tammany Hall and the Tweed Ring

- **Tammany:** the Society of St. Tammany, founded 1789, named for the Lenape leader Tamanend; by the 1850s the Democratic Party's
  organization in Manhattan, run from its "wigwam," Tammany Hall (a new hall on 14th Street opened 1868). [unverified this pass;
  standard; check before narrating dates] Symbol: Nast made the **tiger** its mascot in cartoons. [standard]
- **William M. Tweed** (born April 3, 1823, Cherry Street, Lower East Side; died April 12, 1878), son of a chair maker; alderman 1851,
  congressman 1853 to 1855, on the county Board of Supervisors 1857 to 1870, chairman of the Democratic county committee 1861; by 1867
  at once state senator, school commissioner, deputy street commissioner and president of the Board of Supervisors. [museum: NYC
  Landmarks Preservation Commission, Tweed Courthouse designation report LP-1122, 1984, pp. 2 to 3; birth from Wikipedia] The Ring:
  Tweed; Comptroller Richard B. "Slippery Dick" **Connolly**; Mayor A. **Oakey Hall**; Peter B. "Brains" **Sweeny**. [primary/period:
  Paine 1904; Orth 1919]
- **How the Ring took its cut:** "Almost every person who did work or furnished supplies for the county at this time were informed by
  some member of the Ring that, in order to insure a continuance of the public patronage, increased orders and prompt payment, it
  would be necessary for them to add to their bills a certain percentage in excess of their true face." [primary: *Report of the
  Special Committee of the Board of Aldermen Appointed to Investigate the "Ring" Frauds* (1878), quoted in LPC LP-1122, p. 3]

**The Tweed Courthouse (52 Chambers Street, built 1861 to 1881)**

- **Budget:** the 1858 act let commissioners raise "a sum not exceeding $250,000"; the commissioners asked for $1,000,000 in 1859;
  laws of 1862 to 1871 authorized **$4,550,000** in all. [museum: LPC LP-1122, pp. 5 to 6] Callow: the 1858 law said the building,
  with furnishings, "should not cost more than $250,000." [secondary: Alexander B. Callow Jr., "The House That Tweed Built,"
  *American Heritage*, October 1965]

  dollars; by 1871 it had cost more than eleven million, and it still wasn't finished." (It was finished in 1881.)

  or "according to Nast's biographer, Tweed said...". Note also its condescension toward Tweed's own voters.

**Fall, escape, capture, death**

- **Arrest:** October 27, 1871. [museum: LPC] **Trials:** January 1873 (hung jury); November 1873, convicted on 204 counts; 12 years and
  a $12,750 fine, cut by the Court of Appeals to one year and $250. [museum: LPC; Wikipedia] At the penitentiary: "'What occupation?'
  'Statesman.' 'What religion?' 'None.'" [period: Orth 1919; a good line, flag as anecdote] Released January 1875, re-arrested at once on
  the state's civil suit; held in Ludlow Street Jail on $3,000,000 bail [Orth].
- **Escape:** the evening of **December 4, 1875**, "while on leave from prison" (a home visit with his guards), he slipped away; hid in
  New Jersey and New York, then reached **Cuba**, then **Spain**. [museum: LPC] Details of the route (a schooner to Cuba; sailing from
  Santiago de Cuba to Vigo as a common seaman on a Spanish ship) are in secondary accounts only [unverified this pass].
- **Capture in Spain:** Nast's July 1, 1876 cover "Tweed-le-dee and Tilden-dum" showed Tweed in prison stripes, holding two ragged boys
  by the collar (the "lesser thieves" he offered to catch). Paine: "suddenly came a report--a cable--that one 'Twid' (Tweed) had been
  identified and captured at Vigo, Spain, on the charge of 'kidnapping two American children.' ... The 'street gamins'--to the
  Spanish officer, who did not read English--were two children being forcibly abducted by the big man of the stripes and club."
  "Absurd as it all was, the identification was flawless. Tweed, on board the steamer Franklin, came back to America to die."
  [primary/period: Paine 1904, pp. 335 to 336] MHS: "he fled to Spain where officials identified Tweed from a Nast drawing on the
  cover of *Harper's*"; "they thought he had been charged with child abduction." [museum: MHS] *Harper's* republished the drawing on
  October 7, 1876 with the capture story [Paine], which is why some clippings misdate it; Britannica also credits a Nast caricature
  for the identification and arrest in Vigo, 1876 [secondary]. **Verdict: confirmed as the standard account** (Paine, MHS, Britannica,
  Adler and Hill, *Doomed by Cartoon*, 2008). Caveats: Paine was Nast's champion; U.S. officials also sent descriptions, and Spain
  had no extradition treaty, so the handover was diplomatic. [secondary: Daily Cartoonist summary of Adler and Hill] Arrest date
  usually given as early to mid September 1876 [unverified]; the Spectator (London) reported it on September 16, 1876. Returned on the
  U.S. Navy ship *Franklin*; back in New York **November 23, 1876**. [museum: LPC] Paine says when his baggage was searched "he had
  preserved every cartoon Nast had drawn of him" [period; flag as anecdote].
- **Death:** April 12, 1878, in **Ludlow Street Jail** (pneumonia), still a prisoner, having testified about the Ring in hopes of
  release. [museum: LPC; secondary: Wikipedia citing Ackerman] Buried in Green-Wood Cemetery, Brooklyn. [secondary] LPC quotes a
  contemporary: "a prisoner, exhausted, forsaken, miserable, betrayed, sick, William M. Tweed lies a-dying. From how high up to how low
  down." [museum: LPC, p. 4]
- **Aftermath:** Tammany came back fast: "in 1874 Tammany returned to power," under "Honest John" Kelly. [period: Orth 1919] So by the
  Kellys' 1880s the machines were not gone; they had learned to be careful.

### 4.6 What machines built and gave (the "helper" side)

- **Tweed:** used the city government to build more orphanages, almshouses and public baths, and pushed public money to private
  charities, Catholic schools and hospitals; the 1870 "Tweed Charter" gave the city more home rule (bought with about $600,000 in
  bribes in Albany, Tweed later confessed). [secondary: Wikipedia citing Ackerman and Burrows and Wallace; Orth 1919 on the $600,000]
- **McLaughlin's Brooklyn:** played a role in creating **Prospect Park** and building the **Brooklyn Bridge**. [secondary: Wikipedia
  "Hugh McLaughlin"; thin sourcing, see 4.8]
- **Jobs on public works:** streets, sewers, docks, parks and bridges were the machine's employment office; Plunkitt brags that his
  department friends got raises and that city departments had a job "picked out" for a newcomer. [primary: Plunkitt, chs. 1 and 7]
- **A path into politics for the Irish:** Irish Catholics, shut out of Protestant business elites, rose through the machine: Kelly,
  Croker, Murphy at Tammany; McLaughlin and McCarren in Brooklyn. [secondary/standard; e.g., Steven Erie, *Rainbow's End: Irish-Americans
  and the Dilemmas of Urban Machine Politics, 1840 to 1985* (1988), not checked this pass] Erie's argument (that machines gave the
  Irish jobs but did much less for later groups) is the scholarly counterweight to the "helper" side [unverified summary].
- **Speed and humanity:** Plunkitt's jab at the Charity Organization Society, which "would investigate their case in a month or two
  and decide they were worthy of help about the time they are dead from starvation." [primary: Plunkitt, ch. 6] Pair with the 1888
  squirrel line (4.3) to show why poor voters preferred the machine's help.

### 4.7 Plunkitt (Riordon, *Plunkitt of Tammany Hall*, 1905)

All [primary] from William L. Riordon, *Plunkitt of Tammany Hall* (McClure, Phillips, 1905), Project Gutenberg #2810. Riordon was a
newspaper reporter (he says he first published the talks in the *Evening Post*, *Sun*, *World* and *Boston Transcript*); Plunkitt
"delivered" them "from his rostrum, the bootblack stand in the County Court-house" (which is the Tweed Courthouse).

- **Who:** Tammany leader of the Fifteenth Assembly District (Manhattan's West Side), who "boasts of his record in filling four public
  offices in one year and drawing salaries from three of them at the same time" (1870: Assemblyman, Alderman, Police Magistrate,
  County Supervisor). "Plunkitt is now a millionaire." Started as a cart driver and butcher's boy. (Preface) Born 1842, died 1924
  [unverified this pass; standard].
- **The fire account (Chapter 6, "To Hold Your District: Study Human Nature and Act Accordin'"), exact:**
  > What tells in holdin' your grip on your district is to go right down among the poor families and help them in the different ways
  > they need help. I've got a regular system for this. If there's a fire in Ninth, Tenth, or Eleventh Avenue, for example, any hour
  > of the day or night, I'm usually there with some of my election district captains as soon as the fire engines. If a family is
  > burned out I don't ask whether they are Republicans or Democrats, and I don't refer them to the Charity Organization Society,
  > which would investigate their case in a month or two and decide they were worthy of help about the time they are dead from
  > starvation. I just get quarters for them, buy clothes for them if their clothes were burned up, and fix them up till they get
  > things runnin' again. It's philanthropy, but it's politics, too--mighty good politics. Who can tell how many votes one of these
  > fires bring me? The poor are the most grateful people in the world, and, let me tell you, they have more friends in their
  > neighborhoods than the rich have in theirs.

  Next paragraph: "If there's a family in my district in want I know it before the charitable societies do, and me and my men are
  first on the ground. I have a special corps to look up such cases. The consequence is that the poor look up to George W. Plunkitt
  as a father, come to him in trouble--and don't forget him on election day."
- **Riordon's diary version (Chapter 23):** "6.A.M.: Awakened by fire engines passing his house. Hastened to the scene of the fire,
  according to the custom of the Tammany district leaders, to give assistance to the fire sufferers, if needed. Met several of his
  election district captains who are always under orders to look out for fires, which are considered great vote-getters. Found
  several tenants who had been burned out, took them to a hotel, supplied them with clothes, fed them, and arranged temporary
  quarters for them until they could rent and furnish new apartments." And rival leaders "tries to reach the fires before his rival."
- **Cold open check:** documented: arriving with the engines, any hour, with captains; not asking party; rooms (a hotel, then
  "temporary quarters"); clothes; food. **Not in the text:** "pays the rent" (that is a separate 9 a.m. eviction case) and "food for a
  week." Adjust the cold open to "rooms for the night, new clothes, a hot meal, a place to stay until they're back on their feet."
  The 3 a.m. timing is fine ("any hour of the day or night"); the diary's fire is at 6 a.m.
- **Honest graft (Chapter 1, "Honest Graft and Dishonest Graft"), exact:** "There's an honest graft, and I'm an example of how it
  works. I might sum up the whole thing by sayin': 'I seen my opportunities and I took 'em.'" Example: "I'm tipped off, say, that
  they're going to lay out a new park at a certain place. I see my opportunity and I take it. I go to that place and I buy up all the
  land I can in the neighborhood." "They couldn't make the park complete without Plunkitt's swamp, and they had to pay a good price
  for it. Anything dishonest in that?" Dishonest graft, which he disowns: "blackmailin' gamblers, saloonkeepers, disorderly people,
  etc." On reform audits: "The books are always all right." The epitaph: "George W. Plunkitt. He Seen His Opportunities, and He Took
  'Em." Also the 250,000 old granite paving blocks he bought for $2.50 by buying off the rival bidders.
- **On theft vs. "pull":** "The politician who steals is worse than a thief. He is a fool. With the grand opportunities all around for
  the man with a political pull, there's no excuse for stealin' a cent." (Chapter 7, "On The Shame of the Cities")
- **Caveats for narration:** (1) It is 1905, twenty years after the episode's present and thirty after Tweed; narrate it as "how a
  machine worked," with "a Tammany leader later bragged..." (2) It is performance: Riordon shaped the talks for newspapers, Plunkitt
  was showing off, and the "diary" chapter is Riordon's composite. (3) His "honest graft" is insider dealing (using secret knowledge of
  public plans for private profit): legal-ish then, a crime or ethics violation now. (4) Plunkitt's Fifteenth District was Manhattan
  Tammany, not Brooklyn.

### 4.8 Brooklyn's machine in the 1880s

- **Brooklyn was its own city** until consolidation on **January 1, 1898**; its Democratic machine was not Tammany. [secondary;
  ep3/RESEARCH.md 4.11]
- **Hugh McLaughlin** (born April 2, 1827, Brooklyn, son of Irish immigrants; rope maker by trade; died December 7, 1904): in 1855
  master mechanic at the **Brooklyn Navy Yard**, a post whose hiring he used to build support; Kings County Register 1861 for three
  terms; from **1862** boss of the "Brooklyn ring" for about forty years, until forced out in 1903 by Patrick McCarren. [secondary:
  Wikipedia "Hugh McLaughlin (politician)," citing *Who Was Who in America*; birth year given as 1823, 1826 or 1827 in other sources]
  Nickname "Boss" McLaughlin [secondary: Find a Grave]. His base on **Willoughby Street** (the "Willoughby Street" machine) is a
  commonly repeated detail [unverified this pass; check the *Brooklyn Daily Eagle* via Brooklyn Public Library before narrating].
  **Sourcing is thin**: before narrating anything beyond "Brooklyn had its own Democratic machine, run for forty years by an Irish
  Brooklynite named Hugh McLaughlin," get one more source (NYT or *Eagle* obituary, December 8, 1904; Brooklyn Public Library).
- **Patrick H. McCarren** (born July 8, 1849, East Cambridge, Massachusetts; died October 23, 1909, Williamsburg): trained as a cooper
  (barrel maker); Assembly 1882 to 1883 and 1889; State Senate 1890 to 1893 and 1896 to 1909; Brooklyn's Democratic boss after 1903;
  motto "The Tiger Shall Not Cross The Bridge" (keep Tammany out of Brooklyn). [secondary: Wikipedia, citing Oliver Allen, *The Tiger*,
  1993] Several local histories say he **first worked in Williamsburg's sugar refineries**, then as a cooper, and was paid to protect
  the sugar and oil interests; a local history calls him the "Sugar Senator." [secondary, weak: Bowery Boys; Irish Echo 2022; Greenpointers
  2017, paywalled] **This would tie Michael Kelly's refinery (Ep 1) to the machine.** [VERIFY with a *Brooklyn Daily Eagle* source
  before narrating] McCarren Park in Williamsburg/Greenpoint is named for him.
- **Seth Low**, Republican reform mayor of Brooklyn, elected November 1881, served **1882 to 1885** (two two-year terms; one Wikipedia
  infobox says 1881, but the navbox and standard sources say 1882 to 1885 [flag]). Beat the Democrat James Howell 45,434 to 40,937;
  introduced a civil service code for city jobs, competitive exams for teachers, free textbooks; raised taxes. After him the machine
  returned. Later president of Columbia (1890 to 1901) and mayor of Greater New York (1902 to 1903). [secondary: Wikipedia "Seth Low"]
  The machine vs Low is the Kelly brother's real-world backdrop in 1884: the machine was out of City Hall but still had the county,
  the ward clubs and the Navy Yard.

### 4.9 Reform in the 1880s (only what the episode needs)

- **Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act**, signed **January 16, 1883**, by Chester A. Arthur, after President Garfield was assassinated
  (1881) by a disappointed office seeker; created the Civil Service Commission and **competitive exams**; banned firing covered
  workers for political reasons and forcing them to give money; at first covered **about 10%** of the government's 132,000 employees.
  [museum: National Archives, Milestone Documents, "Pendleton Act"] Only federal jobs: it did not touch city machines directly.
  New York State passed its own civil service law in 1883 (Theodore Roosevelt in the Assembly) [unverified this pass].
- **Municipal reformers** ("good government," later "goo-goos"): businessmen's committees like the 1871 **Committee of Seventy**
  (formed at the second Cooper Institute meeting, September 4, 1871) [period: Orth 1919]; Seth Low in Brooklyn; scientific charity
  (Charity Organization Society, New York, 1882 [unverified]; Brooklyn Bureau of Charities, 1878).
- **Plunkitt's answer to reformers** (useful for balance): reformers are "mornin' glories" (Chapter 4 title): they bloom and fade.

### 4.10 Naturalization rules in the 1880s

- **The law** [primary: Revised Statutes of the United States (1874), Title 30, §§ 2165 to 2167, carrying forward the Act of April 14,
  1802]: an alien man declares on oath, "two years, at least, prior to his admission," his intention to become a citizen ("first
  papers," the declaration of intention), before a federal court "or a court of record of any of the States having common-law
  jurisdiction, and a seal and clerk"; at admission he must prove **five years' residence** in the United States (and one in the
  state, by the Act of 1802) and good moral character, with citizen witnesses. A man who arrived **under 18** and had lived here five
  years could be admitted at 21 without the two-year declaration (§ 2167: three years' residence before turning 21). Wives and minor
  children of a naturalized man became citizens with him (§ 2172 for children). [primary]
- **Why it mattered to machines:** any state court of record could naturalize, so friendly judges did it in bulk before elections;
  ward heelers paid fees, supplied witnesses and walked men through (Orth: "one a minute"; 25,000 to 30,000 in six weeks in 1868).
  [primary/period: Orth 1919] Federal control and standard forms came only with the **Naturalization Act of 1906**, after which
  "Tens of thousands of voters who thought they were citizens found that their papers were only declarations of intentions, or
  'first papers.'" [period: Orth 1919]
- **Who could not:** the Revised Statutes limited naturalization to "free white persons" and persons of African birth or descent, and
  the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act barred Chinese from citizenship (Ep 3). [standard; § 2169 not quoted this pass]
- **The Rabinowitz callback:** the father arrived 1882 (Ep 3); with first papers soon after arrival, his earliest admission is 1887.
  So in 1884 the ward heeler can only say "remember us when you can vote" or help him file first papers. [derived from the rules above]
- **Voting in New York** also required state and district residence periods and (from the 1870s to 1890s, in New York City) registration
  [unverified this pass]; not needed in narration.

## 5. Key terms (bold where first defined in the script)

| Term | Plain definition |
|---|---|
| **Urbanization** | People moving from farms and villages into cities, so cities grow. |
| **Tenement** | A crowded apartment building for poor families (Ep 1 term; repeat briefly). |
| **Skyscraper** | A tall building held up by a metal frame inside instead of thick outside walls. |

## 8. Humor and modern connections (evergreen, light)

- **The receipt:** "Forty chairs and three tables: $179,729.60. That's about $4,500 a chair. For that price, the chair should come with a
  job." Then the deadpan *Times* line: "Articles (probably Brooms, etc.)". Let the cents carry the joke: Paine asked, "Why always these
  odd cents?"
- **The thermometers:** $7,500 for thermometers in a building with no curtains. "It was the hottest building in New York, and nobody
  could prove it."
- **The plasterer:** "$138,187 in two days, in the depths of winter." The *Times* already wrote the joke ("the Prince of Plasterers");
  just read it.
- **Caught by a cartoon:** the most famous wanted poster in American history was a joke drawing, and the police misread the joke.
  Modern hook, kept general: a picture spreads faster than a paragraph; "them damned pictures" is why political cartoons still matter.
- **Two Kellys:** the 1880s Tammany boss was "Honest John" Kelly. A wink: "No relation. Probably." (Only if it does not confuse.)
- **"Honest graft" as a phrase:** students can spot the oxymoron; ask what we call it now (insider dealing, conflict of interest).
- **Plunkitt's school advice:** college "rot" makes you "handicapped at the outset ... the chances are 100 to 1 against him." A light
  line for a student audience; frame it as Plunkitt showing off.
- **The safety net then and now:** today when a family is burned out, the Red Cross and city agencies show up; in the 1880s it was the
  ward boss. Evergreen, no politics.
- Avoid: Irish drinking jokes (Riis and Nast both lean on the stereotype; Nast drew the Irish as apes in some cartoons, so choose Tweed
  cartoons that do not caricature Irish voters, or frame them); no current-politics comparisons by name.
