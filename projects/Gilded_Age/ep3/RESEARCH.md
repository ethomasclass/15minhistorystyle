# Gilded Age Episode 3: research

**Working title:** *The Golden Door* (or *Who Holds the Door*, or *New Neighbors*)
**Status:** research pass 1 and a proposed outline, Nov 11, 2026. Not a script yet.
**Length:** about 1,700 words of narration (10 to 11 minutes).
**Covers:** why Jews left the Russian Empire (the 1881 to 1882 pogroms, the May Laws, the Pale); the steerage crossing from
Hamburg; Castle Garden (1855 to 1890), the Barge Office, and a flash-forward to Ellis Island (1892); "old" vs "new" immigrants and
the numbers; chain migration, letters and prepaid tickets, landsmanshaftn and aid societies; the garment trade and tenement
sweatshops; nativism (Chinese Exclusion 1882, the contract labor law 1885, the APA 1887, the 1891 Act, the Immigration Restriction
League 1894), the Statue of Liberty and "The New Colossus" as contrast; the Ellis Island name-change myth; Keppler's "Looking
Backward" (1893).
**Family thread:** the Rabinowitzes, a composite Jewish family from the Russian Empire, move into the Kellys' Williamsburg
tenement in 1884. Ma Kelly, who came from Ireland through the same Castle Garden about 1870, watches from her window with her arms
folded. The Kellys experience history, they do not cause it. Composite disclaimer on screen for both families.
**Unit rule:** the script never names the unit's compelling question. This episode quietly fills the "ordinary people" column
(family networks, landsmanshaftn, aid societies, an Irish bank built by immigrants, a pedestal paid for in coins) and shows the
limits (sweatshop bosses were often immigrants too; the government's main new role was deciding who to keep out).

Confidence key used below: **[primary]** = checked against an 1880s/1890s/1910s document in this pass; **[museum]** = a museum,
park or archive page; **[secondary]** = reputable secondary source; **[unverified]** = from the brief or memory, not yet checked;
do not narrate until checked.

## 1. What a student should be able to explain after watching

1. In the 1880s immigration to the U.S. hit a record (5.2 million in the decade) and began to shift from northern and western
   Europe ("old" immigrants: Irish, German, British, Scandinavian) toward southern and eastern Europe ("new" immigrants: Jews,
   Italians, Poles, Slovaks and others).
2. Russian Jews left because of violence (the pogroms of 1881 to 1882) and laws that squeezed them (the Pale of Settlement and the
   May Laws of 1882). Unlike most groups, they came as whole families and almost never went back.
3. Most newcomers did not arrive alone or blind. Letters, prepaid tickets, relatives, hometown societies (landsmanshaftn), and aid
   groups carried them across the ocean and into jobs and rooms.
4. In New York many Jewish immigrants went into the garment trade, often in tenement "sweatshops" run by contractors, paid by the
   piece, working far more than ten hours a day.
5. A country built by immigrants also built walls against them: the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) shut out one nationality by law;
   other laws and groups targeted the poor, the sick, contract workers and Catholics. People who had once been targets, like the
   Irish, could turn on the next arrivals.
6. Officials at the station did not change immigrants' names. Names changed later, mostly by the immigrants' own choice.

## 4. Verified facts, by section

### 4.1 Why they left: pogroms, the May Laws, the Pale

- **Assassination.** Tsar Alexander II was killed in St. Petersburg on March 1, 1881 (Old Style; March 13 New Style) by members of
  the revolutionary group People's Will (Narodnaia Volia). A rumor that Jews were behind it fed the violence. [secondary: YIVO via
  search; encyclopedia.com]
- **Pogroms.** The first broke out in Elisavetgrad (today Kropyvnytskyi, Ukraine) on April 15, 1881 (April 27 New Style), from a
  quarrel in a tavern; copycat riots followed through 1881 and into 1882, in more than 150 settlements (estimates of the number of
  pogroms run 165 to 250). The Balta pogrom (spring 1882) was the worst of the second wave. [secondary: YIVO, encyclopedia.com,
  Klier, *Russians, Jews, and the Pogroms of 1881 to 1882*, 2011] Deaths were in the dozens, not thousands; the damage was mostly
  property, beatings and rapes. Safe: "mobs attacked Jewish homes and shops in town after town." Avoid a death toll.
- **Pogrom** (Russian *pogrom*, "devastation") entered English through these events. [unverified this pass; standard]
- **May Laws.** The "Temporary Regulations" of May 3 (15), 1882 barred Jews from settling anew outside towns and townlets, voided
  their property deals outside them, and limited Sunday and holiday trading. "Temporary," they lasted until 1917. [secondary:
  encyclopedia.com "May Laws"; Klier]
- **Pale of Settlement.** The western region of the Russian Empire (much of today's Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, Moldova and
  eastern Poland) where most Jews were required to live, created under Catherine II (from 1791). About five million Jews lived in
  the Russian Empire by the 1890s. [unverified this pass; standard; check YIVO "Pale of Settlement" before narrating numbers]
- **Who left.** Samuel Joseph's 1914 Columbia study (built on U.S. and Jewish charity records) found the Jewish movement was
  "essentially a family movement": from 1886 to 1898, 41.6% of Jewish adults landing at New York were women and 33.8% of all Jewish
  arrivals were children under sixteen; and Jews had the smallest return rate of any group. [primary: Joseph 1914, ch. on Family
  Movement] So a whole Rabinowitz family arriving together, to stay, is typical.

### 4.2 Getting there: rail to Hamburg, the steerage crossing

- **Route.** In 1881 to 1882 many refugees gathered at the Austrian border town of Brody (Galicia), where Jewish charities sent
  them west. Later in the 1880s most crossed into Germany and took trains to Hamburg or Bremen. [secondary: REFERENCES.md notes;
  Hamburg "Jewish Migration" exhibition, keydocuments.net] Over a million Eastern Europeans, mainly Jews, sailed from Hamburg to
  the U.S. between the 1880s and 1914. [museum: keydocuments.net exhibition]
- **Lines.** In the 1880s the two German giants, HAPAG (Hamburg-America Line, Hamburg) and Norddeutscher Lloyd (Bremen), built the
  Eastern European steerage trade. [museum: keydocuments.net] Both lines docked at **Hoboken, New Jersey**, so steerage passengers
  were taken across the Hudson by barge to Castle Garden. [unverified this pass; standard; check before narrating "Hoboken"]
- **Length.** Average Atlantic transit to New York about 11 days in 1890, 10 1/2 by 1900 (all lines). [secondary: Keeling,
  "Oceanic Travel Conditions and American Immigration, 1890 to 1914," MPRA 47850, 2013, p. 2 n. 5] German lines from Hamburg were
  slower than the Liverpool express ships. Safe: "about two weeks" or "ten days to two weeks."
- **Cost.** Keeling: by the 1880s "the average passage price had become equivalent to only about a month's unskilled wages in the
  United States." [secondary: Keeling 2013, p. 2] Dollar fares for 1884 Hamburg steerage were **not found**; $20 to $30 is
  widely repeated but unconfirmed, and rate wars in 1885 to 1895 pushed fares up and down. [unverified] An 1883 NDL prepaid ticket
  for a family cost $72 (GG Archives listing, page now 404; number from search snippet only). Safe: "about a month's pay for a
  laborer in America."
- **Prepaid tickets.** The market in "prepaid" tickets, bought in America for someone in Europe, grew with steam; the lines' New York
  Continental Conference tried to control it from 1885 to 1895. [secondary: Feys, *Revista de Historia Economica*, 2008]
- **Conditions.** The 1882 Passenger Act set space rules but was "honored in breach"; open-berth steerage had poor dining
  facilities, with the excuse that immigrants "are not used to tables and chairs." [secondary: Keeling 2013, quoting period
  officials] Two-tier wooden berths, straw mattresses, single men and women in separate compartments, families in the middle, and
  seasickness are standard in descriptions but mostly come from 1900s reports (Immigration Commission, 1911). [secondary, later]
- **Ship types.** NDL's *Elbe* (1881) and similar single-screw iron liners; see REFERENCES §1. HAPAG ships of the period include
  *Hammonia* (1882) and *Rhaetia* (1883). [unverified this pass]

### 4.3 Castle Garden, the Barge Office, and Ellis Island (flash-forward)

- **Castle Garden.** The round sandstone fort at the Battery (Castle Clinton, built 1808 to 1811) became the Emigrant Landing Depot
  of the **New York State** Commissioners of Emigration in 1855, with virtually no federal role. Opening date: August 1, 1855 (most
  sources) or August 3 (NPS history by George Svejda, 1968). [museum: NPS; Norway Heritage] It closed April 18, 1890, when the
  federal government took over. [museum/secondary, agreed]
- **How many.** About 8 million people (sources range 7.5 to 8.5 million; one count gives 8,280,917 of 10,956,910 arrivals,
  1855 to 1890). [secondary] Safe: "about eight million people," "about two of every three immigrants of those years."
- **Procedure, 1870s to 1880s** (period description, Norway Heritage transcription): a transfer boat with "boarding officers and
  customs officials" met the ship; at the seaward entrance each arrival was asked "questions as to his age, nativity, occupation,
  companions, condition of health"; doubtful cases were held in a "large pen, or cage"; the rest were "hurried on through a
  maze-like succession of barriers"; inside the rotunda were "the money-changers, the railroad-ticket sellers, telegraph operators";
  a labor bureau matched people to jobs, sometimes within hours; the commissioners tried to keep out the **runners** (touts for
  boarding houses who "cast their nets for the unwary stranger") at the steamship wharves. [primary/period via museum site]
- **Health.** Ships were checked first at the state Quarantine station in the lower harbor (Staten Island). [unverified this pass;
  standard]
- **The 1882 federal law.** The Immigration Act of August 3, 1882 put a **50-cent head tax** on each arrival and barred
  "any convict, lunatic, idiot, or any person unable to take care of himself or herself without becoming a public charge"; the
  Treasury contracted with the states (New York kept running Castle Garden). [secondary: multiple; statute 22 Stat. 214, check
  wording]
- **Barge Office.** April 1890 to December 31, 1891, arrivals were processed at the Barge Office at the Battery while Ellis Island
  was built. [secondary, agreed]
- **Ellis Island.** Opened **January 1, 1892**. The first person registered was Annie Moore from County Cork, Ireland, who arrived on
  the steamship *Nevada*; she is often called 15, but genealogist Megan Smolenyak's research (2006) shows she was 17. [secondary:
  Irish Times; HNN; Smolenyak] The wooden station burned June 15, 1897; arrivals went back to the Barge Office until the new
  fireproof building opened December 17, 1900. [secondary; Dec 17 date unverified this pass]
- **The "six-second" exam.** Public Health Service doctors watched each arrival climb the stairs and walk past (the "line
  inspection"); staff called it the six-second medical exam. [secondary: PHS history, National Archives *Prologue* blog]
- **Chalk marks.** Doctors chalked a letter on the coat of anyone needing a closer look, for example E (eyes), H (heart), L
  (lameness), Pg (pregnancy), Sc (scalp), Ft (feet), K (hernia), X (suspected mental defect), X in a circle (definite). About 15 to
  20% were marked; very few (about 2%) were finally sent back. [secondary: AMA *Virtual Mentor* 2008 via search; NPS; numbers to
  check against NPS before narrating]
- **Buttonhook.** Doctors flipped eyelids with fingers or a buttonhook to look for **trachoma**, an eye infection that could cause
  blindness. Trachoma became grounds for exclusion in **1897** and was the leading medical cause of rejection in the 1900s.
  [secondary: National Archives *Prologue* 2014; Public Health Reports 1998 via search] **So the buttonhook belongs to the Ellis
  Island of about 1900 to 1914, not to opening day in 1892, and not to Castle Garden.**
- **The 1891 Act** (March 3, 1891) created the federal Office of the Superintendent of Immigration, added people with "a loathsome or
  dangerous contagious disease" and polygamists to the excluded list, and made steamship lines carry rejected passengers home.
  [secondary: Immigration History (immigrationhistory.org); statute 26 Stat. 1084, check wording]

### 4.4 Old vs new immigrants: the numbers

Official counts (fiscal years; DHS Yearbook 2022, Table 1, summed by decade in this pass) [primary/official]:

| Decade | Arrivals | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1871 to 1880 | 2,812,191 | depression years 1874 to 1879 dip below 200,000 a year |
| 1881 to 1890 | 5,246,613 | record decade; **1882 = 788,992**, the peak year of the century |
| 1891 to 1900 | 3,687,564 | Panic of 1893 cuts arrivals (Ep 6) |
| 1901 to 1910 | 8,795,386 | over 1 million a year in 1905, 1906, 1907, 1910 |

- **1884** (our year): 518,592 arrivals; Joseph counts **11,445 Jewish** (2.2%), of whom 7,867 were from Russia. [primary: Joseph
  1914, Tables; he warns the 1881 to 1885 Jewish figures are estimates]
- **Jewish immigration by decade** (Joseph 1914): 193,021 (1881 to 1890), 393,516 (1891 to 1900), 976,263 (1901 to 1910); Russian
  Jews 135,003 in the 1880s. [primary] Over 1881 to 1914 about two million Eastern European Jews came to the U.S. (estimates 1.7
  to 2+ million). [secondary]
- **"Old" and "new."** Old: Ireland, Germany, Britain, Scandinavia (mostly Protestant except the Irish and many Germans). New:
  Italy, Austria-Hungary, the Russian Empire, later Greece and others (Catholic, Orthodox, Jewish). The new group is commonly said to
  have outnumbered the old from **1896**. [secondary; standard textbook claim, not checked against the yearly tables] The labels
  were made official, and loaded, by the Dillingham Commission (1907 to 1911), which treated "new" as less desirable. Joseph (1914)
  already uses "old" and "new" in quotation marks. [primary: Joseph]
- **Where they settled.** Jews: Manhattan's Lower East Side (the Tenth, Thirteenth and Seventh Wards). Riis (1890) says the crowding
  "run up the record here to the rate of three hundred and thirty thousand per square mile" and "it is said that nowhere in the
  world are so many people crowded together on a square mile as here." [primary: Riis, *How the Other Half Lives*, ch. X]
- **Williamsburg.** Williamsburg's first Jews were **German** Jews (from the 1840s to 1850s); the big Eastern European wave came
  **after the Williamsburg Bridge opened in 1903** (a 1904 paper called it "the Jews' Highway"). [secondary: Barry Popik; Brooklyn
  Jewish Historical Initiative; Macaulay "Peopling of New York"] So **a Russian Jewish family in Williamsburg in 1884 is possible but
  early and unusual.** See Decisions.

### 4.5 Chain migration, letters, prepaid tickets, mutual aid

- **Chain migration** = one family member goes first, then sends letters, money and tickets for the next. Joseph's figures (4.1)
  show Jews coming as families; men often went first as an "avant-garde to prepare the way for their families." [primary: Joseph
  1914]
- **Landsmanshaftn** (Yiddish, singular *landsmanshaft*; a *landsman* is someone from your hometown): hometown societies that gave
  members sick pay, death benefits, doctors, loans, a place to pray and meet, and above all a **burial plot**; many still own plots
  in New York cemeteries. The WPA Yiddish Writers' Group counted **2,468** in New York City in the late 1930s; estimates for all
  time run 1,000 to 10,000. [secondary: Daniel Soyer, *Jewish Immigrant Associations and American Identity in New York, 1880 to
  1939*, 1997; WPA, *Di yidishe landsmanshaftn fun Nyu York*, 1938] In 1884 the movement was young: say "soon" or "in the years
  that followed," not that the Rabinowitzes joined one on arrival. [judgment]
- **Hebrew Emigrant Aid Society (HEAS).** Founded in New York on November 27, 1881, to aid refugees from the pogroms; ran shelters
  including one at Castle Garden and the Schiff refuge on **Ward's Island**; wound down 1883 to 1884, its work passing to the United
  Hebrew Charities. It is **not** HIAS (the Hebrew Immigrant Aid Society, formed 1902, merged 1909), though HIAS dates its roots to
  1881. [secondary: Wikipedia "Hebrew Emigrant Aid Society" and sources there] Many popular sources wrongly say Lazarus worked for
  "HIAS."
- **Emma Lazarus** (1849 to 1887), a New York poet from an old Sephardic Jewish family, volunteered with the refugees in 1881 to
  1882, taught English, and visited Ward's Island; she complained in print that the shelter had "not a drop of running water" in
  the dormitories. [secondary: Jewish Women's Archive; AJHS "Among the Russian Refugees"]
- **The Irish did it first.** The Irish Emigrant Society (1841) founded the **Emigrant Industrial Savings Bank** in 1850 (chartered
  April 10, opened September 30), partly because Irish depositors were mistreated at other banks; through it immigrants bought bills
  of exchange to send money home. [secondary: NYPL Emigrant Savings Bank records; SNAC] Irish "remittances" paid for much of the
  passage of the next family member (Kerby Miller, *Emigrants and Exiles*, 1985). [secondary; book not checked this pass] So Ma
  Kelly likely came on money sent by a relative already here: the same chain she now watches the Rabinowitzes use.

### 4.6 The garment trade and sweatshops (1880s to early 1890s)

All [primary] from Jacob Riis, *How the Other Half Lives* (1890), ch. XI, "The Sweaters of Jewtown," unless marked. Riis writes
with open prejudice ("constitutional greed," the chapter title itself); quote his numbers and scenes, not his judgments.

- **The sweater** "is simply the middleman, the sub-contractor, a workman like his fellows," with "two or three sewing-machines, or
  of credit enough to hire them," who "drums up work among the clothing-houses." "Often there are two, sometimes three, sets of
  sweaters on one job." New arrivals were working the day they landed: "The sun sets upon the day of the arrival of many a Polish
  Jew, finding him at work in an East Side tenement, treading the machine and 'learning the trade.'"
- **Laws that did not reach home.** Factory law said ten hours, no work after 9 p.m., no child under fourteen; but "the bulk of the
  sweater's work is done in the tenements, which the law that regulates factory labor does not reach." In the tenement "the child
  works unchallenged from the day he is old enough to pull a thread." One man asked Riis, about learning English: "When shall we
  find time to learn?"
- **A Ludlow Street shop (Riis's visit):** five men, a woman, two girls "not fifteen," a boy lying about being fifteen; 120 dozen
  knee-pants a week at 70 cents a dozen; "learners" earn "from two to five dollars" a week; "from daybreak" to 9 at night; ten
  machines, six hired at $2 a month; two rooms at $20 a month.
- **Piece rates:** an operator working "twelve hours a day" for "forty-two cents a dozen" makes "less than eight dollars" a week; the
  finisher gets ten cents and the ironer eight cents a dozen. Cloakmakers: idle "six months of the year"; 75 cents a cloak; $7 a week;
  in the busy season two partners worked "from 6 A.M. to 11 P.M." for $7.58 to $9.60 each. A wife finishing earns "a dollar and a
  half" a week; a twelve-year-old Italian girl finisher, "a dollar and a half."
- **The task system** (coat trade): a team (operator at the machine, baster, finisher, presser) contracted to make a set number of
  coats as "a day's work"; pay was per task, so when the contractor cut the price he raised the task and the "day" stretched to 14 or
  more hours. Period reports called it "the height of the sweating evil." [secondary/period: Wisconsin-hosted labor report "Sweating
  in the Garment-Making Trades" via search snippet; John R. Commons, U.S. Industrial Commission vol. XV (1901), not fetched; strikes
  in 1890s New York aimed to abolish it] Safe: "paid by the piece or by the 'task,' a quota that grew whenever the price fell."
- **Scale (later):** by 1897 about 60% of New York's Jewish workers were in clothing, and Jews were about 75% of the trade's workers.
  [secondary: My Jewish Learning] 1880s figures not found; say "soon most of the city's cheap clothing was sewn by Jewish immigrants."
- **Riis's photographs** (about 1889) of Ludlow Street, Division Street and Elizabeth Street workshops are in REFERENCES §5.
- **Law:** New York regulated tenement clothing work in 1892 (licenses for tenement workshops) [unverified this pass, from
  REFERENCES]. Not needed in narration.

### 4.7 Nativism

- **Nativism** = favoring people born in the country over immigrants, often with fear or hatred of newcomers. The 1850s Know-Nothing
  (American) Party targeted Irish and German Catholics. [standard]
- **Chinese Exclusion Act** [museum: National Archives "Milestone Documents"; primary: enrolled Act image in REFERENCES §8]
  - Signed **May 6, 1882**, by President Chester A. Arthur.
  - Suspended the coming of Chinese **laborers** for **ten years**, starting 90 days after passage; merchants, students, teachers,
    diplomats and travelers were exempt; Chinese laborers already here could stay (and needed a certificate to come back after a
    trip); **no court could admit Chinese to citizenship**. (The National Archives summary fetched by tool garbled the 90-day clause
    as expelling residents; the Act's Sec. 1 makes it the start date of the suspension. [Act text, Secs. 1, 4 and 14, as I know them; recheck against the enrolled Act image before quoting])
  - The first major federal law to bar immigrants by nationality or ethnicity. [museum: National Archives]
  - **Geary Act** (May 5, 1892): extended it ten years and required Chinese residents to carry certificates of residence or face
    deportation. **Made permanent in 1902** (indefinite extension; fixed again 1904). **Repealed December 17, 1943** (the Magnuson
    Act), when China was a wartime ally, with a quota of **105** a year and the right to naturalize. [museum: National Archives;
    "Magnuson Act" name standard]
- **Denis Kearney** (born February 1, 1847, County Cork, Ireland; died 1907), leader of the Workingmen's Party of California
  (founded 1877), ended speeches with "The Chinese must go!" The party was gone by 1881; the Act followed in 1882. [secondary:
  Wikipedia; American Heritage] **The Kellys are from Cork too.** This is the documented version of the Irish-turn-on-newcomers irony.
- **Alien Contract Labor Law (Foran Act), February 26, 1885:** made it illegal to bring in foreigners under contract to work,
  including prepaying their passage in exchange for labor. Pushed by unions (the Knights of Labor) against strikebreakers.
  [secondary: encyclopedia.com; immigrationhistory.org] Note the twist for beat 3: a *relative's* prepaid ticket was legal; an
  *employer's* was not.
- **American Protective Association (APA):** founded **March 13, 1887**, in Clinton, Iowa, by lawyer Henry F. Bowers; anti-Catholic;
  members reportedly swore not to hire, vote for, or strike alongside Catholics [oath wording unverified]. Claimed 2.5 million
  members at its 1894 peak; historians estimate about 100,000 dues-payers. [secondary: Wikipedia; EBSCO; Catholic Encyclopedia]
  Its main target was Irish Catholics: **the Kellys**.
- **Immigration Restriction League:** founded in Boston by Harvard graduates Prescott F. Hall, Robert DeCourcy Ward and Charles
  Warren, usually dated **1894** (one textbook says 1893); pushed a **literacy test** (read 40 words in any language), passed by
  Congress and vetoed by Cleveland in 1897, enacted in 1917 over Wilson's veto. [secondary: SNAC; Europeana; vetoes standard,
  unverified this pass]
- **"No Irish Need Apply":** newspaper job ads (mostly for servants) are well attested; window signs are disputed (Jensen 2002 vs
  Fried 2016). Decision already made (REFERENCES): "some newspaper job ads said 'No Irish need apply'"; no window sign on screen.
- **Cartoons:** Opper's "The Irish Declaration of Independence that we are all familiar with" (Puck, May 9, 1883); "The Only One
  Barred Out" (Frank Leslie's, 1882, criticizing exclusion); Keppler's "Welcome to All!" (Puck, 1880) and "Uncle Sam's
  Lodging-House" (Puck, June 7, 1882); Gillam's "A Sop to Cerberus" (Puck, May 17, 1882). See REFERENCES §8 for handling.

### 4.8 The contrast: the Statue and the poem

- **"The New Colossus"** by Emma Lazarus is dated **November 2, 1883** (NPS); written for an art auction to raise money for the
  statue's pedestal (manuscript ends "Written in aid of Bartholdi Pedestal Fund"). [museum: NPS; primary: manuscript image] Lines to
  quote: "Mother of Exiles"; "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free"; "I lift my lamp beside
  the golden door!" [primary: poem]
- **The pedestal was paid for by ordinary people.** After the fund stalled, Joseph Pulitzer's *New York World* restarted the drive
  on March 16, 1885, printed every donor's name, and raised over $100,000 from about 120,000 to 125,000 people, most giving about a
  dollar or less; the goal was announced met on August 11, 1885. [museum: NPS Pulitzer pages; NPS handbook] Pulitzer was himself an
  immigrant (born in Hungary, 1847) [unverified this pass; standard].
- **Dedicated October 28, 1886** (President Grover Cleveland). [museum: REFERENCES print; standard] The poem was little known then;
  a bronze plaque with it was placed inside the pedestal in **1903**. [museum: NPS] Lazarus died in 1887 [secondary: JWA], so she
  never saw it there.
- **Timing for an 1884 episode:** the poem exists (1883), the pedestal is half built, the statue arrives in 1885 and is dedicated in
  1886. Say "two years later" from the cold open.

### 4.9 Names: the Ellis Island myth

- **USCIS History Office article, exact title (checked):** the uscis.gov page is titled **"Immigrant Name Changes"** (Genealogy
  Notebook, uscis.gov/records/genealogy/genealogy-notebook/immigrant-name-changes; "Last Reviewed/Updated: 01/24/2025"). It carries the
  essay **"American Names / Declaring Independence"** by **Marian L. Smith**, then INS Historian, introduced as "a July 4th essay." **No
  original publication date is printed** on the USCIS page or on the reprint; since she signs as INS (not USCIS) historian, it predates
  March 2003. Cite as: Marian L. Smith, "American Names / Declaring Independence," in USCIS, "Immigrant Name Changes" (Genealogy
  Notebook, page updated January 24, 2025). [museum/official: uscis.gov; reprint at iabsi.com]
- **Key lines (Smith):** "American name change stories tend to be apocryphal, that is, they developed later to explain events shrouded in
  the mist of time." "In fact, passenger lists were not created at Ellis Island. They were created abroad, beginning close to the
  immigrant's home." "One third of all immigrant inspectors at Ellis Island early this century were themselves foreign-born," assigned
  by language. Old immigrants who said "it was changed at Ellis Island" often meant the whole experience of becoming American.
  Official records of name changes at naturalization were only required from **1906**, and immigrants often changed names within their
  first five years. [official: USCIS page]
- **How names really changed:** spellings shifted at the ticket office and each transfer point (Cyrillic or Hebrew letters into a German
  clerk's spelling, then English); English-speaking record-keepers wrote names as they heard them on later documents; children changed
  first names at school; adults changed names for work, shop signs, or at naturalization. [official: USCIS; secondary: NYPL blog, Sutton
  2013; Smithsonian]
- **For 1884:** the same logic applies to Castle Garden: the ship's list came with the ship; the clerk copied from it. Castle Garden's own
  registers are largely lost, so say "the clerk" not "Castle Garden's records show." The lookdev clip `ep3_names` (Rabinovich,
  "Rabinowitsch" in Hamburg, "Rabinowitz" in New York, later "Rabin"; Rivke becomes Rebecca at school) is consistent with all of this.

## 5. Key terms (bold where first defined in the script)

| Term | Plain definition |
|---|---|
| **Immigrant** | A person who moves to a new country to live there. |
| **Pogrom** (POH-grum) | A violent attack by a mob on a Jewish community, often with officials looking the other way. |
| **Pale of Settlement** | The western part of the Russian Empire where most Jews were required to live. |
| **May Laws** | Russian rules of 1882 that pushed Jews out of villages and limited where they could live and trade. |
| **Steerage** | The cheapest space on a ship, below deck, packed with bunks. |
| **Prepaid ticket** | A ship ticket bought in America and mailed to someone in Europe. |
| **Chain migration** | One family member moves first, then helps the next one come, and so on, like links in a chain. |
| **Castle Garden** | New York's immigrant landing station from 1855 to 1890, in an old fort at the tip of Manhattan. |
| **Ellis Island** | The federal immigrant station in New York Harbor that replaced it in 1892. |
| **Manifest** (passenger list) | The ship's list of every passenger, written at the port where they boarded. |
| **"Old" and "new" immigrants** | Labels for the earlier arrivals from northern and western Europe and the later ones from southern and eastern Europe. Later officials used "new" as an insult. |
| **Landsmanshaft** (LONDS-mon-shoft) | A club of people from the same hometown in Europe that helped members with sick pay, loans and burial. |
| **Mutual aid** | People in the same situation pooling money to help whichever member is in trouble. |
| **Sweatshop** | A small, crowded workshop, often in a tenement room, where people sewed for long hours and low pay. |
| **Contractor** ("sweater") | A middleman who took cut cloth from a clothing company and paid others to sew it. |
| **Piecework** | Being paid for each piece you finish, not for each hour you work. |
| **Tenement** | A crowded apartment building for poor families (Ep 1 term; repeat briefly). |
| **Nativism** | Favoring people born in a country over immigrants, often with fear or anger toward newcomers. |
| **Exclusion** | Shutting a group out by law. |
| **Naturalization** | The legal process of becoming a citizen. |

## 8. Humor and modern connections (evergreen)

- **The name myth, flipped:** "Grandpa always said they changed our name at Ellis Island. The clerk didn't. Grandpa did. In 1911.
  Because a shop sign charged by the letter." (Last clause is a joke; do not present as fact.) Students can ask their own families.
- **The pedestal:** France gave the statue; Americans were supposed to pay for the base and almost didn't. A newspaper shamed the rich and
  printed the name of every kid who sent in pennies. The base of the Statue of Liberty was crowdfunded.
- **Remittances and group chats:** immigrants then, like many now, sent part of every paycheck home, and the letter home was the family
  group chat, just slower: two weeks each way.
- **Kids as translators:** Rivke learning English first and translating for her parents is still the experience of many students whose
  families are newer to the country. Handle with warmth, not as a punchline.
- **The quota that grows:** the task system as "the more you finish, the more they expect": a dry line, not a modern-workplace rant.
- **Lazarus's lamp vs the Exclusion Act:** the poem and the law are only a year apart. Let the contrast sit; no joke.
- Avoid: accent humor, food jokes about any group, anything that puts the caricatures' features in the punchline. No current-politics
  comparisons by name; evergreen only.
