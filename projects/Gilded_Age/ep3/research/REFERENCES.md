# Episode 3 (Immigration): visual references for the drawn sets

Look-development references only, for the code-drawn silhouette scenes in `lookdev/ep3.js`. Every file passed the
`tools/find_images.py` public-domain checks (copied from the Reconstruction project); credits in `public/img/credits_ref.json`.
Files live in `public/img/ref/<scene>/`; contact sheets in `research/ref_sheets/<scene>.jpg` (`python3 tools/ref_sheet.py`).
"LoC" = Library of Congress Prints and Photographs ("no known restrictions on publication"); "NYPL" = New York Public Library
scans on Wikimedia Commons (public domain). Setting: about 1884, so the station is **Castle Garden**, not Ellis Island.

Handling rule for the nativism prints (section 8): several contain **slurs or ethnic caricatures**. They are marked
**CARICATURE** or **SLUR** below. On screen they appear briefly, as creased prints on the desk, framed by the narration
(who made it, when, why it mattered), never as a joke, never lingered on, never animated to mock anyone. Crop out slur text where
the point survives without it; where the slur *is* the evidence (a printed law, a sign), say so in narration.

**"No Irish Need Apply"**: genuinely attested as **newspaper help-wanted ads** (mostly for domestic servants) and in a popular
1862 song, but historians dispute how common NINA **signs** in shop windows were. Richard Jensen ("No Irish Need Apply: A Myth of
Victimization," *Journal of Social History*, 2002) argued signs were rare and the memory exaggerated; Rebecca Fried ("No Irish Need
Deny," *Journal of Social History*, 2016) found dozens of NINA ads in American newspapers, including New York ones, through the
1800s. **Decision (Nov 9):** script wording "some newspaper job ads said 'No Irish need apply'"; never show a NINA window sign.

| **Nativism** (required) | Who was "in" and who was "out": the Know-Nothings of the 1850s turned on Irish Catholics, and the mockery was still running when Ma Kelly arrived (about 1870, with Michael as a boy) and in 1883 itself (Opper's Puck cover); the **Chinese Exclusion Act** (signed May 6, 1882 by President Chester Arthur) barred Chinese laborers for 10 years, the first U.S. law to shut out one nationality (extended 1892 by the Geary Act, made permanent 1902, repealed 1943); the **Immigration Act of August 1882** (a 50-cent head tax; barred convicts and anyone "likely to become a public charge"); the **American Protective Association** (founded 1887 in Clinton, Iowa, by Henry F. Bowers; anti-Catholic); cartoons in Puck, Judge and The Wasp. The irony beat: the Irish, mocked a generation earlier, now wary of the newest arrivals; Keppler's 1893 "Looking Backward" makes the same point | Library of Congress Chinese Exclusion Act guide; National Archives (Act text); Higham, *Strangers in the Land* (1955); Kinzer, *An Episode in Anti-Catholicism: The APA* (1964); Jensen 2002 and Fried 2016 for NINA |
| **Name changes** (required) | The story that officials "changed names at the station" is largely a myth. In the 1880s each ship's master filed a passenger list with U.S. Customs (required since the Steerage Act of 1819; National Archives, Customs Passenger Lists, Record Group 36); in Hamburg the emigrant lists were written by German-speaking clerks at the port of departure from the emigrant's own papers (Staatsarchiv Hamburg, Hamburg Passenger Lists 1850 to 1934). Station clerks worked *from* that list: they copied names, they did not invent them. Spellings did shift as names moved between alphabets (Hebrew or Cyrillic letters, then a German clerk's spelling, then English: e.g. Rabinovich, "Rabinowitsch", "Rabinowitz"), and most real changes came **later, made by immigrants themselves**: at school, at work, on shop signs, on naturalization papers (New York had no law requiring a court record of a change, so many left no paper trail). Draft scene `ep3_names` shows exactly this with an invented surname. Script wording: "The clerk copied the name from the ship's list. Most changes came later, made by families themselves." | Philip Sutton, "Why Your Family Name Was Not Changed at Ellis Island (and One That Was)," NYPL blog, July 2, 2013 (nypl.org/blog/2013/07/02/name-changes-ellis-island); "Ask Smithsonian: Did Ellis Island Officials Really Change the Names of Immigrants?" *Smithsonian Magazine* (smithsonianmag.com, article 180961544); JewishGen InfoFile "Ellis Island and the name change myth" (jewishgen.org/InfoFiles/ellismythnames.htm); Vincent J. Cannato, *American Passage: The History of Ellis Island* (2009); USCIS History Office (former senior historian Marian L. Smith has written on immigrant names and naturalization records; **verify the exact article title on uscis.gov before citing**); National Archives, Customs Passenger Lists (RG 36). Note: these sources discuss Ellis Island (1892 on); the same list-based procedure applied at Castle Garden in 1884, whose own registers were largely lost, so say "the station" or "the clerk" rather than claiming a Castle Garden record |

## The neighbours' accent colour

The Jewish neighbour family is drawn in **muted wheat gold, `#E2B864`** (detail tones `#7A5B22` / `#FFF0C9`; `E3.acc` in
`ep3.js`). It reads as a second warm "subject" colour against the teal sets, sits clear of coral (the Kellys) in hue, and does not
collide with teal (explains) or orange (handwriting, which is more saturated and only used for text). **Decision (Nov 9): keep wheat gold.**
