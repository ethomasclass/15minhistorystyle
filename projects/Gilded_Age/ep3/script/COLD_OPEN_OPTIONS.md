# Episode 3 cold open: options (Nov 12, 2026)

The stoop scene (Ma at the window: "More of them.") is quiet and only pays off as irony at the very end. These options open on
danger or a mystery and pay it off inside the episode. The stoop scene moves to beat 5, when the family reaches Brooklyn.

## Option A (recommended): "He sent me"

A con man meets the family at the dock. Real history: Castle Garden's fence and barriers were built partly to keep out
**runners**, touts for boarding houses who "cast their nets for the unwary stranger" (period description, RESEARCH 4.3).

> May, 1884. The Battery, at the bottom of Manhattan.
>
> A woman walks out of an old round stone fort with two children, a roped feather bed and a trunk. Everything they own.
>
> She has been travelling for three weeks. She doesn't speak a word of English. Her husband was supposed to meet them here.
>
> He isn't here.
>
> Then a man in a good coat walks up, smiling, and speaks to her in Yiddish.
>
> RUNNER: [warm, subtitled Yiddish] Welcome, welcome! Who are you waiting for?
>
> SORE: [subtitled Yiddish] My husband. Yankel Rabinowitz.
>
> RUNNER: [subtitled Yiddish] Yankel! Of course. He sent me. Come, I'll take the trunk.
>
> He never met Yankel in his life.
>
> New York had a name for men like him: runners. The state built a fence around this whole fort just to keep them out. They
> waited outside it anyway.
>
> And here's the strange part. The man reaching for that trunk was an immigrant too.
>
> About eight million people came through that fort. Every one of them had to figure out, fast, who to trust.
>
> So that's my question today. *Who helped newcomers make it in America, and who tried to keep them out?*

About 190 words. **Payoff in beat 4 (Castle Garden):** we cut back to the Battery. Rivke (12) notices the man is steering them
away from the ferry to Brooklyn, not toward it; Yankel pushes through the crowd, late from the shop, and the runner melts away.
The narrator then explains runners and the boarding-house racket, and sets up beat 6: the people who really helped were also
immigrants (landsmanshaft, HEAS, letters home). The danger and the help both came from people who had made the same trip. That
answers the driving question without stating the unit question.

To verify before voicing: that runners often worked their own countrymen in their own language, and the overcharging details
(boarding houses, fake tickets, luggage fees). If the sources are thin, keep it to what is already confirmed: touts, the fence,
"the unwary stranger."

Production: new clip `ep3_runner` (Battery, fort behind, the trunk, the hand reaching for it, a hard freeze before the payoff).
Subtitles stay up at least 2.5 s a line. `ep3_moving_in` stays, moved to beat 5.

## Option B: "Look behind them"

Open on Keppler's "Looking Backward" (Puck, 1893) cropped to the rich men turning away a poor immigrant, the shadows hidden.

> Five rich, well-dressed men are slamming the door on a poor immigrant with a bundle. Look at their faces. Now look behind
> them. Actually, don't. Not yet.

Payoff in beat 8: the full cartoon, with each man's shadow showing the poor immigrant he (or his father) used to be. Strong
mystery, but it opens on a cartoon from nine years after the episode is set, and it gives away the irony loop early.

## Option C: "Your name was never changed"

> Maybe someone in your family says their name got changed at Ellis Island. A clerk couldn't spell it, so he wrote down
> something easier. It's a great story. It almost never happened.

Payoff in beat 4 with the USCIS evidence and the Rabinovich to Rabin chain. Very clickable, but it centres Ellis Island
(1892) in an 1884 episode, and the payoff is a fact, not a story.

## Recommendation

**A.** It opens on a real threat to a family the viewer just met, holds a cliffhanger across beats 2 and 3, and the twist
("the man reaching for that trunk was an immigrant too") is the same irony the episode ends on with Ma Kelly. It also
gives a title: "The Con Men Waiting at the Dock | The Gilded Age, Part 3".
