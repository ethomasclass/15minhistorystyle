John D. Rockefeller grew up on farms in upstate New York. His father sold bottles of homemade medicine that he said could cure almost anything. Even cancer. [deadpan] It couldn't.

He'd disappear for months at a time. Years later, it came out that he had a second wife, under a fake name. [dry] Not exactly father of the year.

So John's mother ran the farm and the kids, mostly on her own. And she counted every penny.

As a boy, John raised turkeys and sold them. He lent 50 dollars of his savings to a neighbor, at 7 percent interest, and a year later he got it all back, plus a little extra. Money that made money. The same lesson Carnegie learned from that ten-dollar check.

At 16, he got his first job, as a bookkeeper, keeping track of a company's money. He also kept a little red notebook of his own: every penny he earned, spent and gave away, including what he gave his church. He celebrated the day he got that job every year, like a birthday. Most of us can't track our spending for a week.

At 19, he started his own business, with 1,000 dollars borrowed from his father. [wry] *His father charged him interest.*

Then oil was found in Pennsylvania, and Rockefeller got into refining it into kerosene: lamp oil, the way most families lit their homes at night.

He was obsessed with cutting costs. He liked to tell the story of watching a machine seal oil cans with 40 drops of solder, a kind of metal glue. He asked them to try 38. Some cans leaked. 39 worked. One drop, saved on every can, forever.

In 1872, he went after his rivals. Cleveland had 26 oil refineries. In about six weeks, he bought 22 of them. Some owners were happy to sell. Others said they felt they had no choice.

He had a secret weapon. He shipped so much oil that the railroads gave him a {rebate}: money paid back in secret, so his shipping cost less than everyone else's. Imagine the delivery company charging your competitor full price... and quietly sending *you* a refund.

That's {horizontal integration}: buying up your competitors, the companies that do the same job you do. Line them up side by side, and he owns the whole row. Back to pizza: it's buying every pizza place in town.

By about 1880, his company, Standard Oil, refined about 90 percent of America's oil. That's a {monopoly}: one company controlling almost all of something, so customers have nowhere else to go. [light] Yes, like the board game. Which started out as a warning.

To run it all, he and his partners set up a {trust}: dozens of companies handing control to one small board, which ran them like a single giant business.
