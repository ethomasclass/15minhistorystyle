John D. Rockefeller grew up on farms in upstate New York. His father sold bottles of homemade medicine that he said could cure almost anything. Even cancer. [deadpan] It couldn't.

He vanished for months at a time, and, it turned out, had a second wife under a fake name. [dry] Not exactly father of the year. So John's mother ran the farm, and counted every penny.

As a boy, John lent 50 dollars of his turkey money to a neighbor, and got it back with interest. Same lesson as Carnegie's ten-dollar check: money can make money.

At 16, he got a job as a bookkeeper, and wrote down every penny of his own in a little red notebook. At 19, he started his own business, with 1,000 dollars borrowed from his father. [wry] *His father charged him interest.*

Then came oil. Rockefeller refined it into kerosene: lamp oil, the way most families lit their homes at night.

He was obsessed with cutting costs. He liked to tell how he once got a machine to seal oil cans with 39 drops of solder, a metal glue, instead of 40. One drop, saved on every can, forever.

Then he went after his rivals. In 1872, Cleveland had 26 oil refineries. In about six weeks, he bought 22 of them. Some owners were glad to sell. Others said they felt they had no choice.

His secret weapon was a {rebate}. He shipped so much oil that the railroads secretly paid him money back, so his shipping cost less than everyone else's. Imagine the delivery company charging your rival full price... and quietly sending *you* a refund.

That's {horizontal integration}: buying up your competitors, the companies that do the same job you do. Line them up side by side, and he owns the whole row. Every pizza place in town.

By about 1880, his company, Standard Oil, refined about 90 percent of America's oil. That's a {monopoly}: one company controlling almost all of something. [light] Yes, like the board game. Which started out as a warning.

To run it, he set up a {trust}: dozens of companies handing control to one small board, run like one giant business.
