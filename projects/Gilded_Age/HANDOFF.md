# Gilded Age Video Series: Handoff

Nov 7, 2026 · \@Ellis Thomas

## Series overview

This is a six-episode video series on the Gilded Age (about 1870 to
1900), told through one fictional New York City family and aimed at 8th
to 11th graders. Each episode runs about 10 minutes or less. Episode 1
is fully scripted; episodes 2 to 6 are outlined below and need scripts.

-   **Format:** each episode cuts back and forth between the family\'s
    personal story and the big history that explains it. The family
    scenes make students care, and the history segments explain why it
    is happening to them.

-   **Setting:** New York City, chosen over Chicago because it is the
    strongest setting for immigration (Castle Garden, then Ellis
    Island), tenements (Jacob Riis), political machines (Tammany Hall),
    and high society (Fifth Avenue).

-   **Through-line question:** \"Who got the gold, and who only got the
    gilt?\" Gilded means covered in a thin layer of gold over cheaper
    metal, which is Mark Twain\'s point about the era. Each episode ends
    by asking it again.

-   **Family:** the Kellys, a fictional Irish family on the Lower East
    Side. Michael Kelly, 19 in 1883, works at the Havemeyers & Elder
    sugar refinery in Williamsburg, Brooklyn. So far only Michael and
    his mother appear on the page; the rest of the family is proposed
    below.

-   **Honesty rule:** the Kellys experience history, they do not cause
    it. At most one \"they were there\" moment per episode. Say on
    screen that they are a composite, and keep every event they live
    through documented.

## Rules for every script

-   **Audience:** 8th to 11th graders with no prior context. Assume
    nothing. Define every term, person, place, and institution in plain
    words the first time it appears, usually in the same sentence,
    without talking down.

-   **Length:** about 10 minutes or less, roughly 1,500 words of
    narration at 150 words per minute.

-   **Structure per episode:** cold open with the family (1 to 2 min),
    zoom out to history (4 to 5 min), back to the family, zoom out
    again, then a family closer that sets up the next episode. Family
    scenes and history segments should feel different on screen.

-   **Hooks:** include a few real, verifiable anecdotes per episode to
    grab attention, and flag in the fact-check notes any story that
    comes from one person\'s own retelling.

-   **Defining a term:** put a key terms table in the doc for each
    episode, and bold each term where it is first defined in the script.

-   **Style:** no em dashes in written content. Avoid unexplained
    jargon. Keep sentences short enough to narrate.

-   **Facts:** research current sources for each episode, keep a
    fact-check notes section for numbers where sources disagree, and
    list sources at the end.

## Episode 1 status

Episode 1, \"Steel, Oil, and Sugar,\" is fully scripted at about 1,520
words, or 10 minutes. The full script, a key terms table, fact-check
notes, and sources live in a separate doc: [Gilded Age Ep.
1](https://claude.ai/artifact/5nrnVBNp857NaUYJs2bs3m). Export it as
Markdown and keep it next to this handoff, since a Claude Code session
on your computer may not be able to open the link.

Its structure, which later episodes should copy:

1.  **Cold open (family):** Brooklyn Bridge opens, May 24, 1883; Michael
    and the Lower East Side tenement.

2.  **What was the Gilded Age (history):** Twain and Warner, tariffs,
    laissez-faire, the transcontinental railroad.

3.  **The inventions (history):** Bessemer steel, Bell, Edison\'s Pearl
    Street Station.

4.  **Back to the Kellys (family):** the bridge stampede, Barnum\'s
    elephants, the paycheck.

5.  **Carnegie and vertical integration (history):** defines
    entrepreneur, bobbin boy, telegraph boy, the \$480 million slip of
    paper.

6.  **Rockefeller and horizontal integration (history):** Job Day, the
    39 drops of solder, Cleveland Massacre, monopoly, trust. A bridge
    line ties him to Carnegie.

7.  **The Sugar Trust (family):** Michael\'s refinery joins the trust,
    1887.

8.  **Robber barons or captains of industry (history):** Sherman Act,
    E.C. Knight.

9.  **Closer (family):** the gold-or-gilt question and a hook into
    episode 2.

Fact-check flags worth carrying forward:

-   Sources split on the Cleveland Massacre count (22 vs 21 of 26
    refineries); the script uses 22.

-   Carnegie\'s \"name every business with his eyes shut\" comes from a
    1901 newspaper profile, so it is a period story, not a measured
    fact.

-   The solder story is Rockefeller\'s own retelling and cannot be
    independently checked.

-   Morgan\'s \"I accept this price\" is from an American Heritage
    account; wording varies between tellings.

## Visual and production plan

Visuals are still undecided; this is the direction discussed, not a
final call. The aim is real AI-generated video, made in Google Flow with
the Veo model.

-   **Art style:** a graphic-novel look inspired by the game Hades,
    shown head-on in symmetrical, stage-like frames. Thick ink outlines,
    big flat black shadows, saturated limited palettes, glowing rim
    light, and painterly texture. In prompts, describe the style in
    plain words instead of naming the game.

-   **Color per character:** each family member gets a signature
    rim-light color, for example green for the Tammany brother, furnace
    orange for the factory brother, and cool blue for the maid against
    gold mansion light. Wealth is lit in slightly sickly gold; tenements
    in gas-lamp amber and coal-smoke gray.

-   **Signature shot:** a tenement cross-section with the front wall cut
    away, like a dollhouse, which also works for zoom-in and zoom-out
    transitions to a block, the city, and a national map.

-   **Two visual layers:** warm illustrated or Riis-style family scenes,
    and clean maps, charts, and documents for history segments, so
    viewers always know which layer they are in.

-   **Flow limits:** clips are 4, 6, or 8 seconds. Write one story beat
    per prompt, build each character as a still portrait first and reuse
    it as a reference image, and use first-frame and last-frame control
    for zoom transitions. End every prompt with an \"avoid\" line
    (on-screen text, modern clothing, electric lights in pre-electric
    scenes, photorealism, 3D rendering).

-   **Check every clip** for anachronisms before it goes in front of
    students.

A sample prompt for episode 4\'s cold open, a ward heeler bringing coal
and a turkey to a tenement in winter, was drafted earlier and can be
rebuilt from this style description.

## Series inquiry question (C3 style, chosen Nov 8, 2026)

**Compelling question:** *Who should look out for ordinary people: the rich, the government, or ordinary people themselves?*

Each episode tests one or more of the three answers; students track evidence for each across the series, then argue in a summative
task. The series closer leaves it open; a short **Progressive Era mini unit** follows as the payoff (the government increasingly steps
in, pushed by ordinary people: muckrakers, settlement houses, trust-busting, the 1911 Standard Oil breakup, meat inspection, the
16th and 17th Amendments; many reforms trace back to the Populists of ep 6).

| Ep | Supporting question | Answer(s) it tests |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Industrialization and the tycoons | How did America get so rich, so fast, and who ended up with the money? | the rich (tycoons); the government stands back |
| 2 Wealth and culture | What did the richest Americans think they owed everyone else? | the rich (Gospel of Wealth, philanthropy) vs Social Darwinism |
| 3 Immigration | Was America a land of opportunity for newcomers? | ordinary people (immigrant networks, family, mutual aid) |
| 4 Cities and machines | Did political machines help the poor or exploit them? | the government, the machine version |
| 5 Labor | How did workers fight for a bigger share, and what did it cost them? | ordinary people (unions) |
| 6 Populism | Could ordinary Americans use politics to fight back? | ordinary people through the government |

**The videos never name the compelling question.** It belongs to the classroom (handout, discussion, summative task). Each
script builds evidence for it implicitly: show who helped ordinary people, who didn't, and what it cost, and let students draw the
line. A script may pose its own episode-level question, but never quotes the unit question or the three-way framing. The
classroom handout carries a three-column evidence chart (the rich / the government / ordinary people).
