So how did people like the Vanderbilts feel about all this? A lot of them felt... fine. And they had an idea to explain it.

It came from a British writer named Herbert Spencer. After reading Darwin, Spencer coined a phrase you've probably heard: {survival of the fittest}. In nature, the plants and animals best suited to their world survive and have more babies.

Spencer and his fans said society works the same way. The strongest rise to the top. The weak fall. And helping the poor just gets in the way of nature.

Historians later gave the idea a name: {Social Darwinism}. Though it's worth saying, "fit" in nature doesn't mean "best." It means suited. A cockroach is very fit.

In 1882, Spencer toured America, and the rich treated him like a rock star. They threw him a huge dinner at New York's fanciest restaurant. And in his speech, Spencer told them they worked too hard. Americans, he said, needed to relax more.

Rich men cheered a speech about working less. Their workers were not invited.

An American professor, William Graham Sumner, went further. In 1883, the same year as the ball, he wrote a book called *What Social Classes Owe to Each Other*.

His answer was: nothing.

One line from it: "A drunkard in the gutter is just where he ought to be."

Sumner explained reformers with letters. A and B, he wrote, decide what C shall do for D. D is the poor person being helped. And C? C is the ordinary working man who pays for it. Sumner called him "the Forgotten Man."
