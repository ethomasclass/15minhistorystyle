Not every rich man agreed with Sumner. Remember Andrew Carnegie, from last time? The one who promised himself he'd quit at thirty-five?

In 1889 he was fifty-three. Still not quitting. But he did write an essay. It was called "Wealth," and people nicknamed it {the Gospel of Wealth}.

Carnegie said a rich man shouldn't leave his fortune to his kids, and shouldn't waste it on parties. He should give it away, while he's alive, to things that help people help themselves. Like libraries.

And then the line that made him famous: "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced."

He meant it. He paid to build about seventeen hundred libraries in the United States, and about twenty-five hundred around the world. Many are still open. Check the cornerstone on yours.

That's called {philanthropy}.

But Carnegie also said the rich should act as {trustees} for the poor, deciding what's good for them, because they'd do it "better than they would or could do for themselves."

And at the same time, the men in his steel mills worked twelve-hour days, for low pay. Some of them asked: why not pay us more, and let us buy our own books?

Hold on to that question. It's about to get very loud.
