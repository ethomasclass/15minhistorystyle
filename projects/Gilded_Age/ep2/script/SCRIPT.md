## 1 | The Ball (cold open)

On a Monday night in March, 1883, more than a thousand people came to a party on Fifth Avenue. Nobody was allowed in wearing ordinary clothes.

They came as kings. As queens. As cats, and birds, and stars. One woman came dressed as electricity, holding up a torch that actually lit up, powered by batteries hidden in her dress.

Outside, a crowd packed the sidewalk just to watch them climb out of their carriages. The police had to push people back.

And downstairs, in the kitchen, a seventeen-year-old girl was carrying ice cream up from the cellar.

**NORA:** [whisper] Don't drop it. Don't drop it. Don't drop it.

That's Nora Kelly. Michael's little sister, from last time. She's made up too. But thousands of girls had her job, in houses just like this one.

The party reportedly cost about a quarter of a million dollars. Nora earned less than four dollars a week.

So here's what I want to know. *What did the richest people in America think they owed everyone else?*

## 2 | New Money

The house belonged to William and Alva Vanderbilt. It was brand new: a French castle, in limestone, in the middle of Manhattan.

The Vanderbilts had money. Alva's husband was the grandson of a man who made a fortune in ships and railroads and left about a hundred million dollars when he died. That's more than almost anyone on Earth.

But in New York, money wasn't enough.

At the top sat a small circle of families who'd been rich for generations: {old money}. They decided who was "in." And they looked down on people like the Vanderbilts: {new money}, people who got rich in their own lifetime. Too fast. Too loud. Railroad people.

The queen of old money was Mrs. Caroline Astor. If she came to your house, you were in. If she didn't, you weren't.

Her advisor later said there were only about four hundred people in New York who really belonged in society. The rest made people uncomfortable at parties. That's where the nickname came from: {the 400}.

There's a story, and it's probably only a story, that Alva refused to invite Mrs. Astor's daughter to the ball, because Mrs. Astor had never visited her. So Mrs. Astor gave in. She drove over and left her {calling card}: a little printed card that meant "I accept you."

True or not, Mrs. Astor came to the ball.

So the party had a job. It wasn't just a party. It was an announcement: the Vanderbilts have arrived.

## 3 | The Party

And they arrived on horseback. Sort of.

The guests had practiced dances for weeks, in groups called {quadrilles}. One group strapped fake horses around their waists and pranced down the grand staircase.

These were the most powerful people in America.

The woman with the torch was Alva's sister-in-law, Alice Vanderbilt, dressed as "The Electric Light." In white satin and diamonds. Electric lights had only just started lighting homes in New York. She wore the newest technology in the world, as a dress.

Supper came at two in the morning. The last dance ended at sunrise, and the guests went home as the lamplighters put out the street lamps.

The newspapers printed every costume, every flower, every name. Which was the point.

The bill, as people reported it: about two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. In today's money, roughly eight million. A reported sixty-five thousand of it was champagne.

For one night.

## 4 | The Basement

Now back downstairs.

That night the Vanderbilts pulled cooks and servants from all their family's houses into one kitchen. The richest family in America threw the party of the century, and the supper came from the servants.

Nora was a {domestic servant}. And she was {live-in}: she slept in the house, in a small room near the roof. That meant she was on call almost all the time.

Most days she worked more than ten hours. Some days fourteen. She got one afternoon off a week, and part of Sunday.

Her pay was about three dollars and sixty cents a week, plus her bed and her meals.

So let's do the math.

A quarter of a million dollars, divided by what Nora earned in a year... comes to more than thirteen hundred years.

Nora would have had to start working before the Vikings reached North America to pay for one party.

On her afternoon off, Nora took the ferry home, to the same kind of room you saw last time.

**MA:** [dry] And how were the rich?

**NORA:** [tired] Tired. Like the rest of us. Only they slept till noon.

She put most of her wages on the table. Her mother and her little brother were sewing shirts by the lamp, paid by the piece. That money was how the family made rent.

## 5 | Survival of the Fittest

So how did people like the Vanderbilts feel about all this? A lot of them felt... fine. And they had an idea to explain it.

It came from a British writer named Herbert Spencer. After reading Darwin, Spencer coined a phrase you've probably heard: {survival of the fittest}. In nature, the plants and animals best suited to their world survive and have more babies.

Spencer and his fans said society works the same way. The strongest rise to the top. The weak fall. And helping the poor just gets in the way of nature.

Historians later gave the idea a name: {Social Darwinism}. Though it's worth saying, "fit" in nature doesn't mean "best." It means suited. A cockroach is very fit.

In 1882, Spencer toured America, and the rich treated him like a rock star. They threw him a huge dinner at New York's fanciest restaurant. And in his speech, Spencer told them they worked too hard. Americans, he said, needed to relax more.

Rich men cheered a speech about relaxing, at a dinner that cost more than their workers made in a year.

An American professor, William Graham Sumner, went further. In 1883, the same year as the ball, he wrote a book called *What Social Classes Owe to Each Other*.

His answer was: nothing.

One line from it: "A drunkard in the gutter is just where he ought to be."

## 6 | Sunday

Spencer said Americans should relax. Michael and Nora were working on it.

On a Sunday, the one day both had free, they went to a ball game in Brooklyn.

Baseball had a league for the working class: the American Association. Cheaper seats. Games on Sundays, in some cities. And beer, which the other league banned. Its rivals called it "the Beer and Whiskey League." They meant it as an insult. The fans took it as a compliment.

**MICHAEL:** [amazed] Did you see that? Clean over the fence!

**NORA:** I saw it. I also saw you spill your beer on that man's hat.

The rich had other ideas about Sunday. Polo, brought over from England. Tennis at the beach houses in Newport. Racing yachts the size of houses.

Same day off. Very different Sunday.

## 7 | The Gospel of Wealth

Not every rich man agreed with Sumner. Remember Andrew Carnegie, from last time? The one who promised himself he'd quit at thirty-five?

In 1889 he was fifty-three. Still not quitting. But he did write an essay. It was called "Wealth," and people nicknamed it {the Gospel of Wealth}.

Carnegie said a rich man shouldn't leave his fortune to his kids, and shouldn't waste it on parties. He should give it away, while he's alive, to things that help people help themselves. Like libraries.

And then the line that made him famous: "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced."

He meant it. He paid to build about seventeen hundred libraries in the United States, and about twenty-five hundred around the world. Many are still open. Check the cornerstone on yours.

That's called {philanthropy}.

But Carnegie also said the rich should act as {trustees} for the poor, deciding what's good for them, because they'd do it "better than they would or could do for themselves."

And at the same time, the men in his steel mills worked twelve-hour days, for low pay. Some of them asked: why not pay us more, and let us buy our own books?

Hold on to that question. It's about to get very loud.

## 8 | Rags to Riches

There's one more story the Gilded Age told itself. Maybe the most powerful one.

Popular novels told it over and over: a poor boy works hard, stays honest, and rises to the top. {Rags to riches}. Carnegie seemed to prove it.

But years later, historians looked up where hundreds of real business leaders of the time came from. Most had fathers who were businessmen, or lawyers, or well-off farmers. Carnegie wasn't the rule. He was the exception that made the story famous.

So, what did the richest people in America think they owed everyone else?

Some said nothing at all. The fittest win; that's nature.

Some, like Carnegie, said they owed a great deal, but they'd decide what, and how, and when.

And while they argued about it, Nora went back to Fifth Avenue on the evening ferry, and her week's wages stayed on the kitchen table.

Next time: new neighbors move into the Kellys' building. They've come a very long way.

Thanks for watching. Subscribe for more, and I'll see you next time.
