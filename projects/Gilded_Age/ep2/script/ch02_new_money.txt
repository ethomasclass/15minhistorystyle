The house belonged to William and Alva Vanderbilt. It was brand new: a French castle, in limestone, in the middle of Manhattan.

The Vanderbilts had money. Alva's husband was the grandson of a man who made a fortune in ships and railroads and left about a hundred million dollars when he died. That's more than almost anyone on Earth.

But in New York, money wasn't enough.

At the top sat a small circle of families who'd been rich for generations: {old money}. They decided who was "in." And they looked down on people like the Vanderbilts: {new money}, people who got rich in their own lifetime. Too fast. Too loud. Railroad people.

The queen of old money was Mrs. Caroline Astor. If she came to your house, you were in. If she didn't, you weren't.

Her advisor later said there were only about four hundred people in New York who really belonged in society. The rest made people uncomfortable at parties. That's where the nickname came from: {the 400}.

There's a story, and it's probably only a story, that Alva refused to invite Mrs. Astor's daughter to the ball, because Mrs. Astor had never visited her. So Mrs. Astor gave in. She drove over and left her {calling card}: a little printed card that meant "I accept you."

True or not, Mrs. Astor came to the ball.

So the party had a job. It wasn't just a party. It was an announcement: the Vanderbilts have arrived.
