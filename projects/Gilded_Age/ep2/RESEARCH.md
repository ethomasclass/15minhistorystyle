# Gilded Age Episode 2: research

**Working title:** *The Ball and the Basement* (or *What the Rich Owe*)
**Status:** research pass 1 and a proposed outline, Nov 8, 2026. Not a script yet.
**Length:** about 1,700 words of narration (10 to 12 minutes).
**Covers:** the Vanderbilt ball of 1883; old money vs new money (Mrs. Astor, Ward McAllister, "the 400"); Social Darwinism
(Spencer, Sumner); a live-in maid's life; Sunday leisure for workers vs the rich; Carnegie's "Wealth" and his libraries; the
Horatio Alger myth vs the evidence.
**Family thread:** Michael Kelly's sister (composite, Irish, Brooklyn) is a live-in maid in a Fifth Avenue house. Her "they were
there" moment is the ball, seen from below stairs. Name and age still to be decided.

Confidence key used below: **[primary]** = checked against an 1880s/1890s document in this pass; **[museum]** = a museum or archive
page; **[secondary]** = reputable secondary source; **[unverified]** = from the brief or memory, not yet checked; do not narrate
until checked.

---

## 1. What a student should be able to explain after watching

1. In the 1880s the newest fortunes (the Vanderbilts) fought for acceptance from older rich families (the Astors), and spending
   was a weapon in that fight.
2. **Social Darwinism** was the idea that society works like nature: the "fittest" rise, the poor fail, and helping them only
   gets in the way. It flattered the rich. (The label itself came later.)
3. Carnegie argued the opposite duty: a rich man should give his fortune away while alive. He then gave most of his away, mainly
   to libraries, while paying his workers low wages.
4. A maid in a mansion earned about three or four dollars a week plus room and board, for days of ten to twelve hours or more.
5. The "rags to riches" story was mostly a story. Studies of real business leaders show most came from comfortable families.

## 3. Candidate driving questions (pick one by 0:40)

1. *A single party cost more than a maid could earn in a thousand years. How did Americans convince themselves that was fair?*
2. *What did the richest people in America think they owed everyone else?*
3. *In the Gilded Age, could a poor kid really get rich, or was that just a story?*
4. *Why would a family with a hundred million dollars still need to beg for an invitation?*
5. *Who was the party for: the guests, or the newspapers?*

Recommendation: #2. It ties Spencer/Sumner ("nothing") against Carnegie ("give it all away"), and the closer can land on what
they owed the sister.

## 4. Verified facts, by section

### 4.1 The Vanderbilt ball, March 26, 1883

- **Date, place, host.** Monday night, March 26, 1883, at the new house of William K. and Alva Vanderbilt, northwest corner of
  Fifth Avenue and 52nd Street (660 Fifth Avenue); it doubled as her housewarming. [museum: MCNY] The Sun of March 27, 1883
  places the house "on the northwest corner of Fifth avenue and Fifty-second street." [primary]
- **Primary coverage found:** *The Sun* (New York), Tuesday, March 27, 1883, page 1, headline "MARVELS OF FANCY DRESS"
  (Chronicling America, LOC). OCR is rough; quotes below are cleaned from OCR and should be checked against the page image
  before going on screen.
  - Arrival: carriages began "about 10 1/2 o'clock"; "a crowd had already assembled in the street"; "the police had hard work
    to make room for them." Footmen "in the Vanderbilt ... livery." [primary]
  - Decorations: "Wagon load after wagon load of palms and plants drawn from conservatories all over the city." [primary]
  - Costumes: "Many were ordered from Worth" (the Paris designer) and "foreign picture galleries were searched for models." "Ordinary
    evening dress was absolutely prohibited." [primary]
  - Quadrilles: the dancers assembled in the third-floor gymnasium; "the six quadrilles, comprising in all nearly a hundred ladies
    and gentlemen," moved "in a glittering processional pageant down the grand stairway." The Hobby Horse Quadrille "first took
    the floor"; the horses were "light enough to be easily and comfortably attached to the waists of the dancers." [primary]
  - **Alice Vanderbilt:** "Mrs. Cornelius Vanderbilt appeared as the Electric Light, in white satin trimmed with diamonds and
    with a magnificent diamond headdress." [primary] MCNY adds: designed by Worth, with a torch lit by batteries hidden in the
    dress; MCNY holds the dress. [museum]
  - **Supper (surprise):** "The supper was furnished by the Vanderbilt cooks, the employees of the entire family having been
    turned into the kitchen of William K.'s house. Delmonico supplied the ices and fancy cakes and provided most of the waiters.
    The family servants were in livery with knee breeches and buckled shoes." [primary] This is a gift for the family thread:
    servants from *all* the Vanderbilt houses were pooled in one kitchen, so the sister can plausibly be "borrowed" for the night.
    Supper was served in the second-floor drawing rooms. [primary]
  - Mrs. Astor attended and is listed in the Sun's costume descriptions; "Miss Astor" (Carrie) appears in a quadrille of
    ladies "arrayed as ... stars." [primary, OCR rough]
- **Guests:** about 1,200 [museum: MCNY; secondary: Mental Floss]; Wikipedia's Petit Chateau page says "about a thousand." [secondary]
- **Cost:** "Most contemporary sources put the cost at $250,000 ... That included $65,000 for champagne and $11,000 for flowers."
  [museum: MCNY blog, 2013]. I did not find these numbers in the Sun's front page; their 1883 origin is not yet traced. See flags.
- **Costumes ~$155,000:** not found in any source this pass. Do not use.
- **Supper time and dawn:** supper at about 2 a.m.; Alva led a final Virginia reel at sunrise. [museum: MCNY]
- **Photographer:** José María Mora (Cuban-born, opened a New York studio in 1870) photographed guests in costume; the
  New-York Historical Society and Newport Historical Society hold the album prints. [archive: N-YHS finding aid; Newport HS]
- **Press:** Alva let reporters see the house beforehand (Mental Floss). The Sun also ran criticism of the excess (MCNY says so;
  not yet located in the Sun text). [museum]
- **Modern dollars:** see section 4.4 for the honest comparison (maid-years). Consumer-price conversions of $250,000 in 1883
  published online run from "roughly $6 million" (MCNY, 2013) to "$7.3 million" (Mental Floss, ~2022). Method: multiply by the
  CPI ratio (1883 prices were roughly 1/30th of today's by most CPI back-series), giving about $7 to 8 million in 2026 dollars.
  Check the exact multiplier at MeasuringWorth before narrating. Note on screen: measured against wages, not prices, it is far
  larger.

### 4.2 Old money vs new money

- Commodore Cornelius Vanderbilt died in 1877 leaving about $105 million. [secondary: Mental Floss; commonly cited]
- **Ward McAllister** is credited with "the Four Hundred," saying only about 400 people were truly at home in fashionable
  society. His 1888 line in the *New-York Tribune*: "If you go outside that number, you strike people who are either not at ease
  in a ballroom or else make other people not at ease." [secondary: Wikipedia citing the Tribune]
- **The number's origin** is uncertain; the popular story ties it to the capacity of Mrs. Astor's ballroom at 350 Fifth Avenue.
  In 1892 McAllister himself gave a different explanation (several dances of about 150 guests, about 400 different people over a
  season). [secondary]
- **The list was published Feb. 16, 1892, in the *New York Times*,** nine years after the ball. So in 1883 "the 400" was not yet a
  printed list. [secondary: Wikipedia]
- **Calling-card story:** Alva would not invite Carrie Astor because Mrs. Astor had never called on her; Mrs. Astor then left her
  card, and the invitation came. MCNY itself calls the anecdote "apocryphal." Treat as legend. [museum]

### 4.3 Social Darwinism

- **"Survival of the fittest"** was coined by Herbert Spencer in *Principles of Biology* (1864), after reading Darwin; Darwin
  adopted the phrase in the 5th edition of *Origin* (1869). [unverified this pass; standard, check Britannica]
- **Spencer's US tour, 1882,** ended with a farewell banquet at Delmonico's on **Nov. 9, 1882**, about 200 guests. [secondary: NY
  Sun (2008 review), Wikipedia "Twilight Club"; full report published by Appleton, 1883, *Herbert Spencer on the Americans and the
  Americans on Herbert Spencer*]. The irony: Spencer told the hard-driving Americans, "We have had somewhat too much of the 'gospel
  of work.' It is time to preach the gospel of relaxation." (Wording varies slightly; check Appleton text.) Carnegie was a devoted
  Spencer fan. Book: Barry Werth, *Banquet at Delmonico's* (2009).
- **William Graham Sumner,** Yale professor, *What Social Classes Owe to Each Other* (1883). Verified lines from the Gutenberg text:
  - "A drunkard in the gutter is just where he ought to be, according to the fitness and tendency of things." [primary]
  - On reformers: "Their schemes, therefore, may always be reduced to this type, that A and B decide what C shall do for D." C is
    his "Forgotten Man," the ordinary taxpayer who pays. [primary] (Quote without the dash on screen, or as "A and B decide what C
    shall do for D.")
  - Fairness note: Sumner also opposed tariffs and special favors to the rich, so "he simply defended the rich" is too simple.
- **The label "Social Darwinism"** was rare in the 1880s and was popularized as a critic's term by Richard Hofstadter, *Social
  Darwinism in American Thought* (1944). [unverified this pass; standard] Neither Spencer nor Sumner called himself a "Social
  Darwinist." Say: "historians later called this Social Darwinism."

### 4.4 The maid's life (best primary source: Lucy Maynard Salmon, *Domestic Service*, 1897)

Salmon, a Vassar historian, mailed 5,000 sets of questionnaires in **1889 and 1890** and got 1,025 back; so her data describe
the right decade. [primary]

- **Wages (women, money only, plus room and board):** U.S. average **$3.23 a week** = **$167.96 a year**; "Eastern section"
  (includes New York) **$3.60 a week** (about $15.60 a month). By job: general servants $2.88 to $3.16, chambermaids $3.31 to
  $3.86, parlor maids $3.94, cooks $3.64 to $4.45 a week; male butlers about $6.11, coachmen $7.84. Salmon values room and board at
  about $5 a week more. [primary, Tables VII, VIII, XIV]
- **Hours:** 38% of women servants worked 10 hours a day, 37% more than 10, 25% fewer; a servant "may draw a prize of seven working
  hours or she may draw a blank of fourteen." [primary]
- **Days off:** Salmon says it is "the common belief that the custom is universal of granting Thursday afternoon and Sunday
  evening," and then shows the custom varied (123 combinations), though almost every employer gave some fixed time off. Safe:
  "usually one afternoon a week, often Thursday, and part of Sunday." [primary]
- **Irish women:** 1890 census: 168,993 domestic servants born in Ireland, 95,007 born in Germany (U.S. total). [primary: Salmon
  note 176] In New York City, Irish women were about 74% of domestic servants in 1855 [secondary, 1850s, so do not use for 1883];
  by 1900 Irish-born women were "just under half" of servants in New York and Philadelphia. [secondary, Hasia Diner via HSP] Safe
  for 1883: "Most maids in New York were Irish immigrants or their daughters" is defensible; avoid a percentage.
- **Room:** attic or top-floor rooms for live-in servants in Fifth Avenue houses. [unverified; standard; find a floor plan of 660
  Fifth Ave in Kathrens, *Great Houses of New York*]
- **Staff size** of a Vanderbilt-scale house: not verified this pass. Avoid a number, or say "dozens of servants" only after a
  source. The Sun says employees "of the entire family" were pooled for the ball.
- **The math (show on screen):** $250,000 / $187.20 a year (Eastern average, $3.60 x 52) = **about 1,335 years**. Using the national
  $167.96 = about 1,488 years. Safe wording: **"more than 1,300 years of her wages."** The champagne alone ($65,000, if accurate) =
  about 347 years; the flowers ($11,000) = about 59 years. Flags: the $250,000 is a reported estimate, not an invoice; Salmon's
  data are 1889 to 1890, six years after the ball; room and board are not counted.
- **Michael's wage:** not verified this pass. In the 1886 Williamsburg sugar strike the workers reportedly demanded a 10-hour day
  (down from 12) and $1.75 a day (Greenpointers, secondary), which implies they earned less. Safe: "less than two dollars a day,
  for twelve-hour shifts." Check the *Brooklyn Daily Eagle*, April 1886, and the Commissioner of Labor reports.

### 4.5 Sunday leisure

- **American Association (1882):** cheaper tickets, Sunday games, and beer, in contrast to the National League, which banned
  alcohol on its grounds; several AA clubs were backed by brewers; NL people mocked it as the "Beer and Whiskey League." [secondary:
  Wikipedia, SABR] The exact **25 cents vs 50 cents** is widely repeated but not confirmed in this pass (check SABR, David Nemec,
  *The Beer and Whisky League*). Sunday games were not allowed in every AA city by law, so say "in some cities, on Sundays."
- **Brooklyn:** joined the AA in **1884** (names over the years: Atlantics, Grays, Bridegrooms), moved to the National League in
  1890; this franchise is today's Los Angeles Dodgers. [secondary: Wikipedia] So in March 1883 Brooklyn had no AA team yet; the
  Sunday scene must be set in 1884 or later, or the siblings take the ferry to see the New York Metropolitans (AA from 1883).
  [Metropolitans: unverified this pass]
- **Rich leisure** (all [unverified this pass], standard facts): James Gordon Bennett Jr. brought polo to the U.S. in 1876; the
  first U.S. national lawn tennis championships were held at the Newport Casino in 1881; yachting (the New York Yacht Club,
  America's Cup). Check each in Britannica or the International Tennis Hall of Fame before narrating.

### 4.6 Carnegie, "Wealth" (1889)

- Published as **"Wealth," *North American Review*, vol. 148 (June 1889)**; British reprints called it "The Gospel of Wealth."
  [primary: Fordham Modern History Sourcebook citation]
- Opening line: "The problem of our age is the administration of wealth, so that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together
  the rich and poor in harmonious relationship." [primary]
- The rich man as "the sole agent and trustee for his poorer brethren ... doing for them better than they would or could do for
  themselves." [primary] (Good line to show the condescension critics saw.)
- **"The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced."** [Carnegie Corporation of New York page quoting the essay]
- **Libraries:** 2,509 libraries funded 1883 to 1929, 1,689 in the U.S. (some sources: 1,681 or 1,679); about $56 million for
  construction. First U.S. Carnegie library: **Braddock, Pennsylvania, dedicated March 30, 1889** (an Allegheny library was
  commissioned earlier, 1886, but opened later). The first of all was in his hometown, Dunfermline, Scotland (gift 1881, opened
  1883). [secondary: Wikipedia; Dunfermline dates unverified]
- **How much he gave:** commonly "about $350 million," about 90% of his fortune. Not confirmed this pass; check Carnegie
  Corporation or Nasaw's *Andrew Carnegie* (2006). Safe: "most of his fortune."
- **1901 sale to J.P. Morgan:** $480 million (covered in Ep 1 flags; price solid).
- **Link to Ep 1:** his 1868 memo to himself to quit at 35. In 1889 he was 53 and still working.
- **Critics:** workers asked why not pay higher wages instead of giving libraries; a union man reportedly said workers had no time
  to read after 12-hour shifts. [unverified; find a sourced version or skip]
- **Homestead setup line (Ep 5):** "Three years after he wrote that a rich man should be a trustee for the poor, his company cut
  wages at Homestead, and the workers struck." (Homestead, 1892; Carnegie in Scotland.)

### 4.7 Horatio Alger vs the evidence

- Alger's novels (e.g., *Ragged Dick*, 1868) sold the "rags to respectability" story; his heroes rise by luck and a rich patron as
  much as by work. [unverified this pass; standard; Ragged Dick date check]
- **Studies:** Frances W. Gregory and Irene D. Neu, "The American Industrial Elite in the 1870's: Their Social Origins," in William
  Miller, ed., *Men in Business* (Harvard, 1952), pp. 193 to 211 (confirmed citation). Commonly reported finding: the large
  majority of about 300 top industrialists of the 1870s came from middle- or upper-class, native-born, Protestant families, and only
  a small share (often given as under 10%) had fathers who were workers. **Exact percentages not confirmed this pass; get the
  article before narrating a number.** William Miller's earlier study (1949, of leaders around 1900 to 1910) is usually summarized
  the same way. Later: John Ingham's study of 696 iron and steel manufacturers found continuity of class, not rags to riches.
  [secondary]
- Safe wording: "When historians later looked up where hundreds of real business leaders came from, most had fathers who were
  businessmen, professionals or prosperous farmers. Carnegie was the exception that made the rule famous."

### 4.8 Hand-off to Episode 3

Plausible hooks (pick one): the sister, off on her Thursday afternoon, finds new neighbors moving into the Kellys' building: a
Jewish family from the Russian Empire carrying a feather bed and a samovar; or Michael reads that Castle Garden is processing
record numbers, and his mother, once an immigrant herself, mutters about "newcomers." The 1881 to 1882 pogroms in the Russian
Empire set off the big Jewish migration, so an 1883 to 1884 arrival is plausible. [unverified this pass; standard]

## 5. Key terms (bold where first defined in the script)

| Term | Plain definition |
|---|---|
| **Old money** | Families rich for generations, who looked down on fortunes made recently. |
| **New money** (*nouveau riche*, NOO-voh REESH) | People who got rich in their own lifetime. |
| **High society** | The small circle of rich families who invited each other to parties and decided who was "in." |
| **The 400** | The nickname for New York's most exclusive society, about 400 people. |
| **Calling card** | A small printed card a visitor left at a house; leaving one meant "I accept you socially." |
| **Quadrille** (kwah-DRIL) | A dance for four couples in a square, with set steps. |
| **Conspicuous consumption** | Spending to show off (Thorstein Veblen's phrase, 1899; use only as "a later writer called it"). |
| **Social Darwinism** | The idea, named later by critics, that society works like nature: the strong rise, the weak fall, and helping the poor is a mistake. |
| **Survival of the fittest** | Spencer's phrase: the best-suited survive. In nature "fit" means suited to the environment, not "best." |
| **Philanthropy** | Giving large amounts of money to help the public, like building libraries. |
| **Trustee** | Someone trusted to manage money for others. Carnegie said the rich should be trustees for the poor. |
| **Domestic servant** | A person paid to cook, clean or care for a household, often living in the house. |
| **Live-in** | Sleeping in the employer's house, so you are on call almost all the time. |
| **Rags to riches** | The story of a poor person becoming rich through hard work. |

## 8. Humor and modern connections (evergreen)

- The hobby-horse quadrille: grown adults with fake horses strapped to their waists, prancing down a staircase. Dry line: "These
  were the most powerful people in America."
- Alice dressed as electricity, four months after Edison's Pearl Street station (Ep 1) lit its first customers: the newest
  technology as a fashion accessory. Modern link: showing off the newest gadget.
- The guest list as the original "who got invited" anxiety; Alva invited reporters to tour the house first, the 1883 version of
  posting the party before it happens. Keep it generic, no platform names.
- Spencer flew in (sailed in) to tell workaholic tycoons to relax more. They threw him a giant dinner about it.
- Sumner's A, B, C and D: let students guess who D and C are before the reveal.
- The cooks: the richest family in America threw the party of the century and the food came from the servants.
- Library card as the payoff: many viewers' town libraries may be Carnegie buildings ("check the cornerstone").
