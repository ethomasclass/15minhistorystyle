## 1. Labor: why workers organized, 1877 to 1894

### 1.1 Why workers organized [secondary]
- 10 to 12 hour days, six days a week, common in industry; steel ran 12-hour shifts, with a 24-hour "long turn" when crews switched.
- No workers' compensation, no unemployment pay, no pension. Wage cuts came with every downturn (1873 to 1879, 1884 to 1886, 1893 to
  1897). Owners could replace strikers with new immigrants (ties to Ep 3).
- Source: Library of Congress classroom materials; Britannica "labor movement"; Foner, *History of the Labor Movement*, vol. 2.

### 1.2 The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 [secondary]
- Began July 16, 1877 at Martinsburg, West Virginia, when the Baltimore and Ohio cut wages 10 percent (another cut after earlier ones).
- Spread along the lines to Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Chicago, St. Louis. In Pittsburgh (July 21 to 22) militia fired on a crowd, about
  20 killed; crowds burned the Pennsylvania Railroad's roundhouse and more than 100 locomotives and over 1,000 cars [secondary,
  figures vary].
- President Hayes sent federal troops: the first time federal troops broke a nationwide strike. Roughly 100 people died overall.
- Hook: "the first nationwide strike," and it ended with the U.S. Army. Images already gathered in `ep5/public/img/b_1877_strike/`.

### 1.3 Knights of Labor and the eight-hour movement [secondary]
- Founded 1869, Philadelphia (Uriah Stephens), secret at first (members used symbols; the name was written as five stars in public).
  Terence Powderly led from 1879. Open to skilled and unskilled, women, and Black workers; excluded Chinese workers (and "parasites":
  bankers, lawyers, liquor sellers, gamblers).
- 1885: Knights' railroad strike beat Jay Gould's Wabash line; membership jumped to roughly 700,000 by mid 1886 (often given as about
  730,000) [secondary]. Then collapsed after Haymarket and failed strikes, to about 100,000 by 1890.
- May 1, 1886: some 300,000 to 350,000 workers nationwide struck or marched for an eight-hour day [secondary]. Slogan: "Eight hours
  for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will."
- Secret meetings: fits Michael's teaser ("secret meetings, about a union"). The Knights dropped full secrecy in 1881, but local
  assemblies still met quietly because employers fired union men [secondary]. Script: "meetings the foreman wasn't supposed to know
  about." VERIFIED in spirit.

### 1.4 Haymarket, Chicago, May 4, 1886 [secondary]
- May 3: police killed at least two strikers at the McCormick Harvesting Machine works. May 4: protest meeting in Haymarket Square;
  rain, a small crowd; police moved to disperse it; someone threw a bomb. One officer died at once, six more later (seven police in
  all; some likely hit by other police in the gunfire); at least four civilians killed. The bomber was never identified.
- Eight anarchists convicted with little evidence tying them to the bomb; four hanged November 11, 1887; one (Lingg) killed himself
  in his cell. Gov. John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three survivors June 26, 1893, and was attacked for it.
- Effect: a panic about "anarchists" and immigrants; the Knights, though not involved, were blamed and declined.
- Callback for the finale: the same Altgeld later protests Cleveland's troops at Pullman (1894). Good thread, one line.
- **Sensitive.** Source: Chicago History Museum / Encyclopedia of Chicago "Haymarket"; Library of Congress "Haymarket Riot."

### 1.5 AFL and Gompers (compress to about 20 seconds) [secondary]
- American Federation of Labor founded December 1886 (Columbus, Ohio). Samuel Gompers, a cigar maker born in London (1850) to a Dutch
  Jewish family, led it almost continuously to 1924.
- Skilled trades only, each craft its own union; "pure and simple" goals: wages, hours, conditions. Asked what labor wanted, Gompers is
  known for "More." The longer 1893 line: "We want more schoolhouses and less jails; more books and less arsenals; more learning and
  less vice; more leisure and less greed..." [secondary; the one-word "More" is a later compression: say "his answer boiled down to
  one word: more"].
- Survives because it is narrow; leaves the unskilled (Michael) out. Good contrast: the Knights wanted to change society; the AFL wanted
  a bigger paycheck.

### 1.6 Brooklyn sugar refinery strike, 1886, and the Sugar Trust, 1887 (Michael's real backdrop)
- Spring 1886: workers at Havemeyers & Elder and other Williamsburg refineries struck for a ten-hour day (from twelve) and about $1.75
  a day, plus union recognition; one account says about 3,000 men out, with longshoremen and coopers [secondary: Greenpointers,
  "History of Greenpoint in 25 Buildings: the Havemeyer Sugar Refinery"]. *New York Times*, April 23, 1886, reported a near battle at the
  refinery gates [secondary citing the Times]. The owners waited them out; the men went back with nothing [secondary].
- Knights of Labor involvement in the 1886 refinery strike: **UNSURE** (likely, since the Knights organized widely in Brooklyn in 1886,
  but not found this pass). Script safe: "a union."
- October 1887: the refiners formed the Sugar Refineries Company, "the Sugar Trust," led by Henry O. Havemeyer [secondary: NYU
  Kheel Center finding aid, American Sugar records]. The trust then closed some refineries to cut output (standard account
  [secondary]; **UNSURE** on which Brooklyn plants and when). Story use: Jan 1887 pay cut (Ep 4) is a story beat; keep generic. Late
  1887: "the refineries joined into one giant trust; some closed. Michael kept his job, barely." (Callback to Ep 1 trusts.)
- Job detail: refinery work was hot (filter rooms above 100 degrees F commonly reported) [unverified; use "hot as an oven"].

### 1.7 Homestead, 1892 [secondary unless noted]
- Carnegie Steel's Homestead Works on the Monongahela, near Pittsburgh. The Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers (skilled
  men, AFL) contract ran out June 30, 1892. Henry Clay Frick ran the company; Andrew Carnegie was in Scotland (Britannica: gave Frick a
  free hand). Frick put up a three-mile fence topped with barbed wire, with holes for rifles (workers called it "Fort Frick") and locked
  the workers out; about 3,800 men.
- Night of July 5 to 6: about 300 Pinkerton guards (a private detective agency) towed up the river on two covered barges (the *Iron
  Mountain* and the *Monongahela*); arrived around 4 a.m. on July 6. Workers and townspeople met them at the riverbank. Gunfire through
  the day; strikers tried a burning raft and an oil slick set alight; the Pinkertons surrendered that afternoon and were made to walk a
  gauntlet of angry townspeople. **Sensitive.**
- Deaths: seven workers and three Pinkertons is the Pennsylvania historical marker figure [museum/official: PHMC marker]; other counts
  run higher (nine workers; "at least 11" in the *Post-Gazette*). Script: "at least ten people died." **CORRECTED** from any single
  precise figure.
- July 12: about 8,500 Pennsylvania militia took over the town. Carnegie's cable to Frick from Scotland (July, per the PHMC marker):
  backing him, whether the works ran that year, the next, or never; "no longer a question of wages" [museum; paraphrase on screen].
- July 23: Alexander Berkman, an anarchist with no tie to the union, shot and stabbed Frick in his office. Frick survived and was back
  at work within about a week [secondary]. Public sympathy swung away from the strikers. Strike ended November 1892; the union was broken
  at Homestead and steel stayed largely non-union until the 1930s.
- Hook beats: Pinkerton barges at dawn; Carnegie unreachable at a Scottish shooting lodge (lodge name "Rannoch" **UNSURE**, not
  confirmed; say "a castle in Scotland"? also UNSURE: say "on vacation in Scotland"); Frick shot, back at his desk in a week.
- Coxey's Army marched through Homestead in April 1894 [secondary: Wikipedia, Coxey's Army]: a neat crossover beat.

### 1.8 Pullman, 1894 [secondary]
- George Pullman built a company town south of Chicago (1880 to 1884): brick homes, a library, parks, no saloons. The company owned
  everything; rent came out of paychecks.
- After the Panic of 1893 Pullman cut wages about 25 to 30 percent (figures vary) but did not cut rents. "Nothing to arbitrate."
- Workers joined Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union (founded 1893, open to all railroad workers; about 150,000 members). From late
  June 1894 ARU members refused to handle trains with Pullman cars: up to about 250,000 workers in some 27 states.
- The railroads' General Managers' Association hooked Pullman cars to mail trains. Attorney General Richard Olney (a former railroad
  lawyer and director) got a federal court **injunction** (a judge's order to stop) on July 2, under the Sherman Act and for blocking the
  U.S. mail. President Cleveland sent federal troops into Chicago on July 4 over Illinois Gov. Altgeld's written protest.
- About 30 killed in the violence that followed (estimates vary). Debs jailed six months for contempt; Supreme Court upheld it, *In re
  Debs* (1895). In jail Debs read socialist writers; he later ran for president five times, the last in 1920 from a federal prison cell
  (for opposing World War I), winning about 913,000 votes [secondary]. Wow fact.
- Irony beat: the Sherman Antitrust Act (1890), meant to curb monopolies, was used against a union. VERIFIED (standard).
- Cleveland "If it takes the entire army and navy... to deliver a postal card in Chicago, that card will be delivered": **UNSURE**,
  widely quoted, source not checked; do not narrate.

## 2. Henry George and the 1886 New York mayoral race

- *Progress and Poverty* (1879): why does poverty grow alongside progress? George blamed land speculation and rising land rents; his
  fix was one big tax on the value of land itself, the **single tax**, replacing other taxes [secondary]. It sold millions of copies
  (often "the best-selling nonfiction book of its era after the Bible" [secondary, UNSURE as a claim; say "one of the best-selling books
  of the century"]).
- 1886: New York's Central Labor Union and allies (Knights, trade unions, socialists, reformers) formed the United Labor Party and
  nominated George; he accepted October 5, 1886 [secondary: Wikipedia ULP; LGM "This Day in Labor History"]. He asked for 30,000 signed
  pledges first and got them (standard account) [secondary, UNSURE on exact number].
- Result, November 2, 1886: Abram Hewitt (Democrat, Tammany-backed, an iron manufacturer) 90,552 (41.2%); **Henry George (United Labor)
  68,110 (31.0%)**; **Theodore Roosevelt (Republican, age 28) 60,435 (27.5%)** [secondary: Wikipedia, 1886 NYC mayoral election; City
  Journal, Brookhiser 1993]. VERIFIED.
- "Tammany counted him out": George and many supporters believed fraud cost them the race; Tammany's Richard Croker later said they
  could not allow a man like George to be mayor [secondary: American Heritage, "The Man Who Raised Hell"]. No study proves ballots were
  stolen; many Republicans also switched to Hewitt to stop George. **Disputed. Script: "George's people always believed they'd been
  counted out. Nobody ever proved it."**
- Catholic angle (strong for an Irish family): Father Edward McGlynn, a popular Manhattan priest, campaigned for George; Archbishop
  Corrigan suspended him, and he was excommunicated in 1887 (restored 1892) [secondary, standard; **UNSURE** on exact dates, check].
  Possible Ma line. Optional.
- Brooklyn: the City of Brooklyn was separate until January 1, 1898, so Michael could not vote for George. No Brooklyn mayor's race was
  held in November 1886 (Brooklyn mayors served two-year terms elected in odd years: Daniel D. Whitney elected 1885, Alfred C. Chapin
  1887) [unverified, from memory: **UNSURE**]. A Brooklyn/Kings County United Labor ticket in 1886 was **not found** this pass
  (**UNSURE**); the ULP did run state and local candidates in 1887 [secondary]. Recommendation: Michael "walked across the bridge to
  cheer for a man he wasn't allowed to vote for." Brooklyn Bridge pedestrian toll at opening was one cent [unverified, UNSURE; skip or
  check].
- Brother conflict: Michael's union backs George; Tommy's Brooklyn machine (McLaughlin Democrats, allied with the Democratic ticket)
  opposes. VERIFIED in spirit: the Democratic machines on both sides of the river opposed George.
- Timing fix: Ep 4 ends January 1887; the race was two months earlier. Open the Kelly scene in January 1887 with the argument still hot

## 3. Washington's Problem: Garfield, Guiteau and civil service (45 to 60 seconds)

- **Spoils system:** winners gave government jobs to supporters. Office seekers lined up at the White House [secondary: National
  Archives, Pendleton Act milestone page].
- Charles Guiteau, a failed lawyer and office seeker who believed he had earned a diplomatic post (he asked for Paris, among others)
  for a speech he wrote, shot President James Garfield at the Baltimore and Potomac railroad station in Washington, July 2, 1881.
  Garfield died September 19, 1881, after weeks of doctors probing the wound with unwashed fingers and instruments; infection, more
  than the bullet, likely killed him [secondary; standard; **sensitive**, one sentence, no humor]. Alexander Graham Bell tried a metal
  detector to find the bullet [secondary]: an intriguing hook beat if time allows.
- Guiteau's words on arrest: most often "I am a Stalwart of the Stalwarts! ... Arthur is President now!" (National Archives *Prologue*,
  fall 2016, Bellamy). Other versions: "I did it and will go to jail for it. I am a Stalwart and Arthur will be President" (American
  Heritage). **CORRECTED: wording varies; on screen use "I am a Stalwart... Arthur is President now!" with "witnesses reported."**
  (Stalwarts = the Republican faction that defended spoils; Vice President Chester Arthur was one.) Guiteau was hanged June 30, 1882;
  he was very likely mentally ill (insanity plea rejected).
- **Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act, January 16, 1883:** some federal jobs (about 10 percent at first) filled by competitive exams;
  officeholders could not be forced to give money to parties [official: National Archives]. VERIFIED.
- New York State followed in 1883 (Gov. Grover Cleveland signed; Chapter 354, Laws of 1883). At first it only *allowed* mayors of cities
  over 50,000 to set exam rules; 1884 amendments (Chapters 357 and 410) made it the general law for cities, and Brooklyn's Mayor Seth Low
  issued rules under them [secondary: NY court opinions *Chittenden v. Wurster* (1897), *In re Sweeley* (1895); NYS Civil Service
  mural page]. **VERIFIED: it reached Brooklyn city jobs from 1884.** Laborers and many low jobs stayed outside exams [secondary,
  standard]. Tommy tie-in works: "Now some city clerks had to pass a test. Tommy's kind of job? Still a favor." One line.
- The turn: reform fixed *who got the jobs*, not railroad rates, money, or wages. Interstate Commerce Act 1887 (first federal regulatory
  commission; weak, lost most of its early Supreme Court cases) and Sherman Antitrust Act 1890 (vague; first big use was against a
  union, Pullman 1894; *U.S. v. E. C. Knight*, 1895, let the Sugar Trust stand because refining was "manufacturing," not commerce)
  [secondary]. **Wow fact for the Kellys: the Supreme Court ruled the Sugar Trust (which controlled about 98 percent of U.S. refining)
  was not covered by the antitrust law.** VERIFIED (standard; E. C. Knight, 1895; the 98 percent figure is standard [secondary]).

### 1.9 Collective bargaining and labor key terms (coordinator requirement, Nov 16)
- **Collective bargaining:** workers negotiating together, as a group, through a union, instead of one by one, over wages, hours and
  conditions. First defined when Michael joins the Knights (Jan 1887). Payoffs: (1) Homestead began as a contract fight: the
  Amalgamated Association's three-year contract (wage scale) with Carnegie Steel expired June 30, 1892; the company demanded a lower
  minimum on the sliding wage scale and refused to deal with the union at all after the deadline, then locked the men out [secondary:
  Britannica; PHMC marker; the "$25 to $22" minimum is the standard figure, **UNSURE** on exact numbers, do not narrate them]. VERIFIED
  as "a contract fight." (2) Gompers and the AFL: bargain for "bread and butter" gains (wages, hours) through contracts [secondary].
  (3) Pullman: "nothing to arbitrate" = Pullman refused to bargain. (4) Close: collective bargaining became federal law with the Wagner
  Act, 1935 [official/secondary; VERIFIED].
- **Lockout:** the owner shuts workers out until they accept terms (Homestead, July 1 to 2, 1892). **Blacklist:** a list of union men
  employers would not hire (common after 1877, Homestead, Pullman; Debs's ARU men were blacklisted after 1894 [secondary]).
  **Strikebreaker** (strikers called them "scabs"; insulting then and now: narrate "strikebreaker," show "scab" once at most, in quotes,
  as the strikers' word). **Pinkertons:** the Pinkerton National Detective Agency, a private company hired by owners as guards, spies
  and strikebreakers. **Injunction:** a court order to stop doing something (Pullman, July 2, 1894). **Company town:** a town owned by
  the employer, homes, stores and all (Pullman).

## 4. Farmers and the Populists

### 4.1 The farmers' squeeze [secondary]
- Prices fell for decades: wheat from roughly a dollar a bushel around 1870 to about 50 cents by 1894; cotton from over 10 cents a pound
  in the 1870s to under 6 cents in the mid 1890s [secondary, standard textbook figures; **say "about half"**].
- Corn so cheap that in some years Kansas and Nebraska farmers burned it for fuel (one textbook: as low as ten cents a bushel, no year
  given; a Kansas history dates it to the early 1870s; other accounts to the early 1890s) [secondary: Roark et al., *The American
  Promise*; ksgenweb 1912 Kansas cyclopedia]. **CORRECTED: no fixed year; script "some years."**
- Debt: farmers mortgaged land to buy machines and more land; interest often 8 to 10 percent or more on the plains [secondary]. Falling
  prices meant paying back loans with dollars that were harder to earn (deflation). Railroads charged what they liked where there was
  one line; grain elevator and storage fees. In the South, the crop-lien system.
- Tight money: the U.S. stopped coining silver dollars in 1873 ("the Crime of '73" to silver men); money supply did not keep up with
  population and output; prices fell. Silver men wanted "free silver" at 16 ounces of silver to 1 of gold, more money, higher prices,
  easier debts [secondary]. **Gold standard:** every dollar backed by, and exchangeable for, gold.
- Late 1880s plains bust: boom years of rain (early 1880s) then drought from 1887; many farmers in western Kansas and Nebraska gave up
  [secondary]. Nebraska drought of 1894 severe [secondary, UNSURE on detail].

### 4.2 The Grange and the Farmers' Alliance (compress to about 25 seconds)
- **Grange** (Patrons of Husbandry), founded 1867 by Oliver Hudson Kelley, a Minnesota farmer and clerk at the Agriculture Department
  [secondary]. Light humor: "Oliver Kelley. No relation." Granger laws regulated railroad rates; *Munn v. Illinois* (1877) upheld them;
  *Wabash v. Illinois* (1886) struck down state rules on interstate traffic, which led to the ICC (1887) [secondary]. VERIFIED.
- **Farmers' Alliance:** began in Texas in the late 1870s; Southern and Northern Alliances by the late 1880s, over a million members
  [secondary]. Co-ops to buy and sell together; the "subtreasury plan" (government warehouses lending money against stored crops).
  **Colored Farmers' Alliance** founded in Texas 1886, claimed up to a million (disputed) [secondary]. Lecturers: Mary Elizabeth Lease.
  "Raise less corn and more hell": **CORRECTED: Lease later said reporters invented it; History Matters and Kansas Memory call it
  misattributed. Do not put it in her mouth; "a famous line, which she may never have said" or cut.**
- 1890: Alliance candidates won big in Kansas and the South. "Sockless" Jerry Simpson of Kansas won a House seat; the nickname came from
  a jab about silk stockings (standard story) [secondary, UNSURE on details: light humor candidate].

### 4.3 People's Party and the Omaha Platform, July 4, 1892 [primary text, standard edition]
- Founded formally 1892 (St. Louis Feb, Omaha July). Platform preamble by Ignatius Donnelly of Minnesota (also a writer who argued
  Atlantis was real and Bacon wrote Shakespeare [secondary]: light humor candidate). Key lines [primary, Omaha Platform text, e.g.
  History Matters / Wikisource]:
  - "We meet in the midst of a nation brought to the verge of moral, political, and material ruin."
  - "The fruits of the toil of millions are boldly stolen to build up colossal fortunes for a few..."
  - "From the same prolific womb of governmental injustice we breed the two great classes, tramps and millionaires." (On screen with the
    original punctuation; the source has a dash after "classes," which our no-em-dash rule replaces with a comma in narration only.)
- Demands: free and unlimited coinage of silver at 16 to 1; more money in circulation; a graduated income tax; government ownership of
  railroads, telegraph and telephone; postal savings banks; and, in the attached resolutions, direct election of U.S. senators, a secret
  ballot, the eight-hour day for government work, one term for the president, initiative and referendum, restriction of immigration
  [primary]. VERIFIED (standard text).
- 1892 result: James B. Weaver over one million votes (about 8.5 percent) and 22 electoral votes; carried Kansas, Colorado, Idaho and
  Nevada [secondary: Britannica; exact popular count varies by source, 1,024,000 to 1,041,000: **say "over a million"**]. CORRECTED (exact
  figure not narrated).
- **Populism:** a movement claiming to speak for ordinary "producers" (farmers, workers) against bankers, railroads and big business.

## 5. Hard times: the Panic of 1893 and Coxey's Army

### 5.1 Panic of 1893 [secondary]
- February 1893 the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad failed; May 1893 stock crash; hundreds of banks (often "about 500") and some 15,000
  businesses failed; several big railroads went bankrupt (Northern Pacific, Union Pacific, Santa Fe). Run on the Treasury's gold.
- Unemployment: estimates for 1894 range from about 12 percent (Romer) to about 18 percent (Lebergott). **Disputed: say "more than one
  worker in ten."** CORRECTED.
- 1895: J. P. Morgan and the Rothschilds lent the Treasury gold to save the gold standard [secondary]: a gold beat to set up 1896.

### 5.2 Coxey's Army, 1894 [secondary: Wikipedia, Ohio History Connection, Encyclopedia.com]
- Jacob Coxey, a wealthy quarry owner from Massillon, Ohio, wanted Congress to hire the unemployed to build roads, paid for with new
  paper money (the Good Roads bill; $500 million is the usual figure [secondary, UNSURE]). His partner Carl Browne, a showman and
  ex-cartoonist in a buckskin coat (standard description [secondary]), named it the "Commonweal of Christ" (formal name "Army of the
  Commonweal in Christ"). Browne believed bits of Christ's soul were reincarnated in the marchers [secondary, UNSURE: check before
  narrating]. A "**petition in boots**": a petition delivered by walking. VERIFIED.
- Left Massillon March 25, 1894 with about 100 men; marched through Pittsburgh and Homestead in April; about 500 reached Washington on
  April 30. VERIFIED.
- Coxey's youngest son was named **Legal Tender** Coxey (named for his father's money ideas) and rode with the march; Browne married
  Coxey's daughter Mamie, who rode a white horse as the "Goddess of Peace" at the head of the May 1 parade [secondary]. VERIFIED (son's
  birth year UNSURE; "his little son").
- **May 1, 1894:** police stopped Coxey at the Capitol before he could read his speech; he, Browne and Christopher Columbus Jones were
  charged with carrying banners on the Capitol grounds, and Coxey and Browne with walking on (trampling) the grass. Sentence (May 21):
  20 days in jail for the banners, plus a $5 fine for the grass [secondary: Ohio History Connection; Wikipedia]. Some accounts say
  police clubbed marchers. **CORRECTED: "arrested for walking on the grass" is true but partial; the jail time was for the banners.
  Script: "They were charged with carrying banners on the Capitol grounds, and walking on the grass."** (The third man's name,
  Christopher Columbus Jones, is a light humor beat.)
- Out west: other "industrial armies" rode the rails. On April 21, 1894, William Hogan's men (about 500) seized a Union Pacific train in
  Montana; federal troops caught them near Forsyth, Montana [secondary]. VERIFIED. Charles T. Kelley's army (from California) included
  a young **Jack London**, who joined at Council Bluffs, Iowa, and later wrote about it [secondary]. VERIFIED. **Spelling note:
  "Kelley's Army" (Charles T. Kelley); a "Kelly" echo the script can enjoy: "No relation. Again."**
- 1944: on May 1, Coxey, aged 90, finally read his speech from the Capitol steps [secondary]. Wow fact; VERIFIED (Wikipedia).
- Coxey's idea (government jobs in a depression) returned in the New Deal [secondary interpretive].

## 6. 1896: gold vs silver, and what came after

### 6.1 The campaign [secondary unless noted]
- Democrats split; at Chicago, July 9, 1896, William Jennings Bryan of Nebraska, 36, former congressman, gave the "Cross of Gold" speech
  and won the nomination the next day; the youngest major-party nominee ever [secondary]. Delegates carried him on their shoulders
  [secondary, standard].
- Exact lines [primary: speech text, *Official Proceedings of the Democratic National Convention*, 1896; History Matters]:
  - "The man who is employed for wages is as much a business man as his employer..."
  - "Burn down your cities and leave our farms, and your cities will spring up again as if by magic; but destroy our farms and the grass
    will grow in the streets of every city in the country."
  - "...we will answer their demand for a gold standard by saying to them: You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of
    thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."
  - He ended with arms spread like a cross [secondary, standard account]. VERIFIED (text standard; History Matters URL changed, recheck
    one edition before final: wording above matches the standard text).
- Populists also nominated Bryan (fusion) but with Tom Watson of Georgia for vice president instead of the Democrats' Arthur Sewall:
  Bryan had two running mates [secondary]. Light humor beat. Fusion swallowed the party.
- Bryan traveled about 18,000 miles and gave some 600 speeches; McKinley stayed home in Canton, Ohio, and gave a "front porch"
  campaign to visiting crowds (about 750,000 visitors is the usual figure) [secondary]. Mark Hanna raised about $3.5 million (some
  estimates far higher) vs a few hundred thousand for Bryan [secondary, figures vary: "many times more"].
- Fear campaign: some employers told workers the plant would close if Bryan won [secondary, standard; specific examples UNSURE]. Many
  city workers feared free silver would raise prices faster than wages. This is Michael's dilemma.
- Result: McKinley 7.1 million (about 51 percent) to Bryan 6.5 million (about 47 percent); electoral 271 to 176; turnout about 79 percent
  [secondary/official]. Bryan swept the South and West; McKinley the Northeast and Midwest, including the cities. New York State went
  heavily for McKinley; Kings County (Brooklyn) for McKinley [**UNSURE**, likely; check].
- Why Populism lost: fusion with the Democrats; the cities and workers did not join the farmers; race split the Southern movement; money
  and the fear campaign; then prices rose anyway after 1897 as new gold came from South Africa, the Klondike (1896 to 1899) and the
  cyanide process, expanding the money supply without silver [secondary]. Irony beat: the farmers got more money after all, through gold.
  Gold Standard Act 1900.

### 6.2 What became law later [official/secondary]
- 16th Amendment (federal income tax), ratified February 3, 1913. 17th Amendment (direct election of senators), ratified April 8, 1913.
- Secret ("Australian") ballot adopted by most states from 1888 to the mid 1890s. Postal savings banks 1910. Federal Reserve 1913 (a more
  flexible money supply). Federal Farm Loan Act and Warehouse Act 1916 (echoes of the subtreasury). Eight-hour day for railroad workers,
  Adamson Act 1916; 40-hour week, Fair Labor Standards Act 1938. Initiative and referendum: South Dakota first, 1898; Oregon 1902.
  Collective bargaining protected, Wagner Act 1935. U.S. off the gold standard for domestic money in 1933 (fully in 1971). Women's
  suffrage (many Populists backed it) 1920. VERIFIED (standard dates).
- Series close: the Progressive Era (about 1900 to 1920): reformers in both old parties adopt many Populist and labor ideas.

### 6.3 The Wizard of Oz theory
- Henry Littlefield (1964) read *The Wonderful Wizard of Oz* (1900) as a Populist allegory (silver shoes on a yellow brick road, Oz =
  ounces). Baum never said so; most scholars treat it as a fun reading, not a code [secondary]. **UNSURE/DISPUTED: optional end-card
  joke only, "Some people think... probably not."**

## 7. The Kellys: research hooks for the story
- O'Neill, Holt County, Nebraska: Irish colony founded 1874 by John O'Neill, a Fenian and Civil War veteran; the largest formal Irish
  colony in Nebraska [museum: historical marker HMdb 9632; Wikipedia]. Proposed home for Uncle Owen Kelly (Da's younger brother).
  Nebraska is also Bryan's state and the site of the Omaha convention: his letters can carry 1887 to 1896.
- Brooklyn consolidated into Greater New York, January 1, 1898 [official]: finale image on the bridge.
- Sugar: E. C. Knight (1895) let the Sugar Trust stand: Michael's employer, untouched by the antitrust law.

## Decisions (open, for the user)
1. **Cold open:** Homestead barges at dawn (recommended) means Homestead appears twice: cold open (no names, just the river) and chapter
   6 (who and why). Alternates in PLAN.md.
2. **Michael's union:** Knights of Labor (1887), honest about its fade; by 1893 Michael is laid off and unorganized. Agree?
3. **1896 twist:** Michael leans McKinley (fear of the factory gate and of prices), Tommy's Brooklyn Democrats officially back Bryan:
   the brothers swap sides from 1886. Historically sound (urban workers mostly went McKinley), and emotionally strong. Or keep Michael for
   Bryan and make Ma the swing.
4. **Uncle Owen in O'Neill, Nebraska** (Da's brother). Or a cousin in Kansas (Mary Lease country).
5. **Garfield's doctors:** one line on unwashed fingers is accurate and memorable; cut if it feels gory.
6. **Wizard of Oz:** cut or end-card joke.
7. **"Scab":** narrate "strikebreaker"; "scab" shown once as the strikers' word, or never.
