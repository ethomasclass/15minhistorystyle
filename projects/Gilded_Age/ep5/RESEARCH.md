## 1. Labor: why workers organized, 1877 to 1894

### 1.1 Why workers organized [secondary]
- 10 to 12 hour days, six days a week, common in industry; steel ran 12-hour shifts, with a 24-hour "long turn" when crews switched.
- No workers' compensation, no unemployment pay, no pension. Wage cuts came with every downturn (1873 to 1879, 1884 to 1886, 1893 to
  1897). Owners could replace strikers with new immigrants (ties to Ep 3).
- Source: Library of Congress classroom materials; Britannica "labor movement"; Foner, *History of the Labor Movement*, vol. 2.

### 1.2 The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 [secondary]
- Began July 16, 1877 at Martinsburg, West Virginia, when the Baltimore and Ohio cut wages 10 percent (another cut after earlier ones).
- Spread along the lines to Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Chicago, St. Louis. In Pittsburgh (July 21 to 22) militia fired on a crowd, about
  20 killed; crowds burned the Pennsylvania Railroad's roundhouse and more than 100 locomotives and over 1,000 cars [secondary,
  figures vary].
- President Hayes sent federal troops: the first time federal troops broke a nationwide strike. Roughly 100 people died overall.
- Hook: "the first nationwide strike," and it ended with the U.S. Army. Images already gathered in `ep5/public/img/b_1877_strike/`.

### 1.3 Knights of Labor and the eight-hour movement [secondary]
- Founded 1869, Philadelphia (Uriah Stephens), secret at first (members used symbols; the name was written as five stars in public).
  Terence Powderly led from 1879. Open to skilled and unskilled, women, and Black workers; excluded Chinese workers (and "parasites":
  bankers, lawyers, liquor sellers, gamblers).
- 1885: Knights' railroad strike beat Jay Gould's Wabash line; membership jumped to roughly 700,000 by mid 1886 (often given as about
  730,000) [secondary]. Then collapsed after Haymarket and failed strikes, to about 100,000 by 1890.
- May 1, 1886: some 300,000 to 350,000 workers nationwide struck or marched for an eight-hour day [secondary]. Slogan: "Eight hours
  for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will."
- Secret meetings: fits Michael's teaser ("secret meetings, about a union"). The Knights dropped full secrecy in 1881, but local
  assemblies still met quietly because employers fired union men [secondary]. Script: "meetings the foreman wasn't supposed to know
  about." VERIFIED in spirit.

### 1.4 Haymarket, Chicago, May 4, 1886 [secondary]
- May 3: police killed at least two strikers at the McCormick Harvesting Machine works. May 4: protest meeting in Haymarket Square;
  rain, a small crowd; police moved to disperse it; someone threw a bomb. One officer died at once, six more later (seven police in
  all; some likely hit by other police in the gunfire); at least four civilians killed. The bomber was never identified.
- Eight anarchists convicted with little evidence tying them to the bomb; four hanged November 11, 1887; one (Lingg) killed himself
  in his cell. Gov. John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three survivors June 26, 1893, and was attacked for it.
- Effect: a panic about "anarchists" and immigrants; the Knights, though not involved, were blamed and declined.
- Callback for the finale: the same Altgeld later protests Cleveland's troops at Pullman (1894). Good thread, one line.
- **Sensitive.** Source: Chicago History Museum / Encyclopedia of Chicago "Haymarket"; Library of Congress "Haymarket Riot."

### 1.5 AFL and Gompers (compress to about 20 seconds) [secondary]
- American Federation of Labor founded December 1886 (Columbus, Ohio). Samuel Gompers, a cigar maker born in London (1850) to a Dutch
  Jewish family, led it almost continuously to 1924.
- Skilled trades only, each craft its own union; "pure and simple" goals: wages, hours, conditions. Asked what labor wanted, Gompers is
  known for "More." The longer 1893 line: "We want more schoolhouses and less jails; more books and less arsenals; more learning and
  less vice; more leisure and less greed..." [secondary; the one-word "More" is a later compression: say "his answer boiled down to
  one word: more"].
- Survives because it is narrow; leaves the unskilled (Michael) out. Good contrast: the Knights wanted to change society; the AFL wanted
  a bigger paycheck.

### 1.6 Brooklyn sugar refinery strike, 1886, and the Sugar Trust, 1887 (Michael's real backdrop)
- Spring 1886: workers at Havemeyers & Elder and other Williamsburg refineries struck for a ten-hour day (from twelve) and about $1.75
  a day, plus union recognition; one account says about 3,000 men out, with longshoremen and coopers [secondary: Greenpointers,
  "History of Greenpoint in 25 Buildings: the Havemeyer Sugar Refinery"]. *New York Times*, April 23, 1886, reported a near battle at the
  refinery gates [secondary citing the Times]. The owners waited them out; the men went back with nothing [secondary].
- Knights of Labor involvement in the 1886 refinery strike: **UNSURE** (likely, since the Knights organized widely in Brooklyn in 1886,
  but not found this pass). Script safe: "a union."
- October 1887: the refiners formed the Sugar Refineries Company, "the Sugar Trust," led by Henry O. Havemeyer [secondary: NYU
  Kheel Center finding aid, American Sugar records]. The trust then closed some refineries to cut output (standard account
  [secondary]; **UNSURE** on which Brooklyn plants and when). Story use: Jan 1887 pay cut (Ep 4) is a story beat; keep generic. Late
  1887: "the refineries joined into one giant trust; some closed. Michael kept his job, barely." (Callback to Ep 1 trusts.)
- Job detail: refinery work was hot (filter rooms above 100 degrees F commonly reported) [unverified; use "hot as an oven"].

### 1.7 Homestead, 1892 [secondary unless noted]
- Carnegie Steel's Homestead Works on the Monongahela, near Pittsburgh. The Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers (skilled
  men, AFL) contract ran out June 30, 1892. Henry Clay Frick ran the company; Andrew Carnegie was in Scotland (Britannica: gave Frick a
  free hand). Frick put up a three-mile fence topped with barbed wire, with holes for rifles (workers called it "Fort Frick") and locked
  the workers out; about 3,800 men.
- Night of July 5 to 6: about 300 Pinkerton guards (a private detective agency) towed up the river on two covered barges (the *Iron
  Mountain* and the *Monongahela*); arrived around 4 a.m. on July 6. Workers and townspeople met them at the riverbank. Gunfire through
  the day; strikers tried a burning raft and an oil slick set alight; the Pinkertons surrendered that afternoon and were made to walk a
  gauntlet of angry townspeople. **Sensitive.**
- Deaths: seven workers and three Pinkertons is the Pennsylvania historical marker figure [museum/official: PHMC marker]; other counts
  run higher (nine workers; "at least 11" in the *Post-Gazette*). Script: "at least ten people died." **CORRECTED** from any single
  precise figure.
- July 12: about 8,500 Pennsylvania militia took over the town. Carnegie's cable to Frick from Scotland (July, per the PHMC marker):
  backing him, whether the works ran that year, the next, or never; "no longer a question of wages" [museum; paraphrase on screen].
- July 23: Alexander Berkman, an anarchist with no tie to the union, shot and stabbed Frick in his office. Frick survived and was back
  at work within about a week [secondary]. Public sympathy swung away from the strikers. Strike ended November 1892; the union was broken
  at Homestead and steel stayed largely non-union until the 1930s.
- Hook beats: Pinkerton barges at dawn; Carnegie unreachable at a Scottish shooting lodge (lodge name "Rannoch" **UNSURE**, not
  confirmed; say "a castle in Scotland"? also UNSURE: say "on vacation in Scotland"); Frick shot, back at his desk in a week.
- Coxey's Army marched through Homestead in April 1894 [secondary: Wikipedia, Coxey's Army]: a neat crossover beat.

### 1.8 Pullman, 1894 [secondary]
- George Pullman built a company town south of Chicago (1880 to 1884): brick homes, a library, parks, no saloons. The company owned
  everything; rent came out of paychecks.
- After the Panic of 1893 Pullman cut wages about 25 to 30 percent (figures vary) but did not cut rents. "Nothing to arbitrate."
- Workers joined Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union (founded 1893, open to all railroad workers; about 150,000 members). From late
  June 1894 ARU members refused to handle trains with Pullman cars: up to about 250,000 workers in some 27 states.
- The railroads' General Managers' Association hooked Pullman cars to mail trains. Attorney General Richard Olney (a former railroad
  lawyer and director) got a federal court **injunction** (a judge's order to stop) on July 2, under the Sherman Act and for blocking the
  U.S. mail. President Cleveland sent federal troops into Chicago on July 4 over Illinois Gov. Altgeld's written protest.
- About 30 killed in the violence that followed (estimates vary). Debs jailed six months for contempt; Supreme Court upheld it, *In re
  Debs* (1895). In jail Debs read socialist writers; he later ran for president five times, the last in 1920 from a federal prison cell
  (for opposing World War I), winning about 913,000 votes [secondary]. Wow fact.
- Irony beat: the Sherman Antitrust Act (1890), meant to curb monopolies, was used against a union. VERIFIED (standard).
- Cleveland "If it takes the entire army and navy... to deliver a postal card in Chicago, that card will be delivered": **UNSURE**,
  widely quoted, source not checked; do not narrate.

## 2. Henry George and the 1886 New York mayoral race

- *Progress and Poverty* (1879): why does poverty grow alongside progress? George blamed land speculation and rising land rents; his
  fix was one big tax on the value of land itself, the **single tax**, replacing other taxes [secondary]. It sold millions of copies
  (often "the best-selling nonfiction book of its era after the Bible" [secondary, UNSURE as a claim; say "one of the best-selling books
  of the century"]).
- 1886: New York's Central Labor Union and allies (Knights, trade unions, socialists, reformers) formed the United Labor Party and
  nominated George; he accepted October 5, 1886 [secondary: Wikipedia ULP; LGM "This Day in Labor History"]. He asked for 30,000 signed
  pledges first and got them (standard account) [secondary, UNSURE on exact number].
- Result, November 2, 1886: Abram Hewitt (Democrat, Tammany-backed, an iron manufacturer) 90,552 (41.2%); **Henry George (United Labor)
  68,110 (31.0%)**; **Theodore Roosevelt (Republican, age 28) 60,435 (27.5%)** [secondary: Wikipedia, 1886 NYC mayoral election; City
  Journal, Brookhiser 1993]. VERIFIED.
- "Tammany counted him out": George and many supporters believed fraud cost them the race; Tammany's Richard Croker later said they
  could not allow a man like George to be mayor [secondary: American Heritage, "The Man Who Raised Hell"]. No study proves ballots were
  stolen; many Republicans also switched to Hewitt to stop George. **Disputed. Script: "George's people always believed they'd been
  counted out. Nobody ever proved it."**
- Catholic angle (strong for an Irish family): Father Edward McGlynn, a popular Manhattan priest, campaigned for George; Archbishop
  Corrigan suspended him, and he was excommunicated in 1887 (restored 1892) [secondary, standard; **UNSURE** on exact dates, check].
  Possible Ma line. Optional.
- Brooklyn: the City of Brooklyn was separate until January 1, 1898, so Michael could not vote for George. No Brooklyn mayor's race was
  held in November 1886 (Brooklyn mayors served two-year terms elected in odd years: Daniel D. Whitney elected 1885, Alfred C. Chapin
  1887) [unverified, from memory: **UNSURE**]. A Brooklyn/Kings County United Labor ticket in 1886 was **not found** this pass
  (**UNSURE**); the ULP did run state and local candidates in 1887 [secondary]. Recommendation: Michael "walked across the bridge to
  cheer for a man he wasn't allowed to vote for." Brooklyn Bridge pedestrian toll at opening was one cent [unverified, UNSURE; skip or
  check].
- Brother conflict: Michael's union backs George; Tommy's Brooklyn machine (McLaughlin Democrats, allied with the Democratic ticket)
  opposes. VERIFIED in spirit: the Democratic machines on both sides of the river opposed George.
- Timing fix: Ep 4 ends January 1887; the race was two months earlier. Open the Kelly scene in January 1887 with the argument still hot
  ("Tommy's club had just helped beat Michael's man").

## 3. Washington's Problem: Garfield, Guiteau and civil service (45 to 60 seconds)

- **Spoils system:** winners gave government jobs to supporters. Office seekers lined up at the White House [secondary: National
  Archives, Pendleton Act milestone page].
- Charles Guiteau, a failed lawyer and office seeker who believed he had earned a diplomatic post (he asked for Paris, among others)
  for a speech he wrote, shot President James Garfield at the Baltimore and Potomac railroad station in Washington, July 2, 1881.
  Garfield died September 19, 1881, after weeks of doctors probing the wound with unwashed fingers and instruments; infection, more
  than the bullet, likely killed him [secondary; standard; **sensitive**, one sentence, no humor]. Alexander Graham Bell tried a metal
  detector to find the bullet [secondary]: an intriguing hook beat if time allows.
- Guiteau's words on arrest: most often "I am a Stalwart of the Stalwarts! ... Arthur is President now!" (National Archives *Prologue*,
  fall 2016, Bellamy). Other versions: "I did it and will go to jail for it. I am a Stalwart and Arthur will be President" (American
  Heritage). **CORRECTED: wording varies; on screen use "I am a Stalwart... Arthur is President now!" with "witnesses reported."**
  (Stalwarts = the Republican faction that defended spoils; Vice President Chester Arthur was one.) Guiteau was hanged June 30, 1882;
  he was very likely mentally ill (insanity plea rejected).
- **Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act, January 16, 1883:** some federal jobs (about 10 percent at first) filled by competitive exams;
  officeholders could not be forced to give money to parties [official: National Archives]. VERIFIED.
- New York State followed in 1883 (Gov. Grover Cleveland signed; Chapter 354, Laws of 1883). At first it only *allowed* mayors of cities
  over 50,000 to set exam rules; 1884 amendments (Chapters 357 and 410) made it the general law for cities, and Brooklyn's Mayor Seth Low
  issued rules under them [secondary: NY court opinions *Chittenden v. Wurster* (1897), *In re Sweeley* (1895); NYS Civil Service
  mural page]. **VERIFIED: it reached Brooklyn city jobs from 1884.** Laborers and many low jobs stayed outside exams [secondary,
  standard]. Tommy tie-in works: "Now some city clerks had to pass a test. Tommy's kind of job? Still a favor." One line.
- The turn: reform fixed *who got the jobs*, not railroad rates, money, or wages. Interstate Commerce Act 1887 (first federal regulatory
  commission; weak, lost most of its early Supreme Court cases) and Sherman Antitrust Act 1890 (vague; first big use was against a
  union, Pullman 1894; *U.S. v. E. C. Knight*, 1895, let the Sugar Trust stand because refining was "manufacturing," not commerce)
  [secondary]. **Wow fact for the Kellys: the Supreme Court ruled the Sugar Trust (which controlled about 98 percent of U.S. refining)
  was not covered by the antitrust law.** VERIFIED (standard; E. C. Knight, 1895; the 98 percent figure is standard [secondary]).
