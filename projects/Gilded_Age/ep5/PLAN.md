## Episode question (the video may ask this; never the series compelling question)
**When ordinary people organized, in unions and then in a party of their own, what did they win, and what did it cost them?**
Short form for narration: "Could workers and farmers, by sticking together, get their fair share?"

## Cold open (stands alone; about 60 seconds)

**"Barges at Dawn" (recommended).** July 6, 1892, about 4 a.m., fog on the Monongahela River near Pittsburgh. Two covered barges, no
lights, towed upstream. Inside: about 300 armed men from a private detective agency. On the riverbank: steelworkers and their wives,
awake all night, because a lookout spotted the boats and the town's whistle blew. Nobody yet knows who fires first. Freeze before the
shooting. Turn: "These men weren't soldiers. They worked for a private company, hired by the richest steel company in America. How did
America get to the point where a company sent its own army up a river against its own workers? And what were workers, and farmers, going
to do about it?" [RESEARCH 1.7]
- Why it works: a dawn mystery (who is on the boats?), a ticking clock, a moral question. No gore: fade on the first shot; deaths are
  told later, plainly, in chapter 8.
- Drawn set: fog, river, two barges, silhouettes on the bank, the mill stacks; mill whistle sound cue.

Alternates:
- **"Arrested for Walking on the Grass."** May 1, 1894, Washington. A parade of about 500 jobless men, a woman on a white horse dressed as
  the Goddess of Peace, a little boy named Legal Tender. Their leader climbs the Capitol steps to speak, and police arrest him. Charges:
  carrying banners, and walking on the grass. "How did a protest about jobs end over a lawn?" [RESEARCH 5.2] Funny, safe, strong; but
  it starts the finale in 1894 and weakens chapter 9.
- **"Corn in the Stove."** A Nebraska winter. A farm family feeds the stove, not with coal but with ears of corn: their own crop, worth
  less than fuel. "America was the richest country in the world. Why were its farmers burning their food to stay warm?" [RESEARCH 4.1]
  Emotional; ties straight to Uncle Owen; but the date is soft ("some years"), so it must stay a composite scene.

## Kelly story spine (1887 to 1898)

Family: **Michael** (b. about 1864; 23 in 1887, 32 in 1896), refinery worker; **Tommy** (about 18 in 1887), errand runner for Mr. Duffy's
Democratic club (Brooklyn's McLaughlin machine); **Ma**; **Nora**, maid on Fifth Avenue; upstairs, the **Rabinowitzes** (Yankel, Sore,
Rivke). NEW: **Uncle Owen Kelly**, Da's younger brother, who went west with the 1870s Irish colony to **O'Neill, Holt County, Nebraska**,
and farms 160 acres; his letters (read in his voice, addressed to Ma) carry the farm story. Alternative: a cousin in Kansas.

## Gold vs gilt payoff (series close)
HANDOFF's through-line "Who got the gold, and who only got the gilt?" pays off literally in 1896: the country argues about gold itself.
Closing idea: the Kellys never got the gold; but the things ordinary people fought for (shorter days, a fair vote, a tax on the richest)
were slowly made real, and that was not gilt. End on the question open, never naming the series compelling question.

## Carnegie and Frick: back to "heroes or villains" (user, Nov 16)

The user wants Homestead to land on Carnegie, not just Frick, and to reopen Part 1's question about the tycoons.
- **Make the chain of command unmistakable:** an on-screen org card: ANDREW CARNEGIE (owner) → HENRY CLAY FRICK (chairman, Carnegie Steel) →
  the Homestead works. Narration says it plainly: "Frick worked for Carnegie. It was Carnegie's company, and Carnegie's mill."
- **The contradiction (the hook beat):** in 1886 Carnegie wrote in *Forum* that "the right of the working man to combine and to form trades-unions
  is no less sacred than the right of the manufacturer" to organize, and joked about an eleventh commandment, "Thou shalt not take thy neighbor's
  job" (attribution of that line varies: frame as "he was quoted saying"). In 1892, his company locked out the union and brought in Pinkertons to
  run the mill with strikebreakers. From Scotland he cabled Frick his support and, by one account, said he would rather close the plant than
  rehire a striker [VERIFY exact wording: Nasaw, *Andrew Carnegie* (2006); Krause (1992)]. Carnegie had approved the plan to go non-union before
  sailing [VERIFY].
- **The libraries line:** a worker's reaction to Carnegie's gifts, "After working twelve hours, how can a man go to a library?" (recorded in
  labor histories [VERIFY source]). Optional press line: a St. Louis *Post-Dispatch* editorial said ten thousand Carnegie libraries could not make
  up for Homestead [UNVERIFIED: use only if the text is found].
- **Callback, without naming the series question:** "Remember the question from the start of this series? Carnegie gave away 90 percent of his
  fortune, built thousands of libraries... and his company broke the union at Homestead. Hero, villain, or both?" (Part 1 and Part 2 viewers get the
  payoff; new viewers still follow). Use the Ep 1 cue `ga_heroes_or_villains` under it. Then let Ch 11 widen it from the tycoons to everyone.
