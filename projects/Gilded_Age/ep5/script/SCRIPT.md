## 1. Barges at Dawn

July 6th, 1892. Four o'clock in the morning. The Monongahela River, near Pittsburgh.

Fog on the water. Two covered barges slide upstream behind a tugboat, no lights showing.

A lookout spots them. The mill whistle screams, and the town of Homestead runs down to the river. They tear down the company's fence and pack the landing. Some carry lanterns. Some carry rifles.

Inside the barges are about three hundred men with Winchester rifles. Not soldiers. Not police. Private guards, hired by one of the biggest steel companies in America to take its own mill back from its own workers.

The gangplank goes down.

On the barges, rifles come up. On the bank, rifles come up too.

A company's private army, aiming at American workers. And the workers, aiming right back.

Nobody knows who will fire first.

How did it come to this? This is the story of two groups left behind in the Gilded Age: workers in the cities, and farmers out west. Both fought back. To understand why, start with a job. Twelve hours a day.

## 2. Twelve Hours a Day

Twelve hours a day, six days a week. That's how long Michael Kelly worked at a sugar refinery in Brooklyn. It's January 1887. Michael is 23. He hauls heavy sacks and tends vats of boiling sugar, in heat that could make a man collapse.

That was normal. Here's what normal looked like.

Long hours. Ten to twelve hours a day was common. Steelworkers often worked seven days a week.

Low pay, and no say. The company set your wage, and it could cut it whenever it wanted.

Danger. Machines had no safety guards. The railroads alone killed thousands of their own workers every year. If you got hurt, the company owed you nothing. No pay while you healed. Maybe no job when you came back.

Children. By 1900, about one in six American kids between ten and fifteen had a job.

And no safety net. Lose your job, and there was no unemployment check.

So why not quit, or complain? Because every year, hundreds of thousands of new immigrants arrived looking for work. If Michael complained, the foreman could point at the gate.

And that January, the refinery cut the men's pay. Again.

One worker against a giant company had no chance. But all the workers, together? There was strength in numbers.

## 3. Strength in Numbers

Strength in numbers. That's the idea behind a union.

A {union} is a group of workers who join together to deal with their employer as one. One worker who asks for a raise can be fired. A thousand workers asking at once are much harder to ignore.

When a union and a company sit down to agree on pay, hours and safety for everyone, that's called {collective bargaining}. Collective means together. Bargaining means negotiating.

And if the company says no? The union's biggest weapon is a {strike}: everyone stops working at once. No workers, no product, no profit.

But companies had weapons too. They could fire union members and put their names on a {blacklist}, so nobody else would hire them. They could lock the gates until workers gave in. That's a {lockout}. They could bring in {strikebreakers}, new workers hired to replace the strikers. And they could call on the courts, private guards, even soldiers.

In 1877, when railroad workers went on strike across the country, the President sent in the army. About a hundred people died, and the strike failed.

Ten years later, Michael Kelly climbed the stairs to a back room above a saloon, to a meeting of the biggest union in America. A union that had just been through a fight over eight hours. And a bomb.

## 4. Eight Hours and a Bomb

The union was the {Knights of Labor}. They were once so secret that newspapers printed their name as five stars. By 1886 they had hundreds of thousands of members, and they let almost anyone join: skilled and unskilled workers, women, and Black workers. But not Chinese immigrants.

Their big goal was an eight-hour workday. On May 1st, 1886, hundreds of thousands of workers went on strike for it. Their slogan: eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will.

Three days later, at a protest in Chicago's Haymarket Square, police moved in. Someone threw a bomb. Seven police officers and at least four other people died in the explosion and the shooting that followed. Nobody ever proved who threw it.

Eight {anarchists}, people who wanted no government at all, were convicted anyway, on very little evidence. Four were hanged.

For many Americans, unions now meant violence. The Knights got the blame, and fell apart. A smaller union survived: Samuel Gompers' {American Federation of Labor}, for skilled workers, focused on better pay and shorter hours.

Michael joined the Knights anyway. His little brother was not happy.

TOMMY: [annoyed] A union? Mr. Duffy says the Knights are nothing but trouble.

MICHAEL: [dry] Mr. Duffy doesn't work at the refinery.

Tommy ran errands for the local political machine, and the machine didn't like anything it didn't control. Just that November, the brothers had fought over Henry George, a writer the unions ran for mayor of New York. Michael cheered for him. Tommy's machine helped beat him. George came in second, ahead of a young Theodore Roosevelt. Roosevelt would be fine.

Working people had scared the powerful. Six years later, the powerful struck back, at a steel mill the workers called Fort Frick.

## 5. Fort Frick

Fort Frick was the workers' nickname for the steel mill in Homestead, Pennsylvania. Remember those barges?

The mill belonged to Andrew Carnegie, one of the richest men in the world. His company was run by a tough manager named Henry Clay Frick. Remember that order. Carnegie owned the mill. Frick ran it for him.

In 1892, the union and the company were bargaining over a new contract. The company offered lower pay. When the union said no, Frick refused to deal with the union at all. He put a tall fence around the mill, topped with barbed wire. The workers called it Fort Frick. Then he locked them out.

To run the mill with new workers, Frick hired about three hundred guards from the Pinkerton Detective Agency, a private company that businesses hired to break strikes. Those were the men on the barges.

The shooting at the landing went on most of the day. At least ten people were killed, workers and guards. That afternoon, the Pinkertons surrendered.

But the workers didn't win. The governor sent eight thousand soldiers, and the mill reopened with strikebreakers. Then an anarchist with no connection to the union shot Frick. Frick survived, public opinion turned against the strikers, and the union was crushed.

And Andrew Carnegie? He spent the strike on vacation in Scotland, sending Frick his support.

But six years earlier, Carnegie had written that workers' right to form unions was "no less sacred" than the rights of owners. He gave away most of his fortune and built thousands of free libraries. Still, one steelworker asked, "After working twelve hours, how can a man go to a library?"

So, Andrew Carnegie. Hero? Villain? Or both?

For workers, things were about to get even worse. Hard times were coming.

## 6. Hard Times

Hard times hit in 1893. Banks failed. Railroads went bankrupt. By 1894, more than one worker in ten was out of a job. It was called a {depression}, the worst the country had seen.

The refinery let Michael go. Ma took in washing. Nora's pay as a maid kept the family fed.

In Ohio, a businessman named Jacob Coxey led hundreds of unemployed men on a march to Washington, to ask Congress for jobs building roads. They called themselves Coxey's Army. Coxey had even named his baby son Legal Tender, a term for official money. When they reached the Capitol, Coxey was arrested. The charges: carrying banners. And walking on the grass.

That same spring, near Chicago, the Pullman train car company cut its workers' pay by about a quarter. But the workers lived in a {company town}. Pullman owned their houses. And Pullman did not cut the rent.

The workers went on strike. A national railroad union, led by Eugene Debs, backed them by refusing to run any train pulling a Pullman car. Trains stopped across much of the country.

Then the government stepped in, on the companies' side. A judge issued an {injunction}, a court order banning the strike. President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago. About thirty people were killed. Debs went to jail, and the strike collapsed.

The lesson seemed clear: when workers took on big companies, the government usually sided with the companies.

Workers weren't the only ones who felt that way. Let's leave the city, go back a few years, and head out west, to Uncle Owen's letters.

## 7. Uncle Owen's Letters

Uncle Owen was Michael's uncle, his father's younger brother. He farmed 160 acres near O'Neill, Nebraska, out on the Great Plains. Every few months, a letter came to Brooklyn.

OWEN: [warm, weary] Wheat's selling for half what it did when I came out. Half. And the bank still wants its payment.

Farmers had three big problems.

First, debt. To buy land, seed and machines, most farmers borrowed from banks. Many took out a {mortgage}: a loan where, if you can't pay, the bank takes your farm.

Second, railroads. Often only one railroad served a town, so it charged whatever it wanted.

Third, and worst: crop prices kept falling. Wheat that sold for about a dollar a bushel dropped to around fifty cents. Corn got so cheap that some farmers burned it in their stoves instead of coal.

OWEN: [dry] Burned corn in the stove this winter. Cheaper than coal.

So farmers organized, too. First came the {Grange}, farmers' clubs started by a man named Oliver Kelley. No relation. Then the much bigger {Farmers' Alliance}.

But to understand why farmers were so angry, you need to understand the money problem.

## 8. The Money Problem

The money problem was this: the harder farmers worked, the deeper they sank into debt. Here's how.

Say Uncle Owen borrows a thousand dollars when wheat sells for a dollar a bushel. To pay it back, he needs to sell a thousand bushels.

Ten years later, wheat sells for fifty cents. He still owes a thousand dollars. But now he has to sell two thousand bushels. Same debt. Twice the work.

Why did prices keep falling? Part of the answer was money itself.

The United States was on the {gold standard}. Every dollar was supposed to be backed by gold in a government vault. So the country could only have as many dollars as it had gold.

But America was growing fast, with more and more things to buy and sell. The amount of money barely grew.

Picture a town with a hundred dollars and a hundred loaves of bread. Each loaf sells for a dollar. Now the town bakes two hundred loaves, but there are still only a hundred dollars. Each loaf now sells for fifty cents. More stuff, same money, so prices fall. That's called {deflation}.

Cheaper bread is great if you're buying. But if you're selling the wheat, and you owe the bank, it's a disaster.

Farmers had a solution: silver. If the government made dollars out of silver as well as gold, there would be more money going around. More money, higher prices. Wheat climbs back toward a dollar, and Owen's debt gets easier to pay. That idea was called {free silver}.

Not everyone liked it. Bankers wanted to be paid back in dollars worth as much as before. And city workers like Michael worried that if prices went up, so would bread and rent.

Farmers needed Washington on their side. But Washington wasn't listening. So they decided to build a party of their own.

## 9. A Party of Their Own

A party of their own. Why? Because both big parties seemed busier with something else: handing out government jobs to their supporters, called the {spoils system}. It got so bad that in 1881, a man who thought he was owed a job shot President James Garfield. Congress responded by requiring tests for some government jobs, called {civil service}. But that fixed who got jobs. It didn't fix railroad prices, crop prices or wages.

So on July 4th, 1892, in Omaha, Nebraska, farmers and workers formed a new party: the People's Party, also called the Populists. {Populism} means ordinary people uniting against powerful elites, like big banks, railroads and corporations.

They said America was producing two kinds of people: tramps, meaning homeless wanderers, and millionaires.

Their plan, the {Omaha Platform}, demanded free silver. An income tax, so the richest paid the most. Senators elected directly by the people. Government control of the railroads. And an eight-hour workday.

OWEN: [proud] We've got our own party now. Farmers and working men, together.

That November, the Populists won over a million votes. But the biggest fight came in 1896, when the whole election came down to silver, and a cross of gold.

## 10. The Cross of Gold

The cross of gold came from a speech.

In July 1896, a 36-year-old Nebraskan named William Jennings Bryan argued for free silver at the Democratic convention in Chicago. He said the gold standard was crushing working people, and he finished with his arms spread wide, like a cross:

"You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns. You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."

In plain words: don't sacrifice ordinary people to protect gold.

The Democrats made Bryan their candidate. Then the Populists chose him too.

OWEN: [fired up] Bryan's a Nebraska man. He's one of us.

Bryan crisscrossed the country. His opponent, Republican William McKinley, stayed home in Ohio and spoke from his front porch, while his campaign raised millions from big business. Some factory owners warned workers that if Bryan won, the factory might close.

At the Kellys' kitchen table, the family split. And this time, the brothers had switched sides.

TOMMY: [fired up] Bryan's for the working man! Even Mr. Duffy says so.

MICHAEL: [worried] The foreman says if Bryan wins, the refinery closes. And silver means higher prices for bread.

NORA: [amused] You should hear them on Fifth Avenue. They think Bryan's coming for the silverware.

MA: [firm] Your uncle's losing his farm, and you're arguing about metal.

McKinley won. Most city voters, including many factory workers, chose him. Bryan won the farm states, and it wasn't enough. The Populists soon faded away.

So after all that fighting, what did workers and farmers have to show for it? Was it gold, or just gilt?

## 11. Gold or Gilt

Gold or gilt? By 1900, it looked a lot like gilt.

Workers still had no legal right to form a union. Most still worked ten or twelve hours a day. Children still worked in mills and mines. Farmers were still in debt. And the government still usually took the side of business.

The strikers lost. The Populists lost. But they put their ideas in front of the whole country: shorter workdays, fair pay, the right to organize, a fairer tax, and a government that answered to ordinary people.

The Gilded Age left Americans wanting more. And in the years after 1900, many would start demanding it, in a new age of reform called the Progressive Era.

New Year's Day, 1898. Brooklyn became part of New York City. Michael and Tommy walked across the bridge together, voters in the same city at last. They still didn't vote the same way.

Upstairs, Rivke Rabinowitz was sewing shirts in a factory now. And she had started going to meetings. About a union.

The Kellys never got the gold. But they hadn't stopped fighting for their share.

That's the end of our story of the Gilded Age. Thanks for watching, and for sticking with the Kellys all the way.
