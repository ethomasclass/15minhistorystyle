## 1. Barges at Dawn

July 6th, 1892. Four o'clock in the morning. The Monongahela River, near Pittsburgh.

Fog on the water. Two covered barges slide upstream behind a tugboat, no lights showing.

A lookout spots them. The mill whistle screams, and the whole town of Homestead runs down to the river. Steelworkers. Their families. They tear down the company's fence and pack the landing. Some carry lanterns. Some carry rifles.

Inside the barges are about three hundred men with Winchester rifles. Not soldiers. Not police. Private guards, hired by the biggest steel company in America to take its own mill back from its own workers.

The gangplank goes down.

On the barges, rifles come up. On the bank, rifles come up too.

A company's private army, aiming at American workers, in an American town. And the workers, aiming right back.

Nobody knows who will fire first.

How did America get here, where a company could send its own army up a river? And when ordinary people stuck together, what did they win? And what did it cost them?

To find out, we go back five years, to Brooklyn. And a secret meeting.

## 2. The Secret Meeting

The secret meeting was in a back room above a saloon in Williamsburg. January, 1887.

Michael Kelly was there because the sugar refinery had just cut the men's pay. Again.

He worked twelve hours a day, six days a week, beside vats of boiling sugar. Get burned, and there was no pay while you healed. Complain, and a hundred men outside the gate would take your job.

One worker against one company? The company wins every time. But what if all the workers spoke at once?

That's a {union}. And when workers negotiate together as a group, for pay, hours and safer conditions, that's called {collective bargaining}.

The biggest union in America was the {Knights of Labor}. For years they were so secretive that newspapers printed their name as five stars. They let almost anyone join: skilled and unskilled, women, and Black workers too. But not Chinese workers. Even the Knights kept one door shut.

Workers had fought back before. In 1877, railroad men walked off the job in city after city, the first {strike} to spread across the whole country. The President sent in the army. About a hundred people died.

That night, Michael raised his hand. His little brother found out.

TOMMY: [annoyed] A union? Mr. Duffy says the Knights are nothing but trouble.

MICHAEL: [dry] Mr. Duffy doesn't work at the refinery.

Tommy ran errands for the political machine, and the machine didn't like anything it didn't control.

The brothers had been fighting since the fall. But that story starts the spring before, with a fight famous for two things. Eight hours, and a bomb.

## 3. Eight Hours and a Bomb

Eight hours. That was the dream.

On May 1st, 1886, hundreds of thousands of workers walked off the job for an eight-hour day. Their slogan: eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will.

Three days later, at a rally in Chicago's Haymarket Square, police moved in. Someone threw a bomb. Seven police officers and at least four other people died in the blast and the shooting that followed. Nobody ever proved who threw it.

Eight {anarchists}, people who wanted no government at all, were convicted anyway, on thin evidence. Four were hanged.

After Haymarket, many Americans saw unions as dangerous. The Knights took the blame, and began to fade.

A different union lasted: Samuel Gompers' {American Federation of Labor}, for skilled workers like carpenters and cigar makers. It bargained for bread-and-butter things: pay and hours. Asked what labor wanted, Gompers' answer boiled down to one word. More.

That fall, working people tried another way to win. Not a strike. A vote. For the man across the river.

## 4. The Man Across the River

The man across the river was Henry George.

His bestseller, *Progress and Poverty*, asked why, as America got richer, so many people stayed poor. His answer: one big tax on land, and no other taxes. The {single tax}.

In 1886, New York's unions asked George to run for mayor, for a brand-new labor party.

Brooklyn was a separate city then, so Michael couldn't vote for him. He walked across the Brooklyn Bridge to the torchlight rallies anyway. Tommy's machine, and Tammany Hall, worked to beat him.

George came in second. Ahead of a 28-year-old named Theodore Roosevelt, who came in third. Roosevelt would recover.

George's supporters believed Tammany had counted him out. Nobody ever proved it.

TOMMY: [teasing] Your man lost.

MICHAEL: [stubborn] He came close. Closer than anybody thought.

Out west, someone else in the family was asking George's question: why work harder, just to get poorer? He put it in Uncle Owen's letters.

## 5. Uncle Owen's Letters

Uncle Owen's letters came from O'Neill, Nebraska, an Irish farm colony on the Great Plains. He was Michael's father's younger brother.

OWEN: [warm, weary] Wheat's selling for half what it did when I came out. Half. And the bank still wants its ten percent.

To buy land and machines, farmers borrowed. A {mortgage}: a loan, with the farm as the promise. But crop prices kept falling, so a farmer had to grow twice as much to pay the same debt. And often one railroad was the only way to market. It charged whatever it liked.

OWEN: [dry] Burned corn in the stove this winter. Cheaper than coal.

That really happened. Some years, corn was so cheap that Plains farmers burned it to stay warm.

Underneath it all, there wasn't enough money going around. Dollars got scarcer and harder to earn. That's {deflation}: great if you lend money, terrible if you owe it.

So farmers organized too. First the {Grange}, founded by a man named Oliver Kelley. No relation. Then the bigger {Farmers' Alliance}, with co-ops so farmers could buy and sell together.

But the biggest problems needed Washington. And that was Washington's problem.

## 6. Washington's Problem

Washington's problem was jobs.

Whoever won the White House handed out thousands of government jobs to the people who helped them win. The {spoils system}. Every new president faced a line of job seekers out the door.

One was Charles Guiteau. He'd given a speech or two for James Garfield, and decided he deserved a job. Ideally in Paris. He didn't get one.

On July 2nd, 1881, Guiteau shot President Garfield in a Washington train station. Witnesses said he shouted, "Arthur is President now!"

Garfield lived eleven weeks. His doctors probed the wound with unwashed fingers, and infection did as much harm as the bullet.

Two years later, the {Pendleton Act} said some government jobs required passing a test, not knowing the right people. That's {civil service}. Soon even Brooklyn's clerks took exams. Errand boys, luckily for Tommy, did not.

But fixing who got jobs didn't fix railroad rates, money or wages. New laws on railroads and monopolies had little bite. The Supreme Court even ruled that the Sugar Trust, about 98 percent of America's sugar refining, wasn't covered. Michael's refinery was part of it.

So farmers started their own party, to warn America it was raising tramps and millionaires.

## 7. Tramps and Millionaires

Tramps and millionaires. That's how the new party put it, on July 4th, 1892, in Omaha.

Farmers and workers formed the People's Party, the Populists. {Populism}: ordinary people organizing against powerful elites, like banks, railroads and big business.

Their platform began: "From the same prolific womb of governmental injustice we breed the two great classes: tramps and millionaires."

The {Omaha Platform} demanded {free silver}, coining silver money to put more dollars in circulation. An income tax. Senators elected by voters. Government-run railroads. A secret ballot. An eight-hour day.

Plenty of people called those ideas crazy. Remember them.

OWEN: [proud] We've got our own party now. Farmers and working men, together.

That November, the Populists won over a million votes and four states.

But two days after Omaha, the barges reached the steel mill the workers called Fort Frick.

## 8. Fort Frick

Fort Frick. The workers named it for the fence the company built around the Homestead mill, topped with barbed wire, with holes for rifles.

The man behind it was Henry Clay Frick. And here's the part people forget: Frick worked for Andrew Carnegie. Carnegie owned most of the company. Frick ran it. Carnegie's company. Carnegie's mill.

It started as collective bargaining. The union and the company were negotiating a new contract. The company cut its offer, then refused to deal with the union at all. When the contract ran out, Frick shut the workers out. A {lockout}.

Then he hired about three hundred Pinkerton guards to take the mill for new workers. The men on the barges.

The shooting at the landing went on most of the day. At least ten people were killed, workers and Pinkertons. That afternoon, the Pinkertons surrendered. Days later, eight thousand state troops arrived, and the mill reopened with {strikebreakers}, hired to replace the strikers. The strikers had a harsher word: scabs.

That month, an anarchist with no connection to the union shot and stabbed Frick in his office. Frick was back at his desk in about a week. Public sympathy swung away from the strikers, and the union was broken.

And Andrew Carnegie? On vacation in Scotland, sending Frick his support.

Six years earlier, Carnegie had written that workers' right to form unions was "no less sacred" than the owners' right to organize. This is the man who gave away almost all his fortune and built thousands of libraries. One steelworker asked, "After working twelve hours, how can a man go to a library?"

So, Andrew Carnegie. Hero? Villain? Or both?

Michael read about Homestead on the front stoop. And the country was heading into hard times.

## 9. Hard Times

Hard times hit in 1893. Banks failed. Railroads went bankrupt. By 1894, more than one worker in ten had no job. A {panic}, then the worst depression yet.

The refinery let Michael go. Ma took in washing. Nora's wages kept the family afloat. Tommy's club handed out coal again.

In Ohio, a businessman named Jacob Coxey had an idea: the government should hire jobless men to build roads. He'd march to Washington and ask Congress in person. A petition in boots.

His daughter rode a white horse, dressed as the Goddess of Peace. And Coxey had named his youngest son Legal Tender.

On May 1st, 1894, about five hundred marchers reached the Capitol. Coxey climbed the steps to speak, and police arrested him. The charges: carrying banners. And walking on the grass.

Out west, some jobless "armies" didn't walk. In Montana, one stole an entire train. A young Jack London rode with another: Kelley's Army. No relation. Again.

That spring, near Chicago, the Pullman railroad car company cut wages by about a quarter. But its workers lived in Pullman's {company town}, and Pullman didn't cut the rent.

Eugene Debs's railway union refused to move trains carrying Pullman cars. Rail traffic stopped across much of the country.

A judge issued an {injunction}, a court order to end the strike. President Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago, over the governor's protest. About thirty people were killed. Debs went to jail, and strikers landed on a {blacklist}: names no employer would hire.

Strikes had failed. Marches had failed. That left the vote, and a speech about a cross of gold.

## 10. The Cross of Gold

The cross of gold was about money.

America was on the {gold standard}: every dollar backed by gold, and there wasn't much gold. The Populists wanted silver coined too, to put more money in people's pockets.

Who wins? Uncle Owen: more money means higher crop prices, and an easier mortgage. Michael: higher prices mean dearer bread, and his pay might not keep up.

In July 1896, in Chicago, a 36-year-old Nebraskan named William Jennings Bryan gave the speech of his life. He finished with his arms stretched out like a cross:

"You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns. You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."

The Democrats nominated him. Then the Populists did too, with a different running mate. So Bryan ran with two vice presidents at once.

OWEN: [fired up] Bryan's a Nebraska man. He's one of us.

Bryan traveled eighteen thousand miles. William McKinley stayed home in Ohio and let voters come to his front porch, while his campaign raised a fortune from big business. Some employers warned that if Bryan won, the factory might close.

At the Kellys' table, it was 1886 all over again. Except the brothers had switched sides.

TOMMY: [fired up] Bryan's for the working man! Even Mr. Duffy says so.

MICHAEL: [worried] The foreman says if Bryan wins, the refinery closes. And silver means dearer bread.

NORA: [amused] You should hear them on Fifth Avenue. They think Bryan's coming for the silverware.

MA: [firm] Your uncle's losing his farm, and you're arguing about metal.

McKinley won. The cities and most factory workers went with him. Bryan swept the farm country, and it wasn't enough.

So the Populists lost. Was their fight gold, or just gilt?

## 11. Gold or Gilt

Gold or gilt?

The People's Party soon faded. Then new gold strikes, in Canada's Klondike and in South Africa, put more money in circulation anyway, and the silver fight faded too.

But here's the twist. Remember those crazy ideas from Omaha?

An income tax: the 16th Amendment, 1913. Senators elected by voters: the 17th, also 1913. The secret ballot: nearly everywhere within a few years. The eight-hour day: for railroad workers in 1916, most workers by 1938. Collective bargaining: protected by law in 1935.

And in 1944, at 90, Jacob Coxey finally stood on the Capitol steps and gave his speech. Fifty years late.

The Populists lost the election, but they won a lot of the argument. After 1900, a new wave of reformers took up their ideas, in what's called the {Progressive Era}.

New Year's Day, 1898. Brooklyn became part of New York City. Michael and Tommy walked across the bridge together, voters in the same city at last. They still wouldn't vote the same way.

Upstairs, Rivke Rabinowitz was sewing shirtwaists in a factory now. And she'd started going to meetings. About a union.

The Kellys never got the gold. Most people didn't. But a shorter workday, a fair vote, a tax on the richest: none of that was gilt. Ordinary people fought for it, for decades.

That's the end of our story of the Gilded Age. Thanks for watching, and for sticking with the Kellys all the way.
