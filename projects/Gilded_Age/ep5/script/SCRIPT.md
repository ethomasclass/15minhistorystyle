## 1. Barges at Dawn

July 6th, 1892. Four o'clock in the morning. The Monongahela River, near Pittsburgh.

Fog on the water. Two covered barges slide upstream behind a tugboat, no lights showing.

A lookout spots them. The mill whistle screams, and the whole town of Homestead runs down to the river. Steelworkers. Their families. They tear down the company's fence and pack the landing. Some carry lanterns. Some carry rifles.

Inside the barges are about three hundred men with Winchester rifles. Not soldiers. Not police. Private guards, hired by the biggest steel company in America to take its own mill back from its own workers.

The gangplank goes down.

On the barges, rifles come up. On the bank, rifles come up too.

A company's private army, aiming at American workers, in an American town. And the workers, aiming right back.

Nobody knows who will fire first.

How did it come to this?

This is the story of two groups who felt left behind in the Gilded Age: workers in the cities, and farmers out west. Both decided to fight back. To understand why, we have to start with a job. Twelve hours a day.

## 2. Twelve Hours a Day

Twelve hours a day, six days a week. That's how long Michael Kelly worked at a sugar refinery in Brooklyn. It's January 1887, and Michael is 23. He hauls heavy sacks and tends vats of boiling sugar, in heat that could make a man collapse.

Michael's job was normal for an American factory worker in the Gilded Age. Here's what normal looked like.

Long hours. Ten to twelve hours a day was common. Steelworkers often worked twelve-hour shifts, seven days a week.

Low pay, and no say. The company decided your wage. If it wanted to cut your pay, it just did. There was no minimum wage.

Danger. Machines had no safety guards. The railroads alone killed thousands of their own workers every year. If you got hurt on the job, the company owed you nothing. No pay while you healed. Maybe no job when you came back.

Children. By 1900, about one in six American children between ten and fifteen had a job, many in mills, mines and factories.

No safety net. Lose your job, and there was no unemployment check. Nothing.

So why didn't workers just quit, or complain? Because someone was always waiting to take their place. Every year, hundreds of thousands of new immigrants arrived looking for work. If Michael complained, the foreman could point at the gate, where a line of men wanted his job.

And that January, the refinery cut the men's pay. Again.

One worker against a giant company had no chance. But what about all the workers, together? There was strength in numbers.

## 3. Strength in Numbers

Strength in numbers. That's the whole idea of a union.

A {union} is a group of workers who join together to deal with their employer as one. One worker who asks for a raise can be fired. A thousand workers asking at the same time are much harder to ignore.

When a union sits down with a company to agree on pay, hours and safety for everyone, that's called {collective bargaining}. Collective means together. Bargaining means negotiating.

And if the company says no? The union's biggest weapon is a {strike}: everyone stops working at once. No workers means no product, and no profit.

But companies had weapons too. They could fire union members and put their names on a {blacklist}, so no other company would hire them. They could lock the gates and keep everyone out until the workers gave in. That's a {lockout}. They could bring in {strikebreakers}, new workers hired to replace the strikers. And they could call on the courts, private guards, and even soldiers.

America saw what that looked like in 1877. When the railroads cut wages, railroad workers walked off the job in city after city. It was the first strike to spread across the whole country. Riots broke out, the President sent in the army, and about a hundred people died. The strike failed.

Ten years later, on a winter night, Michael Kelly climbed the stairs to a back room above a saloon. It was a meeting of the biggest union in America. A union that had just been through a fight over eight hours. And a bomb.

## 4. Eight Hours and a Bomb

The union was the {Knights of Labor}. For years, the Knights were so secret that newspapers printed their name as five stars. By 1886, they had hundreds of thousands of members. They let almost anyone join: skilled and unskilled workers, women, and Black workers. But not Chinese immigrants. Even the Knights kept one door shut.

Their big goal was an eight-hour workday. On May 1st, 1886, hundreds of thousands of workers across the country went on strike for it. Their slogan: eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will.

Three days later, at a protest in Chicago's Haymarket Square, police moved in to break it up. Someone threw a bomb. Seven police officers and at least four other people died in the explosion and the shooting that followed. To this day, nobody knows who threw it.

Eight {anarchists}, people who wanted no government at all, were convicted anyway, with little evidence tying them to the bomb. Four were hanged.

For many Americans, unions now meant violence. The Knights had nothing to do with the bomb, but they got the blame, and they started falling apart.

Another union survived: the {American Federation of Labor}, or AFL, led by Samuel Gompers. It only took skilled workers, like carpenters and cigar makers, and it kept its goals simple: better pay and shorter hours. When people asked Gompers what workers wanted, his answer came down to one word. More.

Michael joined the Knights anyway. His little brother was not happy.

TOMMY: [annoyed] A union? Mr. Duffy says the Knights are nothing but trouble.

MICHAEL: [dry] Mr. Duffy doesn't work at the refinery.

Tommy ran errands for the local political machine, and the machine didn't like anything it didn't control. The brothers had already fought about it once, a few months earlier, over a man across the river.

## 5. The Man Across the River

The man across the river was Henry George, in New York City. He'd written a famous book asking a simple question: why does poverty keep growing, even as the country gets richer?

In November 1886, New York's unions decided that if the bosses wouldn't listen, maybe voters would. They ran Henry George for mayor.

Brooklyn was still a separate city, so Michael couldn't vote for him. But he walked across the Brooklyn Bridge to the torchlight rallies anyway. Tommy's machine, and Tammany Hall, worked hard to beat him.

George lost. But he came in second, ahead of a young Republican named Theodore Roosevelt. Roosevelt would be fine.

TOMMY: [teasing] Your man lost.

MICHAEL: [stubborn] He came close. Closer than anybody thought.

Working people had shown they could scare the powerful. Six years later, the powerful showed what they could do. At a steel mill the workers called Fort Frick.

## 6. Fort Frick

Fort Frick was the workers' nickname for the steel mill in Homestead, Pennsylvania. Remember those barges?

The mill belonged to Andrew Carnegie, one of the richest men in the world. To run his company, Carnegie relied on a tough manager named Henry Clay Frick. Remember that order. Carnegie owned the mill. Frick ran it for him.

In 1892, the steelworkers' union and the company were bargaining over a new contract. The company offered lower pay. When the union said no, Frick refused to deal with the union at all. He put up a tall fence around the mill, topped with barbed wire. The workers called it Fort Frick. Then he locked them out.

To get the mill running with new workers, Frick hired about three hundred guards from the Pinkerton Detective Agency, a private company that businesses hired to protect property and break strikes. Those were the men on the barges.

On July 6th, the shooting started at the landing, and it went on most of the day. At least ten people were killed, workers and guards. That afternoon, the Pinkertons surrendered.

But the workers didn't win. The governor sent eight thousand state soldiers, and the mill reopened with strikebreakers. A few weeks later, an anarchist who had nothing to do with the union shot and stabbed Frick. Frick survived, and was back at work in about a week. Public opinion turned against the strikers, and the union was crushed.

And Andrew Carnegie? He spent the strike on vacation in Scotland, sending Frick his support.

Here's what makes it complicated. Six years earlier, Carnegie had written that workers had a right to form unions, a right he called "no less sacred" than the rights of owners. He was also the man who gave away most of his fortune and built thousands of free libraries. One steelworker asked, "After working twelve hours, how can a man go to a library?"

So, Andrew Carnegie. Hero? Villain? Or both?

For workers, things were about to get even worse. Hard times were coming.

## 7. Hard Times

Hard times hit in 1893. Banks failed. Railroads went bankrupt. Thousands of businesses closed. By 1894, more than one American worker in ten was out of a job. It was called a {depression}, the worst the country had ever seen.

The refinery let Michael go. Ma took in washing. Nora's pay as a maid kept the family fed.

In Ohio, a businessman named Jacob Coxey had an idea: the government should hire jobless men to build roads. To ask Congress in person, he led a march of unemployed men to Washington. They were called Coxey's Army. Coxey's daughter rode a white horse, dressed as the Goddess of Peace. And Coxey had named his baby son Legal Tender. That's a term for official money. Coxey really cared about money.

On May 1st, 1894, about five hundred marchers reached the Capitol. Coxey climbed the steps to speak, and police arrested him. The charges: carrying banners. And walking on the grass.

That same spring, near Chicago, the Pullman train car company cut its workers' pay by about a quarter. But its workers lived in a {company town}. Pullman owned their houses. And Pullman did not cut the rent.

The workers went on strike. A national railroad union, led by Eugene Debs, backed them by refusing to run any train that pulled a Pullman car. Trains stopped across much of the country.

Then the government stepped in, on the companies' side. A judge issued an {injunction}, a court order banning the strike. President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago. About thirty people were killed. Debs went to jail, and the strike collapsed.

After Homestead and Pullman, the lesson seemed clear: when workers took on big companies, the government usually sided with the companies.

Workers weren't the only ones who felt that way. Let's leave the city, go back a few years, and head out west, where farmers were struggling too. You can hear it in Uncle Owen's letters.

## 8. Uncle Owen's Letters

Uncle Owen was Michael's uncle, his father's younger brother. He farmed 160 acres near O'Neill, Nebraska, out on the Great Plains. Every few months, a letter came to Brooklyn.

OWEN: [warm, weary] Wheat's selling for half what it did when I came out. Half. And the bank still wants its payment.

Farmers in the Gilded Age had three big problems.

First, debt. To buy land, seed and machines, most farmers borrowed from banks. Many took out a {mortgage}: a loan where, if you can't pay it back, the bank takes your farm.

Second, the railroads. A farmer's crop was worthless unless he could ship it to the city. Often only one railroad served his town, so it could charge whatever it wanted.

Third, and worst of all: crop prices kept falling. Wheat that sold for about a dollar a bushel in the early 1880s dropped to around fifty cents in the 1890s. Corn got so cheap that some farmers burned it in their stoves instead of coal.

OWEN: [dry] Burned corn in the stove this winter. Cheaper than coal.

So farmers did what workers did. They organized. First came the {Grange}, farmers' clubs that pushed states to control railroad prices. It was started by a man named Oliver Kelley. No relation. Then came the much bigger {Farmers' Alliance}.

But to understand why farmers were so angry, you need to understand one thing: the money problem.

## 9. The Money Problem

The money problem was this: the harder farmers worked, the deeper they sank into debt. Here's how.

Say Uncle Owen borrows a thousand dollars when wheat sells for a dollar a bushel. To pay back that loan, he needs to sell a thousand bushels of wheat.

Ten years later, wheat sells for fifty cents. He still owes a thousand dollars. But now he has to sell two thousand bushels to pay it. Same debt. Twice the work.

So why did prices keep falling? Part of the answer was money itself.

The United States was on the {gold standard}. Every dollar was supposed to be backed by gold in a government vault. So the country could only have as many dollars as it had gold.

But America was growing fast. More people, more farms, more factories, more things to buy and sell. And the amount of money barely grew.

Picture a town with a hundred dollars and a hundred loaves of bread. Each loaf sells for a dollar. Now the town bakes two hundred loaves, but there are still only a hundred dollars. Each loaf now sells for fifty cents. More stuff, same money, so prices fall. That's called {deflation}.

Cheaper bread sounds great if you're buying. But if you're the one selling the wheat, and you owe the bank, it's a disaster.

Farmers had a solution: silver. If the government made dollars out of silver as well as gold, there would be more money going around. More money, and prices go back up. Wheat climbs back toward a dollar. Owen's debt gets easier to pay. That idea was called {free silver}.

Not everyone liked it. Bankers wanted their loans paid back in dollars worth just as much as before. And many city workers, like Michael, worried that if prices went up, so would the cost of bread and rent, and their pay might not keep up.

Farmers needed Washington on their side. But Washington wasn't listening. So farmers decided to build a party of their own.

## 10. A Party of Their Own

A party of their own. Why? Because for years, both big parties seemed more interested in something else: jobs.

In the Gilded Age, the party that won an election handed out government jobs to its supporters. That was called the {spoils system}.

In 1881, it turned deadly. A man named Charles Guiteau decided President James Garfield owed him a government job for helping his campaign. When he didn't get one, he shot the president in a Washington train station. Garfield died eleven weeks later, partly because his doctors kept probing the wound with unwashed hands.

Congress responded with the {Pendleton Act}, which said some government jobs had to go to people who passed a test. That's called {civil service}. It was a real reform. But it fixed who got government jobs. It didn't fix railroad prices, falling crop prices or low wages.

So on July 4th, 1892, in Omaha, Nebraska, farmers and workers formed a new party: the People's Party, also called the Populists. {Populism} means ordinary people uniting against powerful elites, like big banks, railroads and corporations.

They said America was producing two kinds of people: tramps, meaning homeless wanderers, and millionaires.

Their plan, the {Omaha Platform}, demanded free silver. An income tax, so the richest paid the most. Senators elected directly by the people. Government control of the railroads. And an eight-hour workday.

OWEN: [proud] We've got our own party now. Farmers and working men, together.

That November, the Populist candidate won over a million votes. But the biggest fight came four years later, in 1896, when the whole election came down to silver, and a cross of gold.

## 11. The Cross of Gold

The cross of gold came from a speech.

In July 1896, a 36-year-old Nebraskan named William Jennings Bryan stood up at the Democratic convention in Chicago and argued for free silver. He said the gold standard was crushing working people. And he finished with his arms spread wide, like a cross:

"You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns. You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."

In plain words: don't sacrifice ordinary people to protect gold.

The crowd went wild. The Democrats made Bryan their candidate. Then the Populists chose him too.

OWEN: [fired up] Bryan's a Nebraska man. He's one of us.

Bryan traveled eighteen thousand miles and gave hundreds of speeches. His opponent, Republican William McKinley, stayed home in Ohio and spoke to visitors from his front porch, while his campaign raised millions of dollars from big business. Some factory owners warned their workers that if Bryan won, the factory might close.

At the Kellys' kitchen table, the family split. And this time, the brothers had switched sides.

TOMMY: [fired up] Bryan's for the working man! Even Mr. Duffy says so.

MICHAEL: [worried] The foreman says if Bryan wins, the refinery closes. And silver means higher prices for bread.

NORA: [amused] You should hear them on Fifth Avenue. They think Bryan's coming for the silverware.

MA: [firm] Your uncle's losing his farm, and you're arguing about metal.

McKinley won. Most city voters, including many factory workers, chose him. Bryan won the farm states of the South and West, and it wasn't enough. The Populists soon faded away.

So after all that fighting, what did workers and farmers have to show for it? Was it gold, or just gilt?

## 12. Gold or Gilt

Gold or gilt? By 1900, it looked a lot like gilt.

Workers still had no legal right to form a union. Most still worked ten or twelve hours a day. Children still worked in mills and mines. Farmers were still in debt. A handful of giant companies still controlled whole industries. And the government still usually took the side of business.

The strikers at Homestead and Pullman lost. The Populists lost. But they put their ideas in front of the whole country: shorter workdays, fair pay, the right to organize, a fairer tax, and a government that answered to ordinary people.

The Gilded Age left Americans wanting more. In the years after 1900, many of them would start demanding it, in a new age of reform called the Progressive Era.

New Year's Day, 1898. Brooklyn became part of New York City. Michael and Tommy walked across the bridge together, voters in the same city at last. They still didn't vote the same way.

Upstairs, Rivke Rabinowitz was sewing shirts in a factory now. And she had started going to meetings. About a union.

The Kellys never got the gold. But they hadn't stopped fighting for their share.

That's the end of our story of the Gilded Age. Thanks for watching, and for sticking with the Kellys all the way.
