## 1. Barges at Dawn

July 6th, 1892. Four o'clock in the morning. The Monongahela River, just outside Pittsburgh.

Fog on the water. Two long, covered barges slide upstream behind a tugboat, with no lights showing.

On the riverbank, a lookout spots them. The mill whistle screams, and the whole town of Homestead comes running down to the water. Steelworkers. Their wives. Their kids. Some carry lanterns. Some carry rifles.

Inside the barges are about three hundred armed men. They aren't soldiers, and they aren't police. They work for a private detective agency, hired by the biggest steel company in America to take control of its own mill, away from its own workers.

The gangplank goes down.

How did America get here? To a place where a company could send its own army up a river?

And what could ordinary people do about it? When workers and farmers stuck together, what did they win? And what did it cost them?

To find out, we have to go back five years, to Brooklyn. And a secret meeting.

## 2. The Secret Meeting

The secret meeting was in a back room above a saloon in Williamsburg. January, 1887.

Michael Kelly was there because of a pay cut. The sugar refinery where he worked had just cut the men's pay. Again.

He worked twelve hours a day, six days a week, beside vats of boiling sugar. If a man got burned, there was no pay while he healed. If he complained, a hundred men outside the gate would take his job.

One worker against one company? The company wins every time.

But what if all the workers spoke at once?

That's a {union}: workers joining together to deal with the boss as a group. And when they negotiate together, for pay, hours and safer conditions, that's called {collective bargaining}.

The biggest union in America was the {Knights of Labor}. For years, they were so secretive that newspapers printed their name as five stars.

By 1887 they were out in the open, and huge. The Knights let almost anyone join: skilled and unskilled workers, women, and Black workers too. But not Chinese workers. Even the Knights kept one door shut.

Workers had tried fighting back before. In 1877, the railroads cut wages, and railroad men walked off the job in city after city. It was the first {strike} to spread across the whole country. The President sent in the army. About a hundred people died. [VERIFY]

That night in the back room, Michael raised his hand.

Somebody in the family wasn't happy about it.

TOMMY: [annoyed] A union? Mr. Duffy says the Knights are nothing but trouble.

MICHAEL: [dry] Mr. Duffy doesn't work at the refinery.

Tommy ran errands for the political machine. And the machine didn't like any organization it didn't control.

The brothers had been fighting since the fall. But that story starts the spring before, with a fight that became famous for two things. Eight hours, and a bomb.

## 3. Eight Hours and a Bomb

Eight hours. That was the dream.

On May 1st, 1886, hundreds of thousands of workers across America walked off the job for an eight-hour day. Their slogan: eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will.

Three days later, at a rally in Chicago's Haymarket Square, police moved in to break it up. Someone threw a bomb.

Seven police officers died, and at least four other people, from the bomb and the shooting that followed. Nobody ever proved who threw it.

Eight men were convicted anyway. They were {anarchists}, people who wanted no government at all, and the evidence against most of them was thin. Four were hanged.

After Haymarket, many Americans saw unions as dangerous. The Knights took the blame, though they had nothing to do with the bomb. Within a few years, they were fading fast.

A different kind of union lasted: the {American Federation of Labor}, led by Samuel Gompers. It organized skilled workers, like carpenters and cigar makers, and bargained for bread-and-butter things: pay and hours. When people asked Gompers what labor wanted, his answer boiled down to one word. More.

But that fall, a lot of working people decided there was another way to win. Not a strike. A vote. And the man they voted for lived across the river.

## 4. The Man Across the River

The man across the river was Henry George.

He'd written a bestseller called *Progress and Poverty*. It asked a simple question: why, as America got richer, did so many people stay poor? His answer was one big tax on land, and no other taxes. People called it the {single tax}.

In 1886, New York's unions asked George to run for mayor. Not for one of the big parties. For a brand-new labor party.

Brooklyn was a separate city back then, so Michael couldn't vote for him. He walked across the Brooklyn Bridge to the torchlight rallies anyway, with thousands of others.

Tommy's machine worked to beat him. So did Tammany Hall.

On election day, Henry George came in second. Ahead of a 28-year-old named Theodore Roosevelt, who came in third. Roosevelt would recover.

George's supporters believed Tammany had counted him out. Nobody ever proved it.

TOMMY: [teasing] Your man lost.

MICHAEL: [stubborn] He came close. Closer than anybody thought.

Out west, someone else in the family was asking Henry George's question. Why were people working harder and getting poorer? He put it all down on paper, in Uncle Owen's letters.

## 5. Uncle Owen's Letters

Uncle Owen's letters came from Nebraska.

He was Michael's father's younger brother. Years before, he'd gone west to an Irish farm colony on the Great Plains: O'Neill, Nebraska. Every few months, a letter arrived in Brooklyn.

OWEN: [warm, weary] Wheat's selling for half what it did when I came out. Half. And the bank still wants its ten percent.

Here's the squeeze farmers were in. To buy land and machines, they borrowed. That's a {mortgage}: a loan, with the farm as the promise. But crop prices kept falling. Wheat that sold for about a dollar a bushel dropped to around fifty cents. [VERIFY] A farmer had to grow twice as much to pay the same debt.

Then there was the railroad. In a lot of places, one railroad was the only way to get a crop to market. And it charged whatever it liked.

OWEN: [dry] Burned corn in the stove this winter. Cheaper than coal.

That really happened. Some years, corn was so cheap that farmers in Kansas and Nebraska burned it to stay warm.

Underneath it all, there wasn't enough money going around. Dollars got scarcer and harder to earn. That's called {deflation}. It's great if you lend money. It's terrible if you owe it.

So farmers organized too. First came the {Grange}, farm clubs that pushed states to regulate the railroads. It was founded by a man named Oliver Kelley. No relation. Then came the bigger {Farmers' Alliance}, which set up co-ops so farmers could buy and sell together.

But the biggest problems needed Washington. And that was Washington's problem.

## 6. Washington's Problem

Washington's problem was jobs. Government jobs.

For decades, whoever won the White House handed out thousands of jobs to the people who'd helped them win. It was called the {spoils system}, as in, "to the winner go the spoils." Every new president faced a line of job seekers out the door.

One of them was a man named Charles Guiteau. He'd given a speech or two for James Garfield in 1880, and decided he deserved a job. Ideally in Paris. He didn't get one.

On July 2nd, 1881, Guiteau shot President Garfield in a Washington train station. Witnesses reported that he shouted, "I am a Stalwart of the Stalwarts. Arthur is President now!"

Garfield lived for eleven weeks. His doctors probed the wound with unwashed fingers, and the infection probably did as much harm as the bullet. He died that September.

Two years later, Congress passed the {Pendleton Act}. Some government jobs now required passing a test, not knowing the right people. That's called {civil service}. Soon even Brooklyn's city clerks were taking exams. Errand boys, luckily for Tommy, were not.

But fixing who got the jobs didn't fix what farmers and workers needed: fair railroad rates, more money, better wages. Congress did pass a railroad law in 1887 and a law against monopolies in 1890. Neither had much bite. The Supreme Court later decided that the Sugar Trust, which controlled about 98 percent of America's sugar refining, wasn't covered at all. Michael's refinery was part of it.

So farmers decided that if neither party would listen, they'd start their own. And they'd tell America the truth: it was raising tramps and millionaires.

## 7. Tramps and Millionaires

Tramps and millionaires. That's how the new party put it, on July 4th, 1892, in Omaha, Nebraska.

Farmers and workers formed the People's Party, also called the Populists. {Populism} means ordinary people organizing against powerful elites. Here, that meant banks, railroads and big business.

Their platform opened with this: "From the same prolific womb of governmental injustice we breed the two great classes: tramps and millionaires."

The {Omaha Platform} listed what they wanted. {Free silver}: coining silver money without limit, to put more dollars in circulation. An income tax, so the richest paid the most. Senators elected directly by the voters. The government running the railroads. A secret ballot. And an eight-hour day.

Plenty of people called those ideas crazy. Remember them.

OWEN: [proud] We've got our own party now. Farmers and working men, together.

That November, the Populist candidate, James Weaver, won over a million votes and carried four states.

But two days after Omaha, the working men back east were in trouble. Because the barges reached Homestead. And the steel mill the workers called Fort Frick.

## 8. Fort Frick

Fort Frick. That's what the workers called the mill at Homestead, after the company built a fence around it miles long, topped with barbed wire, with holes cut for rifles. [VERIFY]

The man behind it was Henry Clay Frick. And here's the part people forget: Frick worked for Andrew Carnegie. Carnegie owned most of the company. Frick ran it. It was Carnegie's company, and Carnegie's mill.

The fight started as collective bargaining. The steelworkers' union and the company were negotiating a new contract. The company offered lower pay, then refused to deal with the union at all. When the contract ran out, Frick shut the workers out of the mill. That's a {lockout}: the owner closes the doors until the workers give in.

Then he hired about three hundred men from the Pinkerton Detective Agency to take the mill, so new workers could run it. Those were the men on the barges.

On July 6th, the shooting at the landing went on for most of the day. At least ten people were killed, workers and Pinkertons. That afternoon, the Pinkertons surrendered. A week later, the governor sent about eight thousand state troops, and the mill reopened with {strikebreakers}, workers hired to replace the strikers. The strikers had a harsher word for them: scabs.

That same month, an anarchist with no connection to the union burst into Frick's office, shot him twice and stabbed him. Frick was back at his desk in about a week. Public sympathy swung against the strikers, and the union at Homestead was broken.

And Andrew Carnegie? He was on vacation in Scotland. He sent Frick messages of support. [VERIFY]

Six years earlier, Carnegie had written that the right of working men to form unions was "no less sacred" than the right of owners to organize.

This was the same man who gave away almost all of his fortune and built thousands of public libraries. One steelworker asked, "After working twelve hours, how can a man go to a library?" [VERIFY]

So, Andrew Carnegie. Hero? Villain? Or both?

Michael read about Homestead on the front stoop. He had no idea how bad things were about to get. Because America was heading into hard times.

## 9. Hard Times

Hard times hit in 1893. Banks failed. Railroads went bankrupt. Thousands of businesses closed. By 1894, more than one American worker in ten had no job. It started as a {panic}, and it became the worst depression the country had ever seen.

The refinery let Michael go. Ma took in washing. Nora's wages from Fifth Avenue kept the family afloat. And Tommy's club handed out coal again.

In Ohio, a businessman named Jacob Coxey had an idea. If the government hired jobless men to build roads, everybody would win. So he'd march to Washington and ask Congress in person. He called it a petition in boots.

His partner, Carl Browne, dressed like a Wild West showman. [VERIFY] Coxey's daughter rode a white horse, dressed as the Goddess of Peace. [VERIFY] And Coxey had named his youngest son Legal Tender.

On May 1st, 1894, about five hundred men reached the Capitol. Coxey climbed the steps to give his speech, and police arrested him. The charges: carrying banners on the Capitol grounds. And walking on the grass.

Out west, other jobless "armies" didn't bother walking. In Montana, one group stole an entire train. A young Jack London rode along with another one, called Kelley's Army. No relation. Again.

That same spring, near Chicago, the Pullman railroad car company cut its workers' wages by about a quarter. But the workers lived in Pullman's {company town}, and Pullman didn't cut the rent. The company said there was nothing to talk about.

Eugene Debs's railway union refused to handle any train carrying Pullman cars. Trains stopped across much of the country.

The railroads went to court, and a judge issued an {injunction}: an order to stop the strike. President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago, over the protests of the governor of Illinois. About thirty people were killed. Debs went to jail, and thousands of strikers ended up on a {blacklist}, a list of union men nobody would hire.

Strikes had failed. Marches had failed. That left the vote. And in 1896, it all came down to a speech about a cross of gold.

## 10. The Cross of Gold

The cross of gold was about money. Real money.

America was on the {gold standard}: every dollar was backed by gold, and there wasn't much gold to go around. The Populists wanted free silver: coin silver too, and put more money in people's pockets.

Who wins? Think about Uncle Owen. With more money around, crop prices go up, and his mortgage gets easier to pay. Now think about Michael. If prices go up, bread costs more, and his pay may not keep up.

In July 1896, at the Democratic convention in Chicago, a 36-year-old former congressman from Nebraska named William Jennings Bryan gave the speech of his life. He finished with his arms stretched out, like a cross:

"You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns. You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."

The hall went wild. The Democrats nominated him. Then the Populists nominated him too, with a different running mate. So Bryan ran with two vice presidents at once.

OWEN: [fired up] Bryan's a Nebraska man. He's one of us. Tell the boys.

Bryan traveled eighteen thousand miles and gave hundreds of speeches. His opponent, William McKinley, stayed home in Ohio and let voters come to his front porch, while his campaign manager raised a fortune from big business. Some employers warned their workers that if Bryan won, the factory might close.

At the Kellys' kitchen table, it was 1886 all over again. Except the brothers had switched sides.

TOMMY: [fired up] Bryan's for the working man! Even Mr. Duffy says so.

MICHAEL: [worried] The foreman says if Bryan wins, the refinery closes. And silver means dearer bread.

NORA: [amused] You should hear them on Fifth Avenue. They think Bryan's coming for the silverware.

MA: [firm] Your uncle's losing his farm, and you're arguing about metal.

In November, McKinley won. The cities and most factory workers went with him. Bryan swept the farm country of the South and West, and it wasn't enough.

So the Populists lost. But was it the end? Was their fight gold, or just gilt?

## 11. Gold or Gilt

Gold or gilt?

The People's Party faded within a few years. Then, by bad luck for them, new gold strikes, in Canada's Klondike and in South Africa, put more money into circulation anyway. Prices rose, and the silver fight just faded away.

But here's the twist. Remember that list of crazy ideas from Omaha?

An income tax: the 16th Amendment, 1913. Senators elected by the voters: the 17th Amendment, also 1913. The secret ballot: the law almost everywhere within a few years. The eight-hour day: law for railroad workers in 1916, and for most workers by 1938. And collective bargaining: protected by federal law in 1935.

And Jacob Coxey? In 1944, at the age of 90, he finally stood on the Capitol steps and gave the speech he'd been arrested for. Fifty years late.

The Populists lost the election. They won a lot of the argument. After 1900, a new wave of reformers took up many of their ideas, in a time called the {Progressive Era}.

New Year's Day, 1898. Brooklyn became part of New York City. Michael and Tommy walked across the bridge together. For the first time, they'd vote in the same city. They still wouldn't vote the same way.

Upstairs, Rivke Rabinowitz was sewing shirtwaists in a factory now. And she'd started going to meetings. About a union.

The Kellys never got the gold. Most people didn't. But a shorter workday, a fair vote, a tax on the richest: none of that was gilt. Ordinary people fought for it, and it took decades.

That's the end of our story of the Gilded Age. Thanks for watching, and for sticking with the Kellys all the way. Subscribe for more history in bite-size chunks.
