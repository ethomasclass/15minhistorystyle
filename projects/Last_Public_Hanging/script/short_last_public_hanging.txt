Sunrise. August 14th, 1936. Owensboro, Kentucky. About 20,000 people are standing in an empty lot, waiting to watch a man be hanged.

It's usually called *the last public execution in America*.

That June, a 70-year-old woman named Lischia Edwards had been attacked and killed in her home. A young man named Rainey Bethea confessed. He was Black. She was white.

Under Kentucky law, murder meant the electric chair, inside prison walls. But one crime, sexual assault, could still mean a *public* hanging, in the county where it happened. So prosecutors charged him with that crime only.

He pleaded guilty. An all-white jury took about four and a half minutes.

Then reporters found their headline. The sheriff was a woman, Florence Thompson, who took the job when her husband died that spring. Would she pull the lever?

She didn't. A volunteer did.

Papers across the country called it a carnival. Some people who were there said the papers exaggerated. Either way, Kentucky was embarrassed. In 1938, it ended public hangings.

Twenty thousand people came to watch. What they really saw was the end of watching.
