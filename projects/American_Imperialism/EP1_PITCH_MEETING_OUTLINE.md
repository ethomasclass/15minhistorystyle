## The hook: a queen locked in a room

**Cold open (in the world):** ʻIolani Palace, Honolulu, early 1895. An upstairs room. A woman in a dark dress stands at the window
with her back to us. A guard with a rifle stands outside the door. She is Liliʻuokalani, the Queen of Hawaiʻi, and she is a prisoner
in her own palace. The men who put her there were mostly Americans, or the sons of Americans.

**Driving question (by 0:45):**
> How does a country that was born breaking away from a king end up locking a queen in her own palace?

**Bridge (why it matters):** The arguments that got America there are the same four arguments you'll hear in every episode of
this series. Some of them still sound convincing. That's the point.

**Promise:** "We'll come back to this room."

**Answer (the last chapter says it):** Not with one decision. Through a pitch. In the 1880s and 1890s, four kinds of people made
the case for expansion: the businessman, the admiral, the preacher and the "scientist." Each had a different reason, and all
four pointed at the same islands. Americans in Hawaiʻi took the government with U.S. troops standing by. An American president
called it an act of war and could not undo it. Five years later Congress annexed Hawaiʻi anyway, in the middle of a different
war. America had taken a kingdom. It still would not call itself an empire.

**Series question, Ep 1's answer:** *Almost. It took a kingdom, but it wouldn't use the word.* (This replaces "Not yet. But it's
practicing." in `SERIES.md`, because Ep 1 ends with the annexation.)

**Possible titles** (packaging gets its own round before the script locks):

2. **Locked in Her Own Palace** · *How Hawaiʻi Became American* (the stronger click: it is the cold open)

## Chapter by chapter

### 1 · The Locked Room (0:00, 65 words, world)
- First sentence is the hook, not a date: *The Queen of Hawaiʻi is a prisoner in her own palace.*
- The guard at the door, the window, the city below. One line on who holds the key: a new government run mostly by Americans
  and the sons of Americans.
- **Visual:** Flow plate p6 (the Queen's room), a new plate of the corridor with the guard (the code-drawn marcher, standing
  and breathing). The camera starts on the door and drifts to the window.
- **Sound:** room tone, a clock, the guard's boots once.

### 2 · The Question (0:25, 65 words)
- The driving question, the bridge, "We'll come back to this room."
- **Visual:** pull back out through the window, past the palace, and *land on the desk*: the Declaration of Independence on
  the black grid, the camera settling on "consent of the governed." This is the first world-to-desk crossing, and it plants
  the parchment for chapter 7.
- **Source:** Declaration, National Archives transcript (`research/sources/declaration.txt`).

### 3 · The Old Guard (0:50, 150 words)
- **Isolationism**, defined: staying out of other countries' wars and alliances. Jefferson, 1801: "entangling alliances with
  none." Washington's farewell: "steer clear of permanent alliances." For most of the 1800s, America grew by spreading across
  its own continent instead.
- **Alaska, 1867**, in one breath: bought from Russia for $7.2 million, under two cents an acre. Newspapers called it
  "Seward's Folly." The first step off the continent.
- **1890:** the census reports that "there can hardly be said to be a frontier line." The continent is full. *So where next?*
- **Visual:** desk for the documents (Jefferson, the Alaska check, the 1890 census map with the frontier line gone). World for
  the last line: an illustrated wagon road that runs west and stops at a cliff over the Pacific; the camera keeps going out
  over the water.
- **Sources:** `jefferson_1801.txt`, `washington_farewell.txt`, `alaska.txt`, `statedept_alaska-purchase.txt`, `turner_1893.txt`.

### 4 · The Pitch Meeting (1:45, 480 words, about 45 seconds a pitch plus definitions)
**The set:** an illustrated office in 1890s Washington, the "pitch room." Portraits of Washington and Jefferson on the wall (the
old guard, watching). A big wall map of the Pacific. Each pitcher stands in turn; the camera flies through a picture on the
wall into that pitch's world, then hard-cuts back. People move: clerks, a man pacing, a stenographer typing.

- **Opening:** two words defined plainly. **Imperialism**: a strong country taking control of weaker places, by owning them
  outright or by running them from outside. **Interventionism**: stepping into other countries' affairs to protect your own
  interests. The model everyone had in mind was the British Empire.
- **The Businessman (markets).** American farms and factories make more than Americans can buy. Prices fall. New customers
  are overseas. **World:** a dockside warehouse stacked to the roof, wagons still arriving. **Desk:** the crop-price chart from
  the 14.1 deck, redrawn in channel style.
- **The Admiral (Mahan, 1890).** Britain ruled the world because it ruled the sea. A navy needs bases, and steamships need coal
  every few thousand miles: "It is equally vain to acquire distant coaling stations without maintaining a powerful navy." And
  rivalry: the European powers were taking colonies fast, and America did not want to be left out. **World:** a steel cruiser
  at sea, coal smoke, a coaling station on a rock. **Re-hook on the 3:00 dip:** the camera finds the wall map's best harbor in
  the middle of the Pacific. *It belonged to a queen.*
- **The Preacher (Strong, 1885).** Christianity and "Western civilization" as gifts America owed the world. Missionaries had
  been in Hawaiʻi since 1820 (the 1993 Apology Resolution: more than 100 sent by one church board). **World:** a white mission
  church, a congregation filing in.
- **The "Scientist" (Social Darwinism).** Charles Darwin's idea about animals, "survival of the fittest," bent to fit nations
  and races: strong peoples were meant to rule weak ones. It sounded like science. It wasn't. **The loop closes:** the preacher
  and the scientist were often the same man. Strong's own book: "the final competition of races, for which the Anglo-Saxon is
  being schooled." **Desk only** for this pitch (Strong's page, the phrase circled in teal). No illustrated racial imagery.
- **Close:** the old guard's portraits are still on the wall. Somebody takes one down.
- **Sources:** `seanpowerinf00maha.txt`, `statedept_mahan.txt`, `ourcountryitspos00strorich.txt`, `apology_resolution.txt`.

### 5 · The Same Islands (4:45, 40 words, world) · the twist
- Four reasons, one place. Markets: a sugar trade already tied to America. The navy: the best harbor in the Pacific. The
  church: three generations of missionaries. And the "science" told them they had the right.
- **Visual:** the four pitchers turn to the wall map; four pins land on Hawaiʻi in quick hard cuts. The camera pushes into
  the map and through it into the islands (portal move), into the Honolulu harbor plate.

### 6 · The Heist (5:00, 400 words)
- **Sugar (1875):** a trade treaty lets Hawaiian sugar into the U.S. with no tax. Planters, many of them missionaries' sons,
  get rich. **World:** cane fields, workers cutting cane, a mill chimney.
- **The harbor (1887):** the treaty is renewed and the U.S. gets the right to use Pearl Harbor. The same year, armed planters
  force the king to sign a new constitution that strips most of his power and most Native Hawaiians' votes. People called it
  the Bayonet Constitution. **Desk:** the 1887 document.
- **The squeeze (1890):** the McKinley Tariff lets all foreign sugar in tax-free and pays American growers a bonus. Hawaiʻi's
  advantage is gone overnight. The fastest way back in: become part of America.
- **The Queen (1891):** Liliʻuokalani takes the throne and plans a new constitution to give power back. **Desk:** her 1891
  portrait (LoC), coral subject on the teal grid. *(Our hard cut from s7.)*
- **The coup (January 1893):** **Coup**, defined: a small group seizing a government by force. A "Committee of Safety" of
  planters and businessmen moves. Minister Stevens lands sailors and Marines from the USS *Boston*. **World:** the marching
  column past the palace (sample 8, built out). She gives way to avoid bloodshed: "I yield to the superior force of the United
  States of America." Sanford Dole becomes president of the new government. On February 1, Stevens raises the U.S. flag and
  declares Hawaiʻi an American **protectorate** (planted; Ep 4 defines it fully).
- **Both readings, fairly:** the planters said the Queen was the one breaking the law, that they were protecting property and
  American lives, and that if America didn't take the islands, Britain or Japan would. The Queen's side: a lawful government
  was overthrown by foreigners backed by foreign troops.
- **The president (1893):** Cleveland sends an investigator, James Blount. *He read the report and did something no president
  had done.* He told Congress the overthrow was an "act of war, committed with the participation of a diplomatic
  representative of the United States and without authority of Congress," and a "substantial wrong." He asked for the Queen
  to be restored. Dole refused. **Desk:** Cleveland's photo, the message.
- **Annexation**, defined: one country officially adding another place to itself. The annexation treaty stalls in the Senate.
  *A stack of paper will help stop it* (loop to ch 8).
- **Sources:** `apology_resolution.txt`, `nara_newlands.txt`, `nara_petition_lesson.txt`, LoC images, Hawaii_Samples `FACTS.md`.

### 7 · He Has / We Have (7:30, 70 words) · the George III callback
- The parchment from chapter 2 returns. One grievance lights up: "For taking away our Charters, abolishing our most valuable
  Laws, and altering fundamentally the Forms of our Governments." "He has" is struck through; "We have" is written over it in
  teal. Then push through the parchment into the world: the Hawaiian flag lowered at ʻIolani Palace, 1898 (s5's flag match
  cut).
- Same camera move, same sound, every episode, so viewers learn it.

### 8 · Under Protest (8:00, 170 words, desk only) · the quiet chapter
- Slower voice, no jokes, teal-only quiet palette, no illustration.
- **January 1895:** after a failed attempt to restore her, she is arrested, sentenced to "a fine of $5,000, and imprisonment
  at hard labor for five years," and held in the palace. *We're back in the room.* She signs away the throne on January 24,
  1895, as a prisoner. Her own caption for the place: "My palace was my prison."
- **1897:** more than 21,000 Native Hawaiians sign petitions against annexation, 21,269 names on 556 pages for one of them.
  The treaty never gets the two-thirds it needs.
- **Her own words**, from her 1898 book, given room: "Oh, honest Americans, as Christians hear me for my down-trodden people!
  Their form of government is as dear to them as yours is precious to you. Quite as warmly as you love your country, so they
  love theirs."
- **July 7, 1898:** Congress annexes Hawaiʻi anyway, by a simple vote in both houses instead of a treaty, while the country is
  at war with Spain.
- **Visual:** the petition on the light table (sample 3), her portrait, her book's title page, the Newlands Resolution.
- **Sources:** `hawaiisstorybyh00lili.txt`, `nara_petition_lesson.txt`, `nara_newlands.txt`, `apology_resolution.txt`.

### 9 · Back to the Question (9:15, 120 words)
- "So let's go back to the question." The answer, in three sentences (see top).
- **The harbor loop closes:** the harbor the Admiral wanted became a naval base and coaling station. Forty-three years later its
  name would be the most famous in American military history: Pearl Harbor. **World:** the harbor plate, steel ships at anchor,
  the camera pulling back.
- **The Ledger, row 1:** *What America said:* "Entangling alliances with none." · *What America did:* took a kingdom's harbor,
  then the kingdom · *Who answered back:* Liliʻuokalani.
- **Reversal over the opening image:** a hard cut to 1993. A hundred years after the overthrow, Congress passed a resolution
  apologizing to Native Hawaiians for it. **Desk:** Public Law 103-150.
- **Hand-off:** "Hawaiʻi only got annexed because America was suddenly at war. To see how that war started, we need a ship
  that blew up in Havana Harbor." Then the end screen.
