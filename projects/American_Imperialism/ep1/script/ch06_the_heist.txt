Sugar coming into the United States from another country paid a tax at the border, called a {tariff}. In 1875, a trade deal let Hawaiian sugar in with no tariff at all, so it was cheaper than everyone else's. Plantations boomed and their owners got very rich. Many of them were the sons and grandsons of those missionaries.

In 1887 the deal was renewed, and this time the U.S. got something extra: the right to use a harbor called Pearl Harbor. That same year, a group of armed planters forced King Kalākaua, under threat of force, to sign a new constitution that took away most of his power, and its new money and property rules kept many Native Hawaiians from voting. Asian residents were shut out completely. People called it the Bayonet Constitution.

Then, in 1890, Congress passed a new tax law, the McKinley Tariff, and it changed two things. It took the tariff off all foreign sugar, so everyone's sugar got in free, and Hawaiʻi's head start was gone overnight. And it paid sugar growers inside the United States a bonus of two cents a pound. Hawaiian sugar didn't get the bonus, because Hawaiʻi wasn't part of the United States.

So the planters saw a fix. If Hawaiʻi became part of America, their sugar would count as American sugar, and it would get the bonus too.

In 1891, a new ruler took the throne: Liliʻuokalani, Kalākaua's sister. She wanted a new constitution that gave power back to her people. For the planters, that settled it.

In January 1893, they acted, and they gave themselves a name: the Committee of Safety.

A small group taking a government by force is called a {coup}. This one had help.

The help came from the American minister in Honolulu, John Stevens, who ordered sailors and Marines ashore from the warship USS *Boston*. Minister, here, means diplomat, not preacher.

Stepping into another country's affairs to protect your own interests is called {interventionism}.

The Queen would not start a war with the United States, so she stepped aside, in writing, and under protest: "I yield to the superior force of the United States of America."

The new government's president was a lawyer named Sanford Dole. [beat] Yes. That Dole.

On February 1st, Stevens raised the American flag and put Hawaiʻi under America's protection, which is called a {protectorate}.

The planters told it differently. The Queen, they said, was the one breaking the law, and if America didn't take Hawaiʻi, Britain or Japan would.

In Washington, a new president, Grover Cleveland, sent an investigator to find out what happened, and then he told Congress the overthrow was an "act of war, committed with the participation of a diplomatic representative of the United States and without authority of Congress." He called it a "substantial wrong," and he asked for the Queen to be put back on her throne.

For a moment, Washington's answer was no.

Dole refused.

On July 4th, 1894, Dole's government declared itself the Republic of Hawaii.

A few years later his cousin James arrived and started growing pineapples. His company is why the name Dole is on pineapple cans today.

What the planters wanted was for the United States to officially add Hawaiʻi to itself, which is called {annexation}. But their treaty kept stalling in the Senate. [beat] Partly because of a stack of paper.
