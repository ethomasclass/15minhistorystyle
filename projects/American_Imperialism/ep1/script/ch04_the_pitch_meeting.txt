By the 1890s, a lot of powerful Americans thought it was time to stop staying out of it. Their arguments came from businessmen, naval officers, ministers and writers. They made them in books, speeches and newspapers over about thirty years, and nobody ever put them all in one room.

[beat] So let's do it for them. A pitch meeting: four pitchers, one investor, the United States.

What they were pitching has a name. A strong country taking control of weaker places, either by owning them outright or by running them from the outside, is called {imperialism}, and the model everybody had in mind was the biggest empire on earth: Britain's.

So, in this series' words: was American imperialism a betrayal of American ideals, or an expression of them? Every one of these pitches was made in the name of something Americans believed in.

*The Businessman.* America's farms and factories were making more than Americans could buy: more wheat, more cotton, more steel. If you make too much of something, prices fall, so the Businessman's answer was new customers overseas. Anywhere with people and money.

*The Admiral.* Alfred Thayer Mahan. Technically, a navy captain. In 1890 he wrote a book about how Britain got so powerful, and his answer was this: Britain ruled the world because it ruled the sea. But a navy needs more than ships. Steamships burned coal, and they burned through it fast, so you needed places to refuel, scattered across the ocean: bases and coaling stations. Mahan wrote that "it is equally vain to acquire distant coaling stations without maintaining a powerful navy." Translation: the stations are no use without the navy. You need both. At the same time, Britain, France and Germany were grabbing colonies all over the world, and a lot of Americans didn't want to be left out.

Now look at the Pacific. It's enormous, and right in the middle of it sat one of the best harbors anywhere. [beat] It belonged to a queen.

*The Preacher.* Josiah Strong was a Protestant minister, and in 1885 he wrote a bestseller called *Our Country*. His pitch was that America had been given Christianity and civilization, and it owed them to the world. In Hawaiʻi, missionaries had been arriving since 1820.

*The "Scientist."* A British writer named Herbert Spencer took Charles Darwin's ideas about plants and animals and coined a phrase: "survival of the fittest." People started applying it to nations and races, and the idea was that strong peoples were meant to rule weaker ones. It's called {Social Darwinism}. It sounded like science. It wasn't.

The Preacher and the Scientist were often the same man. Josiah Strong's own book looked forward to "the final competition of races, for which the Anglo-Saxon is being schooled."
