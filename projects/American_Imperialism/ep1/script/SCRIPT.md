## 0:00 | The Locked Room

`ch01_the_locked_room.txt` · 58 words · about 21 s

The Queen of Hawaiʻi is a prisoner in her own palace.

It's 1895, and Hawaiʻi is still its own country. There's an armed guard outside her door, and from her window she can see the city she used to rule.

The men who put her here aren't a foreign army. They're mostly Americans. Or the sons of Americans.

## 0:20 | The Question

`ch02_the_question.txt` · 99 words · about 34 s

In 1776, America told the world something new: a government gets its power from "the consent of the governed." [beat] The people have to say yes.

The men who locked this door called their government a republic. Just like America's.

So when America started taking control of other places, was it betraying its own ideals, the things it said it stood for, or living them out? That's what this series is trying to figure out.

It starts with four arguments for going overseas, and some of them still sound convincing.

We'll come back to this room. First, the old rule.

## 0:54 | The Old Guard

`ch03_the_old_guard.txt` · 153 words · about 52 s

For most of the 1800s, America had a simple rule for the rest of the world: stay out of it. Staying out of other countries' wars and alliances has a name, {isolationism}, and Thomas Jefferson put it in his first speech as president, in 1801: "entangling alliances with none."

America did grow, but it grew across its own continent. In 1867 it bought Alaska from Russia for $7.2 million, which works out to less than two cents an acre. A few critics mocked it, and one nickname stuck: "Seward's Folly," after the Secretary of State who made the deal.
> **CUT:** CONTRADICTION. On "Folly," a gold nugget drops onto the desk with a dull thud. 1 s of nothing.

Then people found gold there.

For a century, Americans had talked about the frontier, the edge where their settlements stopped and Native land began. The 1890 census reported that "there can hardly be said to be a frontier line" anymore. As far as the government was concerned, the continent was full.

So where next?

## 1:46 | The Pitch Meeting

`ch04_the_pitch_meeting.txt` · 478 words · about 157 s

By the 1890s, a lot of powerful Americans thought it was time to stop staying out of it. Their arguments came from businessmen, naval officers, ministers and writers. They made them in books, speeches and newspapers over about thirty years, and nobody ever put them all in one room.
> **CUT:** THE ROOM. On "one room," the pitch room (c1_pitch_room), empty: the long table, the chairs, the blank easel. 1 s of room tone.

[beat] So let's do it for them. A pitch meeting: four pitchers, one investor, the United States.
> **CUT:** On "do it for them," the four pitchers are standing in the room. No sound.

What they were pitching has a name. A strong country taking control of weaker places, either by owning them outright or by running them from the outside, is called {imperialism}, and the model everybody had in mind was the biggest empire on earth: Britain's.

So, in this series' words: was American imperialism a betrayal of American ideals, or an expression of them? Every one of these pitches was made in the name of something Americans believed in.

*The Businessman.* America's farms and factories were making more than Americans could buy: more wheat, more cotton, more steel. If you make too much of something, prices fall, so the Businessman's answer was new customers overseas. Anywhere with people and money.

*The Admiral.* Alfred Thayer Mahan. Technically, a navy captain. In 1890 he wrote a book about how Britain got so powerful, and his answer was this: Britain ruled the world because it ruled the sea. But a navy needs more than ships. Steamships burned coal, and they burned through it fast, so you needed places to refuel, scattered across the ocean: bases and coaling stations. Mahan wrote that "it is equally vain to acquire distant coaling stations without maintaining a powerful navy." Translation: the stations are no use without the navy. You need both. At the same time, Britain, France and Germany were grabbing colonies all over the world, and a lot of Americans didn't want to be left out.

Now look at the Pacific. It's enormous, and right in the middle of it sat one of the best harbors anywhere. [beat] It belonged to a queen.

*The Preacher.* Josiah Strong was a Protestant minister, and in 1885 he wrote a bestseller called *Our Country*. His pitch was that America had been given Christianity and civilization, and it owed them to the world. In Hawaiʻi, missionaries, people sent to spread Christianity, had been arriving since 1820.

*The "Scientist."* A British writer named Herbert Spencer took Charles Darwin's ideas about plants and animals and coined a phrase: "survival of the fittest." People started applying it to nations and races, and the idea was that strong peoples were meant to rule weaker ones. It's called {Social Darwinism}. It sounded like science. It wasn't.

The Preacher and the Scientist were often the same man. Josiah Strong's own book looked forward to "the final competition of races, for which the Anglo-Saxon is being schooled." By Anglo-Saxon, he meant "all English-speaking peoples," and he called them a race.

## 4:37 | The Heist

`ch06_the_heist.txt` · 567 words · about 186 s

Sugar coming into the United States from another country paid a tax at the border, called a {tariff}. In 1875, a trade deal let Hawaiian sugar in with no tariff at all, so it was cheaper than everyone else's. Plantations boomed and their owners got very rich. Many of them were the sons and grandsons of those missionaries.

In 1887 the deal was renewed, and this time the U.S. got something extra: the right to use a harbor called Pearl Harbor. That same year, a group of armed planters forced King Kalākaua, under threat of force, to sign a new constitution that took away most of his power, and its new money and property rules kept many Native Hawaiians from voting. Asian residents were shut out completely. People called it the Bayonet Constitution.
> **CUT:** THE LINE, 1 of 3. On "Bayonet Constitution," hard cut to the Declaration, the words "consent of the governed" large and readable. One teal underline draws itself. 1.5 s of room tone.

Then, in 1890, Congress passed a new tax law, the McKinley Tariff, and it changed two things. It took the tariff off all foreign sugar, so everyone's sugar got in free, and Hawaiʻi's head start was gone overnight. And it paid sugar growers inside the United States a bonus of two cents a pound. Hawaiian sugar didn't get the bonus, because Hawaiʻi wasn't part of the United States.

So the planters saw a fix. If Hawaiʻi became part of America, their sugar would count as American sugar, and it would get the bonus too.

In 1891, a new ruler took the throne: Liliʻuokalani, Kalākaua's sister. She wanted a new constitution that gave power back to her people. For the planters, that settled it.

In January 1893, they acted, and they gave themselves a name: the Committee of Safety.
> **CUT:** CONTRADICTION. On "Safety," hard cut to rifles being laid out on a table. Snap zoom. 1.5 s of silence.

A small group taking a government by force is called a {coup}. This one had help.

The help came from the American minister in Honolulu, John Stevens, who ordered sailors and Marines ashore from the warship USS *Boston*. Minister, here, means diplomat, not preacher.
> **CUT:** TRUE CAPTION. Lower third, held a beat longer than needed: JOHN L. STEVENS / U.S. Minister to Hawaiʻi / Not authorized by Congress.

Stepping into another country's affairs to protect your own interests is called {interventionism}.

The Queen would not start a war with the United States, so she stepped aside, in writing, and under protest: "I yield to the superior force of the United States of America."

The new government's president was a lawyer named Sanford Dole. [beat] Yes, Dole. As in the fruit cups.
> **CUT:** THE FRUIT CUP, setup. Dole's portrait on the desk, and a drawn fruit cup sitting beside it (no real logo). Nobody explains it yet.

On February 1st, Stevens raised the American flag and put Hawaiʻi under America's protection, which is called a {protectorate}.
> **CUT:** THE LINE, 2 of 3. On "flag," hard cut to the Declaration, the words "consent of the governed" large and readable. A second teal underline draws itself under the first. 1.5 s.

The planters told it differently. The Queen, they said, was the one breaking the law, and if America didn't take Hawaiʻi, Britain or Japan would.

In Washington, a new president, Grover Cleveland, sent an investigator to find out what happened, and then he told Congress the overthrow was an "act of war, committed with the participation of a diplomatic representative of the United States and without authority of Congress." He called it a "substantial wrong," and he asked for the Queen to be put back on her throne.

For a moment, Washington's answer was no.

Dole refused.

On July 4th, 1894, Dole's government declared itself the Republic of Hawaii.

A few years later his cousin James arrived and started growing pineapples. The company James started is where the name on those fruit cups comes from.
> **CUT:** THE FRUIT CUP, payoff. Back on Dole's portrait and the fruit cup. Hard cut to a period pineapple-can label with DOLE on it. Silence.

What the planters wanted was for the United States to officially add Hawaiʻi to itself, which is called {annexation}. But their treaty kept stalling in the Senate. [beat] Partly because of a stack of paper.

## 7:43 | He Has, We Have

`ch07_he_has_we_have.txt` · 51 words · about 19 s

The Declaration of Independence has a list of complaints against King George the Third. One of them: "For taking away our Charters, abolishing our most valuable Laws, and altering fundamentally the Forms of our Governments."

That was what a king did to America. [beat] In Hawaiʻi, Americans did it to a queen.

## 8:01 | Under Protest

`ch08_under_protest.txt` · 212 words · about 78 s

In January 1895, the Queen's supporters tried to put her back in power, and they failed. She was arrested and brought here, to this room.

Eight days later, as a prisoner, she signed away her throne. Then a military court sentenced her to "a fine of $5,000, and imprisonment at hard labor for five years." She later put it simply: "My palace was my prison."

She was let out on parole about eight months later, and she didn't stop. She wrote. In 1897, Native Hawaiians signed petitions against annexation, and one petition alone gathered 21,269 names, on 556 pages. The treaty never got the votes it needed.

In 1898, she published a book, and she wrote to Americans directly.

"Oh, honest Americans, as Christians hear me for my down-trodden people! Their form of government is as dear to them as yours is precious to you. Quite as warmly as you love your country, so they love theirs."

[pause] On July 7th, 1898, the United States annexed Hawaiʻi anyway. Not with a treaty, which needed two-thirds of the Senate, but with a joint resolution, which only needed a majority in each house. The country was at war with Spain, and suddenly a navy base in the middle of the Pacific looked useful.

## 9:19 | Back to the Question

`ch09_back_to_the_question.txt` · 221 words · about 74 s

So let's go back to the question. Was this a betrayal of American ideals, or an expression of them?

All four pitches spoke the language of American ideals: markets, a strong navy, Christianity, progress. The men who took Hawaiʻi even declared their republic on the Fourth of July.

But the people of Hawaiʻi were never asked. No vote on annexation was ever held there.
> **CUT:** THE LINE, 3 of 3. Hard cut to the Declaration, "consent of the governed" large and readable, two underlines. The teal pen circles it. 2 s of silence.

This series keeps a ledger, one line per episode. What America said: "entangling alliances with none." What it did: took a kingdom's harbor, then the kingdom. Who answered back: Liliʻuokalani, and 21,269 of her people.

The harbor the Admiral wanted became a naval base, and forty-three years later its name would be the most famous in American military history: Pearl Harbor. A base that far from home let the navy reach across the whole Pacific, and that's what people mean when they say a country "projects its power."

In 1993, a hundred years after the overthrow, Congress formally apologized to Native Hawaiians.

So, betrayal or expression? [beat] In Hawaiʻi, the answer is both. Everything was done in the name of American ideals. Except the asking.

Hawaiʻi only got annexed because America was suddenly at war, a war that started, depending on who you ask, with a ship that blew up in Havana, Cuba. Or with a newspaper.
