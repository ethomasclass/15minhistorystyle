### Writing rules (follow in every file)
- **No em dashes.** Ellis does not want them in any written content. Use periods, commas, colons or parentheses.
- Assume the viewer knows nothing. Define every term in plain words the first time it appears, without dumbing it down.
- Lean into the most engaging stories. Episodes should not just restate the class slides; they need an original angle (see Section 3).
- Stay historically honest. Show the imperialists' own arguments fairly and give voice to the people on the receiving end. Let the evidence carry the irony; avoid lecturing.
- Do not invent quotes. Anything in quotation marks must come from a real, checkable source (see Section 6).
- If you write multiple-choice questions (for a classroom companion), arrange choices psychometrically and never make the correct answer the longest option.

## 3. Series-wide creative devices

These were developed in the planning chat and Ellis approved them. They give the series its identity.

### A. The George III callback (Ellis's favorite)
Each episode shows the Declaration of Independence on parchment. One grievance against King George lights up, the words "He has" are struck through, and "We have" is written over them, because the U.S. is now doing the same thing. Do it once per episode so viewers start to expect it.

| Ep | Grievance (exact Declaration text) | U.S. parallel |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "For taking away our Charters, abolishing our most valuable Laws, and altering fundamentally the Forms of our Governments" | Planters overthrow Hawaii's queen and write their own government |
| 2 | "He has kept among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the Consent of our legislatures" | U.S. troops stay in Cuba after the war (pays off in Ep 4 with the Platt Amendment) |
| 3 | "He has plundered our seas, ravaged our Coasts, burnt our towns, and destroyed the lives of our people" | Philippine-American War (Samar, Balangiga) |
| 4 | "He has dissolved Representative Houses repeatedly" | 1917: Marines under Smedley Butler dissolve Haiti's legislature |

**Series bookend:** Ep 1 opens on "deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed." Ep 4 ends on Wilson's Fourteen Points promising self-determination, then cuts back to that same line with no narration.

Grievances can be swapped if a better match turns up during scripting, but keep one per episode.

### B. Cold opens from the future
Each episode opens on a later, dramatic moment, then rewinds to explain how we got there.

### C. The other side of the ledger
Each episode gives real voice to someone on the receiving end of U.S. power, so the story is not only Americans arguing with each other.

### D. Geographic split
Ep 3 is the **Pacific** (Philippines and China); Ep 4 is the **Caribbean and Latin America**. China becomes the reason the Pacific mattered, and Cuba's Platt Amendment lands in Ep 4 as the payoff to the Teller Amendment from Ep 2.

### E. Answering the compelling question
Each episode should end with its own partial answer, and the answers get darker:
- Ep 1: "Not yet... but it's practicing."
- Ep 2: "We're liberators, not an empire." (Then the U.S. ends up holding colonies.)
- Ep 3: "Yes, and some Americans knew it."
- Ep 4: "Yes, just without the name." Then the question goes to the audience (see the Ep 4 finale).

## 5. Episode breakdowns

### Episode 1: The Pitch Meeting (1867-1898)

**Cold open:** December 7, 1941. Why was a U.S. fleet sitting in the middle of the Pacific? Rewind 50 years.

**Spin:** The motivations for imperialism are staged as four characters pitching America on empire:
- **The Businessman:** new markets for overproduction (use the deck's before/after crop prices visual)
- **The Admiral:** Alfred Thayer Mahan, *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890). Great nations need great navies, navies need bases and coaling stations.
- **The Preacher:** spreading Christianity and "Western civilization" (Josiah Strong, *Our Country*, 1885)
- **The "Scientist":** Social Darwinism, "survival of the fittest" applied to nations and races

The "old guard" they are trying to convince is isolationism: "no entangling alliances."

**Hawaii as a heist:** The 1890 McKinley Tariff wipes out Hawaiian sugar's advantage, so American planters want annexation. In 1893 they overthrow Queen Liliʻuokalani with U.S. Marines ashore. Sanford Dole heads the new government (his cousin James later founds Dole pineapple). Cleveland's own investigation (the Blount Report) calls the overthrow illegal and he tries to restore her, but the planters refuse. She is later imprisoned in her own palace. Hawaii is annexed in 1898, with Pearl Harbor as a naval base and refueling station.

**Ledger voice:** Liliʻuokalani. Close on the 1993 U.S. Apology Resolution.

**Must cover (deck 14.1):**
- [ ] Isolationism (1800s: avoid conflict, neutrality) vs. interventionism (1900s: engage other nations to promote U.S. interests)
- [ ] Alaska Purchase, 1867; the "no more land left in North America" idea
- [ ] The British Empire as the model ("We could totally do this!")
- [ ] All four motivations: Social Darwinism, markets, Christianity/Western civilization, naval bases and sea power (Mahan)
- [ ] Hawaii annexation 1898, Pearl Harbor as naval base and refueling station
- [ ] America "projects its power"
- [ ] Seed the theme of resentment from people who wanted independence (pays off in Ep 3)

### Episode 2: Whose War Was It? (1895-1898)

**Cold open:** 1912. The U.S. raises the wreck of the USS *Maine* from Havana Harbor and sinks it again at sea with full honors, and nobody agrees on what blew it up.

**Spin:** Cuba had been fighting Spain for three years before America showed up, and the U.S. still named the war after itself. Cuban general Calixto García was kept out of the Spanish surrender at Santiago. Second angle: media literacy, with the Hearst/Pulitzer newspaper war as the original viral misinformation. Debunk the famous "You furnish the pictures and I'll furnish the war" line (probably apocryphal).

**The *Maine* mystery:** Americans blamed a Spanish mine at the time. Later investigations, notably Admiral Rickover's 1976 study, pointed to an internal explosion, probably a coal bunker fire. Present it as an open, evidence-based question.

**Ledger voice:** José Martí, who already worried in 1895 about U.S. intentions.

**Must cover (deck 14.2):**
- [ ] Cuban revolts: 1868 (failed) and 1895, led by José Martí
- [ ] U.S. investment in Cuban sugar plantations
- [ ] Spain's reconcentration camps; Gen. Valeriano "Butcher" Weyler; about 200,000 dead
- [ ] **Yellow journalism**: definition and examples (Hearst, Pulitzer)
- [ ] Cleveland says no (deck notes his racist reasoning about Cuban self-government); expansionists say yes; McKinley is pushed; TR is eager
- [ ] De Lôme letter (stolen letter calling McKinley weak)
- [ ] USS *Maine* sent to Havana, explodes February 1898
- [ ] **Jingoism**: definition
- [ ] Teller Amendment: U.S. promises not to annex Cuba
- [ ] "Splendid little war": about 5,500 Americans dead but only about 379 in combat; the rest from disease, heat (wool uniforms in the tropics), bad meat, accidents; old Civil War-era weapons
- [ ] Dewey at Manila Bay; war over in under 3 months
- [ ] Results: U.S. gets the Philippines and Guam (Pacific), Puerto Rico and a "free" Cuba (Caribbean)

**Engaging extras:** Rough Riders who had to leave most of their horses in Florida.

**Ending:** The map splits into two oceans, setting up Ep 3 (Pacific) and Ep 4 (Caribbean).

### Episode 3: The Road to China (1898-1902)

**Cold open:** Summer 1900, Beijing. U.S. Marines fight during the 55-day siege of the foreign legations, and many of the American troops sent to the rescue came straight from the war in the Philippines.

**Spin:** Draw the Pacific as stepping stones: Hawaii, Guam, Manila, then China's roughly 400 million potential customers. The Philippines matters because it is the doorstep to China.

**China / Open Door (required, not in decks):**
- European powers and Japan carve China into spheres of influence
- Secretary of State John Hay's Open Door notes (1899, 1900): the U.S., a latecomer with no sphere, asks everyone to share trade equally
- The Boxer Rebellion; the U.S. joins the international force that relieves the siege
- **Ending irony:** America wanted China's markets but not Chinese people. The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) was renewed in 1902.
- **Fun closer:** The U.S. later returned part of its Boxer indemnity to fund Chinese students in America, and that money helped found Tsinghua University (1911).

**The Philippines:**
- McKinley says he prayed over whether to keep the islands
- Kipling's "The White Man's Burden" (1899), written to urge the U.S. to take the Philippines. Public domain, can be quoted at length.
- Mark Twain's "To the Person Sitting in Darkness" (1901) attacks imperialism in both China and the Philippines, so it ties the episode together
- Anti-Imperialist League (Twain, Carnegie, Jane Addams)

**Ledger voices:** Emilio Aguinaldo; Wu Tingfang, China's minister in Washington, who publicly challenged Chinese exclusion.

**Must cover (deck 14.2 + 14.1):**
- [ ] Public pushes McKinley to keep territories as "fruits of empire"
- [ ] Hawaii annexed during this moment for naval strategy (callback to Ep 1)
- [ ] Fear that Germany would take the Philippines if the U.S. didn't
- [ ] Aguinaldo had helped the U.S. defeat Spain; Filipinos outraged
- [ ] Philippine-American War, 1899-1902: guerrilla warfare, "insurrectos" blending into civilians and jungle
- [ ] Casualties (deck figures): about 5,000 U.S., 25,000 Filipino fighters, 200,000 civilians (verify; estimates vary)
- [ ] Concentration camps
- [ ] William Howard Taft as governor of the Philippines (sets him up for Ep 4)
- [ ] "Why was it so brutal?": Gen. Jacob H. Smith, "Kill every one over ten"; the *New York Journal* cartoon (May 5, 1902)
- [ ] Social Darwinism and "The White Man's Burden" as justification
- [ ] Resentment: Filipinos (and Cubans) who sought independence

### Episode 4: America's Backyard (1898-1917)

**Cold open:** 1898. The battleship USS *Oregon* takes about 67 days to sail from San Francisco around South America to reach the war in Cuba. That voyage is the whole argument for a canal.

**Opener:** The Platt Amendment (1901) breaks the Teller promise from Ep 2, the series' "told you so" moment.

**Spin by president:**
- **TR, Big Stick:** Panama. Colombia refuses, so TR backs a Panamanian revolution in 1903 with U.S. warships nearby. A Frenchman, Philippe Bunau-Varilla, signs the canal treaty on Panama's behalf. TR later brags, "I took the Isthmus." Canal opens 1914.
- **Taft, Dollar Diplomacy:** Bankers in place of battleships, until the Marines land in Nicaragua anyway.
- **Wilson, Moral Diplomacy:** The idealist who promised a break from imperialism and still sent troops into Latin America repeatedly. Mexico: refuses to recognize Huerta, Tampico incident, occupation of Veracruz (1914). Pancho Villa raids Columbus, New Mexico (1916); Pershing's Punitive Expedition uses airplanes and motor vehicles; a young George Patton fights a car-mounted shootout. Haiti occupation (1915) and dissolution of its legislature (1917). Optional crossover: Ambrose Bierce vanished in Mexico riding with Villa (Ellis has a separate Bierce episode).

**Ledger voice:** Choose during scripting (options: a Haitian voice such as Charlemagne Péralte, or a Mexican revolutionary voice).

**Closing voice option:** Smedley Butler, who later called himself "a gangster for capitalism."

**Must cover (deck 14.2 + 16.3):**
- [ ] Platt Amendment terms: no treaties without U.S. approval; U.S. may intervene in civil unrest; permanent naval lease at Guantánamo Bay
- [ ] **Protectorate**: definition; Puerto Rico in a similar situation
- [ ] Big Stick Diplomacy, "Speak softly and carry a big stick; you will go far"
- [ ] Panama Canal: 13,000+ mile trip around South America; Colombia refused; 1903 revolution; canal opens 1914
- [ ] Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine: U.S. as "police" of Latin America
- [ ] Dominican Republic: U.S. takes control of customs houses to pay off European debts
- [ ] Dollar Diplomacy (Taft): encourage U.S. investment abroad, stabilize economies, protect U.S. interests
- [ ] Nicaragua, 1911-1912: loans, then Marines
- [ ] Moral Diplomacy (Wilson): promote democracy and human rights, oppose dictators, peaceful influence over force
- [ ] Mexican Revolution: Victoriano Huerta's coup; Wilson refuses to recognize him and helps his enemies; Huerta falls; U.S.-Mexico relations sour
- [ ] Terms: diplomacy, investment, infrastructure, intervention, coup, democracy, human rights
- [ ] "Connection to today" beats from the deck (U.S. role in Latin America, IMF/World Bank, sanctions) can become a short outro

**Series finale (from Ellis's "Imperial Isle" critical thinking activity):** Describe a fictional Caribbean island in crisis: American businesses, a friendly but harsh government, a growing rebel movement, Americans killed. "You're the president. Do you go TR, Taft, or Wilson?" Viewers argue it in the comments. This doubles as Ellis's classroom critical thinking task and as engagement for the channel, and it hands the compelling question to the audience. Then the Declaration bookend.

## 6. Fact-check list (verify before scripting final)

- "You furnish the pictures..." Hearst quote: treat as likely apocryphal and say so
- *Maine* cause: 1898 Navy inquiry (mine) vs. 1976 Rickover study (internal explosion); 1999 National Geographic analysis was inconclusive
- Spanish-American War deaths: about 5,500 total, about 379-385 in combat
- Philippine-American War casualty estimates vary widely; cite a source
- Reconcentration deaths: deck says 200,000; estimates range roughly 150,000-400,000
- USS *Oregon* voyage length (about 66-67 days, roughly 14,000 miles)
- Sanford Dole / James Dole relationship
- Wu Tingfang's statements on exclusion
- Tsinghua and Boxer indemnity remission
- Smedley Butler "gangster for capitalism" (1935, *Common Sense* magazine)
- Butler's role in dissolving Haiti's legislature (1917)
- Claim that Wilson intervened in Latin America more than TR or Taft: phrase carefully and source it

**Copyright:** Primary sources from before 1929 (Declaration, Kipling, Twain, Mahan, Hay's notes, speeches) can be quoted. For modern historians, paraphrase.
