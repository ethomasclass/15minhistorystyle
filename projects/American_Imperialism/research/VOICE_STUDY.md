# Voice study: how good modern explainers talk, and what it means for our narration

For *Chunk History*, American Imperialism Ep 1, "The Pitch Meeting" (`../ep1/script/SCRIPT.md`, draft 3). Written November 2026.
The study reads real transcripts of educational and history channels that students watch, looks at the research on narration
and attention, and turns both into a voice guide that works with a **deadpan cloned voice** and the series' humor rules
(`../HUMOR.md`: the narrator never jokes, the cut is the joke).

## 1. Summary: what the best ones have in common

1. **They sound spoken, not written.** Contractions everywhere, plain verbs, and "you" used to put the viewer inside the
   situation ("a California grape farmer like you", Half as Interesting; "your whole life is basically invisible in comparison",
   Kurzgesagt). Half as Interesting, Vox and Heimler say "you" about **1.1 to 1.4 times per 100 words**. We say it **0.4**.
2. **Their rhythm varies a lot, and ours doesn't.** The surprise of this study: our script is *already shorter and choppier
   than every channel studied* (mean 10.8 words a sentence, 48% of sentences 8 words or fewer). The others average 13 to 22
   words, and a fifth to a third of their sentences run 25 words or more. What makes a line land is a long, easy sentence
   followed by a short flat one. When every sentence is short, none of them lands. Ours reads "teacher-ish" because of
   uniform short sentences plus signposts ("So here's the question", "Two words before we start"), not because the
   sentences are long.
3. **The dry humor is understatement and precise detail, said straight.** History Matters: "the issue was that he was
   lying." Half as Interesting: "Robbery, murder, robbing a murderer. Boring." Kurzgesagt: "The prime suspect was a lake."
   None of them signals that a line is funny. The jokes that aged badly are all topical: Crash Course 2013's "not that
   Matthew Perry" and "born in Kenya", Half as Interesting's "a baller time", Johnny Harris's "communist vibes".
4. **Sarcasm needs the speaker's real voice, and a flat clone can't supply it.** Heimler's line about McKinley seeing the
   Filipinos as "backwards and brown and un-Christian" only works because his voice tells you he means the opposite.
   Read flat, sarcasm sounds sincere, and for students that's a real risk. A deadpan narrator has to say things that are
   *literally true*. The irony comes from putting facts side by side, and from the cut.
5. **They define terms in passing, in plain words, as they go.** "Muhammad Raasha, which just means king" (Johnny Harris);
   "what's called a stone fruit, meaning there's a hard pit at the center" (Vox); "non-contiguous or not connected to the
   mainland" (Crash Course). Nobody stops to announce a vocabulary list.
6. **They open on a thing, then turn.** "This is a peach." then "But here's the thing." (Vox); "This is a map ... And there's
   something weird about it." (Crash Course); "This village was struck by an invisible killer." (Kurzgesagt). Our cold
   open already does this well. Keep it.
7. **Slang works only when the slang is the joke, or when the speaker is the same age as the audience.** Lauren Cella's
   "Gen Z Teaches History" is a deliberate persona (a "Gen Z teacher from the future"), and most of her audience is her age
   or older. Teens surveyed about brands copying their slang say it makes them cringe. In communication research, an adult shifting toward a younger audience's
   speech and overdoing it is called *overaccommodation*, and listeners hear it as patronizing. A plain spoken register,
   with no slang, ages best and is what most of the channels studied use.
8. **The research backs a conversational style, used in moderation.** Conversational wording improves retention (d = 0.30)
   and transfer (d = 0.54) in lessons under about 35 minutes. It helps most when it isn't overdone and when learners are
   beginners, but it can backfire on distressing content. Funny asides that aren't tied to the point ("seductive details")
   lower learning, and the effect is stronger in social studies. Faster speech holds attention better; our 185 wpm is
   already in the fast range, so the deadpan read should never slow down to sound "serious" (the quiet chapter excepted).

What to take from it:
- **We don't need shorter sentences. We need a wider spread of lengths.** Our SD (7.6) is the lowest in the table. Half as

- **TED-Ed is the textbook pole**: no "you", no contractions, no questions, steady 20-word sentences. It's clear, but it's
  the voice students describe as school.

## 4. What the research says about attention and comprehension

| Finding | What it means for our voice | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Conversational wording beats formal wording: d = 0.30 for retention and 0.54 for transfer across 3,312 students; the effect fades in sessions longer than about 35 minutes | Our 10-minute episodes sit right in the range where it works. Use "you", contractions, direct address | Ginns, Martin and Marsh 2013, meta-analysis |
| The personalization principle works best "when it is not overdone" and for beginners. Small changes, like swapping "the" for "your", are enough | About 1 "you" per 100 words is plenty. Our 8th to 11th graders are beginners on this topic | Mayer, *Multimedia Learning*; Clark and Mayer |
| On distressing content, personalization can backfire: it lowered learning and drew attention away from the images | No "you" in the quiet chapter, and never put the viewer in the place of the Queen or of Native Hawaiians | Zander, Wetzel, Kühl and Bertel 2017; Kühl and Zander 2017 |
| Seductive details (interesting but irrelevant additions) lower learning: 58 studies, more than 7,500 students, and the harm is larger in social studies | Every wry line has to carry a fact or a contrast. A joke for its own sake costs learning | Sundararajan and Adesope 2020 |
| Humor tied to the course material helps recall; too much humor and sarcasm hurt; restating the point after a joke helps | One wry line about once a minute, always on the content, then the plain point | Banas, Dunbar, Rodriguez and Liu 2011 |
| Across 6.9 million edX sessions, shorter videos and faster speech held engagement better (rates ranged 48 to 254 wpm, mean 156). The authors linked fast speech to enthusiasm | 185 wpm is fast already. Deadpan means no performed emotion, so the energy has to come from pace, rhythm, new information in every sentence, and the edit. Never slow the read down to sound weighty (the quiet chapter excepted) | Guo, Kim and Rubin 2014 |
| A modern text-to-speech voice matched a human recording on credibility and facilitated learning, and beat an older engine | The cloned voice isn't a handicap. But a flat voice can't carry irony or stress a word mid-sentence, so the *wording* has to carry the emphasis | Craig and Schroeder 2017 |
| Listening doesn't automatically beat reading for older students: one study found reading comprehension ahead of listening by grade 8, and the two don't simply become equal | Our viewers can't re-read a sentence. Spoken syntax has to be *easier* than a textbook's: right-branching sentences, the key word last, one idea per clause | Shanahan; Texas A&M Corpus Christi thesis comparing grades 4, 6 and 8 |

## 5. What sounds "cringe" to teenagers, and what sounds natural

**What reads as trying too hard**
- **Borrowed slang from an adult or a brand.** In a Business Insider survey of 104 teens (about 2018), words like "swag" and
  "bae" made them cringe when brands used them, and the article's own point is that the words had already gone out of date. A Preply survey ranked "6-7" and "Skibidi" as Gen Z's top cringe
  terms, which shows how fast the list turns over.
- **Overaccommodation.** Communication accommodation theory calls adjustments toward a listener's speech
  *overaccommodation* when they're "excessive, inappropriate, unnecessary, or patronizing". Simplification and repetition
  that the listener didn't need are textbook examples. That covers adult slang, and it also covers over-explaining
  ("Two words before we start").
- **The narrator telling you how to feel**: "let that sink in", "Yes, unfortunately you heard that right", "the honest answer".
- **Jokes that need you to recognize something** (a celebrity, a franchise, a meme) and that date: the 2013 Crash Course
  jokes now read as a time capsule. In an r/badhistory thread, one commenter who grew up on Crash Course called watching it
  now "a cringe fest" (quoted from a search summary; the thread itself couldn't be opened). One reader's view, but a common one.
- **Dad-joke energy can work, but only as the teacher's own personality.** Heimler's students remember his "dumb" jokes
  (Atlanta Magazine), and his success shows a teacher's real voice can be an asset. His voice is enthusiastic, though, and
  ours is deadpan. Copy the structure, not the jokes.

**What sounds natural**
- **Plain spoken English said with confidence**: contractions, "you", "kind of", "basically", "it turns out", used sparingly.
- **Not visibly caring whether you laugh.** That's what dry voices share: History Matters, Half as Interesting, the
  narrator of *Arrested Development* (see `HUMOR.md`).
- **The facts doing the work.** Cella: "I'm not making it interesting. I'm just telling you what happened." A student
  reviewer of OverSimplified said the animation is funny "even when no obvious jokes are made".
- **Respect for the viewer's intelligence**: no vocabulary announcements, no "Remember X?" quizzes, no repeating the
  question twice.

## 6. The proposed voice guide (15 rules)

**The voice in one line:** a sharp 25-year-old explaining this to a friend who missed class: plain, quick, unbothered, and
exact. It works with `script-writing.md` and `HUMOR.md`. Where they disagree, `HUMOR.md` wins for this series.

1. **Rhythm: long, then short.** Aim for a chapter mean of 11 to 14 words a sentence, with a spread (SD) of 8 or more and at
   least 1 sentence in 7 running 18 words or more (draft 3: 10.8, 7.6, and about 1 in 10). Hook and punchline stretches
   can sit lower; the explaining stretches carry the long sentences. Every paragraph of three or more sentences gets one
   sentence of 18 words or more. No more than three sentences in a row under 8 words (the cold open's
   stacked images are exempt). The deadpan landing is 6 words or fewer and follows a sentence at least twice its length.
   *Check:* run the metrics script on the chapter files.
2. **Build long sentences for a flat voice.** Chain clauses forward with *and, so, which, because*. Never put a clause
   between a subject and its verb, and no parentheses mid-sentence. **The important word goes last**, because a flat voice
   stresses the end. *Check:* read it in a monotone. If you have to lean on a middle word for it to make sense, rebuild the
   sentence.
3. **Contract by default** (*it's, didn't, wasn't, they'd, that's, wouldn't*). Uncontracted only for weight, at most once a
   chapter ("The Queen would not start a war"). *Check:* grep for *did not, was not, is not, it is, there is*.
4. **About 1 "you" per 100 words in the explaining chapters** (3 to 6). Use it to put the viewer in a decision ("so you needed
   places to refuel") or in the act of looking ("Now look at the Pacific"). Never in the place of the people on the
   receiving end, none in the quiet chapter, and never "you guys". *Check:* count per chapter.
5. **Name who did it.** Never "we" for the United States. Write *the planters, Stevens, the Senate, Congress, Cleveland*,
   and use "America" only when the country itself acted (a treaty, a law, a president). This also keeps the series
   question honest. *Check:* grep for "we " and "our ".
6. **Plain meaning first, the word second, inside the sentence.** "A small group taking a government by force is called a
   {coup}." or "X, which just means Y". One {term} per paragraph, brought in when the story needs it. No vocabulary
   announcements. *Check:* no paragraph has two `{}` terms; no "Two words", "That's called X:" or "It's called" leading off.
7. **Ask the question; don't announce it.** Banned: "So here's the question", "Two words before we start", "Listen to",
   "Think of it as", "Remember the ...?", "In this video", "Let's dive in", "Fun fact", "Buckle up", "Spoiler". Allowed, once
   each: "We'll come back to this room." and the channel's "So let's go back to the question."
8. **Connect with *but, so, because, which*.** At most three sentences per chapter start with "And". Use "Then" only for a
   real sequence, and "Meanwhile" not at all. *Check:* grep for sentence-initial And, Then, Meanwhile.
9. **The deadpan line is true, short, and gives no tell.** It comes after a longer sentence, ends on the surprising word, and
   would still be accurate in a court transcript. Banned tells: "Yes.", "Seriously.", "Oops.", "Awkward.", "Subtle.", "Plot
   twist", "Spoiler". *Model:* "It sounded like science. It wasn't." / "Dole refused."
10. **No sarcasm or irony that depends on tone.** If a sentence means the opposite of what it says, cut it. Put the
    contradiction in two true sentences side by side, or in the cut. *Check:* read the line as if a student takes it
    literally. Is it still true?
11. **Every wry line carries content.** If you delete it and the viewer loses no fact and no contrast, delete it. About one a
    minute, never two in a row, never on or right after a quote from the receiving end.
12. **Spoken words at about a grade 7 to 9 level, no slang.** Timeless casual words are fine, one per paragraph at most:
    *basically, pretty, kind of, a lot, huge, weird, it turns out, the catch*. **Banned:** slang and meme formats (*vibes,
    rizz, lowkey, no cap, sus, based, it's giving, main character, rent free, speedrun, the audacity, W/L, let that sink
    in*); pop-culture comparisons the viewer has to recognize; stock idioms (*the last straw, made their move, a perfect
    storm, little did they know, fast forward, the rest is history, beyond its shores*).
13. **Numbers: one per sentence, said the way people say them, with a comparison next to it** ("less than two cents an
    acre"). A precise odd number from the script can be the deadpan on its own ("21,269 names, on 556 pages").
14. **Quotes: plain lead-in, the quote, then a translation.** "Mahan wrote that ..." then a one-sentence *Translation:* when
    the wording is old ("vain"). Nothing wry goes on or after a quote from the receiving end.
15. **Tense: present only inside the room** (the cold open, and the return to the room in chapter 8). Everywhere else,
    past. Don't switch tense mid-paragraph.

**Note on existing guidance.** `script-writing.md` §2 lists asides like "Okay. Dramatic.", "Subtle." and "Which is
basically your vice president running a burner account against you." Those are the narrator noticing a joke, plus slang.
They belong to the channel's older, warmer voice. For this series, rules 9 to 12 and `HUMOR.md` replace them.

## 7. Rewrites

Every fact and every quotation is kept exactly as in SCRIPT.md. Nothing new is added except non-factual connective words,
and one promise in (a) (*Hold on to that.*), which `HUMOR.md` already calls for to plant the running Declaration cut.
CUT cues stay on the same anchor words.

Measured with the same script as section 2: the explaining passages (b) and (c) together average 12.1 words a sentence,
with an SD of 8.7 and 5 of 22 sentences at 18 words or more (the same passages in draft 3: 10.9, SD 6.1, 4 of 22), so
they meet rule 1. The recommended (a) stays lower (9.2, SD 6.0; draft 3: 7.7, SD 3.8) because it's hook and landing
material, which rule 1 allows.

so it gets only one join, which gives the room beat a long-then-short rhythm. Chapter 2 asks the question in one breath
instead of announcing it, ends the question beat on *figure out* (curious, not preachy), plants the Declaration line for the
running cut, and drops the stock phrase *reach beyond its own shores*. Word count: 51 (ch 1) and 96 (ch 2), the same as draft 3; the announcement's words now go to the plant.

### (b) The Admiral (chapter 4, line 126), and the paragraph that lands it (line 128)

**Current:**

> *The Admiral.* His name was Alfred Thayer Mahan, a navy captain, and in 1890 he wrote a book about how Britain got so powerful. His answer: Britain ruled the world because it ruled the sea. And a navy needs more than ships. Steamships burned coal, and they burned through it fast. So you need bases and coaling stations, scattered across the ocean. Mahan wrote that "it is equally vain to acquire distant coaling stations without maintaining a powerful navy." You need both. Meanwhile, Britain, France and Germany were grabbing colonies all over the world, and a lot of Americans did not want to be left out.
>
> And here's the thing about the Pacific. It's enormous. And right in the middle of it sat one of the best harbors anywhere. [beat] It belonged to a queen.

**Rewrite:**

> *The Admiral.* Alfred Thayer Mahan. Technically, a navy captain.
>
> In 1890 he wrote a book about how Britain got so powerful, and his answer was this: Britain ruled the world because it ruled the sea.
>
> But a navy needs more than ships. Steamships burned coal, and they burned through it fast, so you needed places to refuel, scattered across the ocean: bases and coaling stations.
>
> Mahan wrote that "it is equally vain to acquire distant coaling stations without maintaining a powerful navy." Translation: the stations are no use without the navy. You need both.
>
> At the same time, Britain, France and Germany were grabbing colonies all over the world, and a lot of Americans didn't want to be left out.
>
> Now look at the Pacific. It's enormous, and right in the middle of it sat one of the best harbors anywhere. [beat] It belonged to a queen.

What changed: *Technically, a navy captain.* fixes a real confusion (a pitcher labeled *The Admiral* introduced as a captain)
and makes it a dry correction in the *Arrested Development* narrator's style, true and with no tell. *His name was* is gone.
The plain words (*places to refuel*) come before the term (*coaling stations*). The archaic quote gets the channel's
*Translation:*. *Meanwhile*, *did not* and both *here's the thing* and *And right* are gone. *Now look at the Pacific*
points at the map with words (`explainer-craft.md` rule 3), and the landing, *It belonged to a queen.*, is unchanged.

### (c) The Committee of Safety and the coup (chapter 6, lines 157 to 165)

**Current:**

> In 1891, a new ruler took the throne: Liliʻuokalani, the king's sister. She wanted a new constitution that gave power back to her people. For the planters, that was the last straw.
>
> In January 1893, they made their move. They called themselves the Committee of Safety.
> > **CUT:** CONTRADICTION. On "Safety," hard cut to rifles being laid out on a table. Snap zoom. 1.5 s of silence.
>
> That's a {coup}: a small group seizing a government by force. And the American minister in Honolulu, John Stevens, ordered sailors and Marines ashore from the warship USS *Boston*.
> > **CUT:** TRUE CAPTION. Lower third, held a beat longer than needed: JOHN L. STEVENS / U.S. Minister to Hawaiʻi / Not authorized by Congress.
>
> The Queen would not start a war with the United States. She stepped aside, in writing, and under protest: "I yield to the superior force of the United States of America."

**Rewrite:**

> In 1891, a new ruler took the throne: Liliʻuokalani, the king's sister. She wanted a new constitution that gave power back to her people. For the planters, that settled it.
>
> In January 1893, they acted, and they gave themselves a name: the Committee of Safety.
> > **CUT:** (unchanged) CONTRADICTION. On "Safety," hard cut to rifles being laid out on a table. Snap zoom. 1.5 s of silence.
>
> A small group taking a government by force is called a {coup}. This one had help.
>
> The help came from the American minister in Honolulu, John Stevens, who ordered sailors and Marines ashore from the warship USS *Boston*. Minister, here, means diplomat, not preacher.
> > **CUT:** (unchanged) TRUE CAPTION. Lower third, held a beat longer than needed: JOHN L. STEVENS / U.S. Minister to Hawaiʻi / Not authorized by Congress.
>
> The Queen would not start a war with the United States, so she stepped aside, in writing, and under protest: "I yield to the superior force of the United States of America."

What changed: two stock idioms are gone (*the last straw*, *made their move*). *For the planters, that settled it.* carries the
same interpretation as before, so the fact-check's closing judgment call still applies. The definition no longer interrupts
the action. It comes after the rifles cut, plain meaning first, and turns into a short loop: *This one had help.* (backed by
Cleveland's own words later in the chapter, "committed with the participation of a diplomatic representative of the
United States"). *Minister* gets defined, because chapter 4 just used *minister* for the Preacher, and an 8th grader will
hear a clergyman. That line is also the dry beat, and it's simply true. *Safety* stays the last word for the cut, the
Stevens sentence still carries the caption, and the Queen's quote stays clean, with nothing wry near it.
