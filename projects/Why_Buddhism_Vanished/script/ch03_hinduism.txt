Start with Hinduism. It's one of the oldest religions on Earth, with roots stretching back thousands of years in the river valleys of the Indus and the Ganges. And it's unusual: there's no single founder. It grew over centuries out of many traditions and sacred texts, including the Vedas.

Today there are about 1.2 billion Hindus. Almost all of them live in India and Nepal. That tight cluster is the signature of an ethnic religion. Being Hindu is deeply tied to Indian culture, family, and place, and Hindus generally don't try to convert anyone.

You can see that connection in daily life. Many Hindu homes have a small shrine, where families do puja each day: offering flowers or food, lighting a lamp, saying a prayer. Many Hindus worship one ultimate reality through many gods and goddesses, like Vishnu, Shiva, Lakshmi, and Ganesh, the elephant-headed remover of obstacles. Ideas like karma and reincarnation, the belief that the soul is reborn into new lives, shape how people think about how to live.

It even shows up on the menu. Many Hindus are vegetarian, and the cow is considered sacred. So when McDonald's opened in India, it did something it does almost nowhere else: it dropped beef completely.

And it shows up in the landscape. In the city of Varanasi, pilgrims step into the Ganges at dawn, because many Hindus believe its water washes away sins.

Every few years, that pull toward sacred rivers produces something almost unbelievable: the Kumbh Mela. It's a pilgrimage festival so big you can see it from space. At the 2025 gathering in Prayagraj, officials estimated more than 600 million visits over six weeks. That's nearly twice the population of the United States.

But wait. If Hinduism stays home, why are there Hindu temples on islands in the Caribbean and the Pacific?

Here's what happened. Starting in the 1830s, after Britain abolished slavery, plantation owners in its colonies needed new workers. So the British recruited hundreds of thousands of Indians as indentured laborers and shipped them to sugar plantations around the world. They didn't come to spread their religion. But they brought it with them.

That's relocation diffusion. And you can still see it today. In Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, Fiji, and Mauritius, Diwali, the festival of lights, is a national holiday. In Mauritius, off the coast of Africa, about half the country is Hindu.

More recent migration carried Hinduism to the UK, Canada, and the United States. And sometimes the culture spreads further than the religion itself. If you've ever done a color run, you've borrowed from Holi, the Hindu festival of colors.

So Hinduism mostly stays home. When it moves, it moves because people move. Now compare that to the religion born right next door.
