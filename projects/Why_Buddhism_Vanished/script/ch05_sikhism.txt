Our third religion is the youngest. Around the year fifteen hundred, in the Punjab region, now split between India and Pakistan, a teacher named Guru Nanak founded Sikhism. Nine more Gurus followed him. Sikhs believe in one God, in the equality of all people, in honest work, and in serving others. Their holy book, the Guru Granth Sahib, is treated with the respect of a living teacher.

And there's no better place to see what that equality means than here: the Golden Temple in Amritsar, Sikhism's holiest site.

Every Sikh house of worship, called a gurdwara, runs a langar: a free community kitchen. Anyone can walk in and eat, regardless of religion, caste, or how much money they have. Everyone sits on the floor, side by side, at the same level. At the Golden Temple, volunteers serve tens of thousands of free meals a day, and more than 100,000 on big holidays. The temple even has doors on all four sides, a sign that everyone is welcome from every direction.

Sikh identity is visible. Many Sikhs keep the Five Ks, articles of faith that include uncut hair, which many Sikh men and some women cover with a turban, and a steel bracelet called a kara. Many Sikh men share the surname Singh, meaning "lion," and many women share Kaur, meaning "princess." The names were meant to wipe out differences of caste and family status.

Sikhism is classified as a universalizing religion, because anyone can become a Sikh. But Sikhs don't really focus on converting people. Instead, it spread mostly the way Hinduism did: through relocation diffusion, as Sikh families migrated around the world.

And here's a wild stat. Sikhs make up a bigger share of Canada's population than India's. In cities like Brampton, Ontario, and Surrey, British Columbia, you'll find gurdwaras serving langar just like in Amritsar.

So Sikhism shows something important: a religion can be universalizing and still spread mainly because people move.
