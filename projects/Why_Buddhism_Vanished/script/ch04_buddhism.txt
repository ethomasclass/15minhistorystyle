About 2,500 years ago, near today's border between India and Nepal, a prince named Siddhartha Gautama grew up surrounded by luxury. The story goes that when he finally left the palace, he saw an old man, a sick man, and a dead body, and it shattered him. If everyone suffers, what's the point of all this?

He gave up everything to find an answer. Eventually, he sat under that tree at Bodh Gaya and reached enlightenment. From then on, people called him the Buddha: the awakened one.

His answer was this: suffering comes from desire and attachment. Follow a set of practices called the Eightfold Path, and you can break free of it and reach nirvana.

And crucially, the Buddha taught that this path was open to anyone, of any family or background. That's what makes Buddhism a universalizing religion. So how did it leave India?

The answer starts with a war. Around 260 BCE, an Indian emperor named Ashoka conquered the kingdom of Kalinga in a battle that killed over 100,000 people. Afterward, he was horrified by what he'd done. He turned to Buddhism, and then he did what emperors can do: he spread it everywhere. He carved Buddhist teachings onto stone pillars across his empire, and he sent missionaries abroad, including his own son, who carried Buddhism to Sri Lanka.

That's hierarchical diffusion.

Then came the second wave. Monks and merchants carried Buddhism person to person along the trade routes of the Silk Road into China, then on to Korea and Japan, and by sea into Southeast Asia. That's contagious diffusion.

As it traveled, Buddhism adapted to each new place, forming different branches: Theravada in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, Mahayana in China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, and Vajrayana in Tibet and Mongolia.

Walk through Luang Prabang, in Laos, just after sunrise, and you'll see Buddhism in daily life. A long line of monks in saffron robes walks silently through town, and people kneel by the road to place sticky rice in their bowls. The community feeds the monks; the monks offer teaching and blessings.

In Thailand and Myanmar, many young men become monks for a short time, sometimes just a few weeks. Families throw a celebration, the young man shaves his head, puts on the robes, lives at the temple, and then comes home. It's a rite of passage, kind of like a gap semester for the soul.

And Buddhism is still diffusing, in new ways. Those meditation apps on your phone, and that mindfulness minute your teacher runs before a test? Both trace back to Buddhist meditation practice.

Which brings us back to our puzzle. If Buddhism spread so far, why did it fade in India?

Historians point to a few reasons. Over time, Buddhism lost the support of Indian kings, the same kind of royal backing that had spread it in the first place. Hinduism revived and absorbed many Buddhist ideas; some Hindus even came to honor the Buddha as a form of the god Vishnu. And around twelve hundred, invading armies destroyed great Buddhist monastery-universities like Nalanda, whose ruins you can still visit today.

In other words, the same thing that made Buddhism spread, that it could travel anywhere, also meant it didn't need India to survive. Its hearth faded, but the religion lived on everywhere it had traveled.
