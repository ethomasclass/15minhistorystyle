# The Candy Man: The Halloween Poison Scare (Short)

**15 Minute History** · standalone vertical Short (1080×1920) · 160 words · about 51 s at the locked pace (188 wpm) + a 3 s end card

**Driving question:** How many kids has a stranger's Halloween candy actually killed?
**Answer:** None that anyone can find. The one real poisoned-candy murder (1974) was a father killing his own son, and the 1982 Tylenol murders turned that story into a national panic.

## 0:00 | The Candy Man

`ch01_candy_scare.txt` · 160 words

A dad comes back from a dark house holding five giant Pixy Stix. Nobody opened the door, he says. Just a hand.

Within an hour, his 8-year-old son is dead. Halloween, 1974, outside Houston. The candy is full of cyanide. And the other four Pixy Stix are in four other kids' bags.

Police find all four. Nobody else ate one. And there was no hand. The poisoner was *the dad*. He'd just bought life insurance on his children.

But people remembered the stranger. In 1982, someone in Chicago put cyanide in Tylenol on store shelves, and seven people died. That Halloween, some towns banned trick-or-treating. Hospitals offered to X-ray candy.

So how many kids has a stranger's candy actually killed? A sociologist checked newspapers back to 1958. He found hoaxes, some pins, a few cuts. *Not one child killed.*

The only real candy killer wasn't behind a dark door. He was the dad walking his kids from house to house.

**End card (3 s, silent + stamp):** "Another Halloween poison story: DRESSED TO KILL · on the channel"

## Fact-check flags

| Script says | Record | Why the wording is safe |
|---|---|---|
| "five giant Pixy Stix," "just a hand" | O'Bryan told police a person cracked the door of a dark house and handed him five Pixy Stix; he saw only a "hairy" arm. The sticks were 21 inches long, refilled with potassium cyanide and resealed with staples. | His own story, told as his story ("he says"). |
| "Within an hour, his 8-year-old son is dead." | Timothy O'Bryan, 8, died on the way to the hospital less than an hour after eating it, Oct 31, 1974. | Matches. |
| "outside Houston" | The family lived in Deer Park; they trick-or-treated in Pasadena, Texas. Sources split on which town to name. | Both are Houston suburbs, so "outside Houston" is true either way. |
| "four other kids' bags … Police find all four. Nobody else ate one." | He gave the other four to his daughter (about 5) and three other children; all four were recovered uneaten (one boy fell asleep holding his, unable to open the staples). | Matches. |
| "He'd just bought life insurance on his children." | $10,000 each in Jan 1974, $20,000 each about a month before, and more in the days before Halloween. He was about $100,000 in debt. | "Just" fits the last policies; the card shows the build-up. |
| (not in narration) | Convicted of capital murder June 3, 1975; executed by lethal injection March 31, 1984, at Huntsville. Often misreported as executed on Halloween. | Kept out of the Short for time; worth a pinned comment. |
| "In 1982, someone in Chicago put cyanide in Tylenol … seven people died." | Seven deaths in the Chicago area, late Sept to early Oct 1982. No one was ever charged with the murders. | Matches; "someone" because it's unsolved. |
| "some towns banned trick-or-treating" | Vineland, NJ cancelled it outright; suburbs in OH, PA, IL and MA followed; candy sales fell 20–50% in many stores. | "Some towns." |
| "Hospitals offered to X-ray candy." | Communities urged hospitals to make X-ray machines available, and many did through the 1980s. | "Offered," not "all hospitals." |
| "A sociologist checked newspapers back to 1958 … Not one child killed." | Joel Best (with Gerald Horiuchi, 1985) searched coverage 1958–1983: 76 reported incidents, a handful of minor injuries, many hoaxes, no child killed or seriously hurt by a treat from trick-or-treating. Best still says so (2023). The O'Bryan death is the one exception, and it wasn't a stranger. | Named as "a sociologist" (no scholar names on screen); "not one child killed" is his finding about strangers' treats, framed by the question. |
