Meanwhile, in America, the same green was on the same walls.

Massachusetts, 1872. A Boston doctor named Frank Draper writes a report for the state's {board of health}, a government office that tracks disease. It was the first one in the country. His first line: "Everybody knows that arsenic is a deadly poison."

Then he shows where it's hiding. Bedroom walls. Children's paint boxes. The green paper wrapped around candy in the window of one of Boston's best candy shops. He even bound real samples of the poisoned paper into his report.

So, ban it? Some European governments were already restricting these colors. Draper said no. Not here. That would be difficult, he wrote, "under a government organized like our own. The rights of individuals and of industrial pursuits are deemed too sacred."

That's {laissez-faire}, French for "leave it alone." The government stays out of business, and buyers look out for themselves. Stop buying it, Draper said, and they'll stop making it.

Two years later, in Michigan, a chemistry professor named Robert Kedzie decided people needed to see it for themselves.

He collected cases. A nine-year-old girl named Emma got sick in her green bedroom, and got better when she was moved out. Then her six-year-old brother was put in the same room. He got sick too.

And in a little town called Spring Port, the girl who died. Her family's walls were green in nearly every room.

So Kedzie went shopping. He gathered wallpaper from ordinary stores, tested every sample, and found arsenic in all 86. Then he bound them into a book, and mailed it to the libraries. That book.

His headline was a question: "Shall we poison our homes?"
