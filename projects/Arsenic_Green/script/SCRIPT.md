# Dressed to Kill: How Arsenic Green Took Over the 1800s

**15 Minute History** · narration script · 1,685 words · about 10:15 finished (draft 1, before the cut list)

**Driving question (cold open, answered in ch08):** How did a poison end up on the walls, on the dresses, and on the candy? And why did it take so long to get it off?

**Answer:** It got there because it was beautiful and cheap, and people wanted the countryside indoors. It stayed because the proof was messy, the money was big, and nobody had to prove it was safe: the sick had to prove it wasn't. In Britain shoppers, not lawmakers, finally drove it out; in America a state report called a ban impossible "under a government organized like our own," and the first limits came 19 and 28 years later.

## 0:00 | The Library Book

`ch01_cold_open.txt` · 177 words

November 1874. Lansing, Michigan.

A big book goes out in the mail to 100 public libraries. There's almost nothing in it to read. Just page after page of wallpaper. 86 samples, all of it on sale in ordinary stores.

The cover letter makes one odd request. Because of "the poisonous nature of the paper of which this book is made," maybe don't let children handle it.

Every page has *arsenic* in it.

And the very first sample came from the house where a girl, about ten years old, had died that year.

Only four or five copies survive. Librarians handle them with gloves. When one was scanned in 2018, the person scanning it wore a protective suit.

Here's the strange part. By 1874, everybody knew arsenic was poison. So here's the question I want to answer today: How did a poison end up on the walls, on the dresses, and on the candy? And why did it take so long to get it off?

It starts with a Swedish chemist, a beautiful new color, and a warning.

## 1:10 | The Perfect Green

`ch02_perfect_green.txt` · 176 words

1775. Sweden. A chemist named Carl Wilhelm Scheele mixes copper with arsenic and gets something remarkable: a bright, clean green. Brighter than any green you could make from plants. And cheap.

It's called {Scheele's Green}. And Scheele knew exactly what was in it. A couple of years later he wrote that people using it ought to be warned it was poisonous.

Not a great sign.

In 1814, German chemists made an even brighter version, later called {Paris green}. I'm going to call the whole family what one Victorian poet called it: *arsenic green*.

So why did everyone want it? Timing. Factories were filling cities with smoke and soot. Green was the color of the countryside, of everything the city wasn't. And suddenly, ordinary families could afford it. Britain dropped its tax on wallpaper in 1836, and new machines started printing it by the mile.

So arsenic green went everywhere. On parlor walls. On ball gowns. In the fake leaves women pinned in their hair. On candy wrappers, children's toys, even book covers.

All of it, arsenic.

## 2:11 | Dressed to Kill

`ch03_dressed_to_kill.txt` · 208 words

The most dangerous place to wear it? Possibly a dance.

The 1860s fashion was enormous skirts of thin fabric, often dyed bright green, with green leaves and flowers pinned in the hair.

In 1862, a chemist tested one of those hair wreaths. It held enough arsenic, he said, "to poison twenty persons."

The British Medical Journal did the math on a whole dress. A lady at a ball, it wrote, "carries in her skirts poison enough to slay the whole of the admirers she may meet with in half a dozen ball-rooms."

Which is one way to keep admirers at a distance.

The same month, the comic magazine *Punch* ran this. "The Arsenic Waltz." A skeleton in evening clothes, asking another skeleton to dance.

But here's the twist. Most of the women wearing those dresses were fine. One evening in a green gown wasn't usually enough to hurt you. The people in real danger were the ones who *made* them, breathing that green dust all day, every day.

Hold onto that. We're about to meet one of them.

## 3:24 | The Walls *(heavy chapter: no jokes, slower voice)*

`ch04_the_walls.txt` · 227 words

London, 1861. Matilda Scheurer is 19 years old. She makes {artificial flowers}, dusting fake leaves with green powder, in a workshop with about a hundred other young women. They tie thin cloth over their mouths. It doesn't help much.

After a year and a half, she gets sick. She throws up something green. In November 1861, she dies. A doctor finds arsenic in her lungs and her liver. Her sister had died the same way.

The {inquest}, the official investigation into her death, calls it an accident. Caused, it says, by the arsenic "used in her employment."

Nobody was charged. No rule changed.

A few months later, on the other side of London, a bricklayer's family named Turner is losing children, one after another. The doctors call it diphtheria, a throat infection. Then the youngest, three-year-old Ann Amelia, gets sick too.

The surgeon who comes to see her, Thomas Orton, notices the walls. Green paper. Damp. The color came off on your hand "like paint."

A chemist tests it. Arsenic. Four children in about two months.

The jury at the inquest hears all of this, and decides the children died of natural causes.

And that was the pattern. People got sick at home. They got better when they went away. They came home, and got sick again. And very often, nobody thought to look at the walls.

## 4:49 | Witch Fever

`ch05_witch_fever.txt` · 298 words

So if doctors were finding arsenic in the walls, why didn't it stop? Three reasons.

One: the evidence was messy. Plenty of people lived with green wallpaper and felt fine. And arsenic poisoning looks like a lot of other illnesses. So every case could be argued.

Two: doctors couldn't agree on *how* a wall could poison you. The popular theory was a gas. In damp rooms, mold growing on the paper gave off a garlic-smelling arsenic gas. That gas is real. But modern tests suggest it's much weaker than the Victorians feared. The likelier culprit was simpler: dust. Arsenic green flaked off the walls and settled on everything. You breathed it in. Kids touched it and put their fingers in their mouths.

You might have heard that wallpaper killed Napoleon. His really did have arsenic in it. But it almost certainly didn't kill him. Stomach cancer did.

Three: money. One English wallpaper maker said he went through two tons of arsenic a week.

Which brings us to William Morris, probably the most famous designer of his day. His family's fortune came from an arsenic mine, one of the biggest in the world, and from 1871 to 1875 he sat on its board. His early wallpapers used arsenic green.

In 1885, after a customer complained, Morris wrote to a colleague: "As to the arsenic scare a greater folly it is hardly possible to imagine: the doctors were bitten as people were bitten by the witch-fever."

{Witch fever}. In other words: a panic. His own theory? The real problem was the toilets.

To be fair to Morris, his company had stopped using arsenic years earlier, and the science really was disputed. To his critics, it's a man whose fortune came out of an arsenic mine, calling the danger a panic.

## 6:32 | Shall We Poison Our Homes?

`ch06_poison_our_homes.txt` · 282 words

Meanwhile, in America, the same green was on the same walls.

Massachusetts, 1872. A Boston doctor named Frank Draper writes a report for the state's {board of health}, a government office that tracks disease. It was the first one in the country. His first line: "Everybody knows that arsenic is a deadly poison."

Then he shows where it's hiding. Bedroom walls. Children's paint boxes. The green paper wrapped around candy in the window of one of Boston's best candy shops. He even bound real samples of the poisoned paper into his report.

So, ban it? Some European governments were already restricting these colors. Draper said no. Not here. That would be difficult, he wrote, "under a government organized like our own. The rights of individuals and of industrial pursuits are deemed too sacred."

That's {laissez-faire}, French for "leave it alone." The government stays out of business, and buyers look out for themselves. Stop buying it, Draper said, and they'll stop making it.

Two years later, in Michigan, a chemistry professor named Robert Kedzie decided people needed to see it for themselves.

He collected cases. A nine-year-old girl named Emma got sick in her green bedroom, and got better when she was moved out. Then her six-year-old brother was put in the same room. He got sick too.

And in a little town called Spring Port, the girl who died. Her family's walls were green in nearly every room.

So Kedzie went shopping. He gathered wallpaper from ordinary stores, tested every sample, and found arsenic in all 86. Then he bound them into a book, and mailed it to the libraries. That book.

His headline was a question: "Shall we poison our homes?"

## 8:10 | Arsenic-Free

`ch07_arsenic_free.txt` · 157 words

So how did it end? In Britain, not with a law.

Doctors kept writing. Newspapers kept printing. And shoppers got scared. By the 1880s, "arsenic-free" was something you put in the ad.

Other countries did pass bans. Sweden in 1876. Germany in the 1880s. But Britain never banned arsenic wallpaper. It didn't have to. By about 1900, nobody wanted it, so nobody made it.

In America, Draper's "leave it alone" idea got a long test. Massachusetts banned arsenic in toys and candy in 1891. Wallpaper and fabric? 1900. That's 28 years after his report.

But the green didn't disappear. It moved. Around the same time, a beetle was eating its way across America's potato fields. Farmers found that Paris green killed it. So the same poison Americans were tearing off their walls, they were now dusting onto their potatoes. It became one of the first big insecticides in the country.

But that's a story for another time.

## 9:05 | Back to the Book

`ch08_back_to_the_book.txt` · 160 words

So let's go back to the question. How did a poison end up on the walls? And why did it take so long to get it off?

It got there because it was beautiful, and cheap, and people wanted a little bit of the countryside inside. It stayed because the proof was messy, the money was big, and nobody had to prove it was safe. It was up to the sick to prove it wasn't. A family in Limehouse couldn't. A jury looked at four children and green walls, and called it natural.

In Britain, in the end, it wasn't lawmakers who got it off the walls. It was shoppers, who'd finally heard enough.

Kedzie's book is still out there. Four or five copies, sealed in plastic. Still green.

William Morris called it witch fever. But this time, the witch was real.
