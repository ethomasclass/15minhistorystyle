1775. Sweden. A chemist named Carl Wilhelm Scheele mixes copper with arsenic and gets something remarkable: a bright, clean green. Brighter than any green you could make from plants. And cheap.

It's called {Scheele's Green}. And Scheele knew exactly what was in it. A couple of years later he wrote that people using it ought to be warned it was poisonous.

Not a great sign.

In 1814, German chemists made an even brighter version, later called {Paris green}. I'm going to call the whole family what one Victorian poet called it: *arsenic green*.

So why did everyone want it? Timing. Factories were filling cities with smoke and soot. Green was the color of the countryside, of everything the city wasn't. And suddenly, ordinary families could afford it. Britain dropped its tax on wallpaper in 1836, and new machines started printing it by the mile.

So arsenic green went everywhere. On parlor walls. On ball gowns. In the fake leaves women pinned in their hair. On candy wrappers, children's toys, even book covers.

All of it, arsenic.
