Checked 2026-10-15. Every quotation below was read in a digitized period scan (archive.org OCR text, checked against the page headers). "p." means the printed page number in that volume.

| # | Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Scheele warned that his green was poisonous (1777) | **Confirmed in substance**: a letter to J. G. Gahn, 9 Feb 1777 |

## 1. Scheele's warning (ch02)

**Source found.** C. W. Scheele, *Nachgelassene Briefe und Aufzeichnungen*, ed. A. E. Nordenskiöld (Stockholm: Norstedt, 1892). Letter no. 51 **to Johan Gottlieb Gahn**, dated **Köping, 9 February 1777**, pp. 193–195. The editor notes "Das Original ist in deutscher Sprache geschrieben."
https://archive.org/details/nachgelassenebri00scheuoft

Period wording (p. 195):
> "Dass ich Arsenik in der grünen Farbe angegeben, geschah deswegen, weil ich besorgt bin, dass sich Jemand bei dessen Praeparirung auf dem Stein oder sonsten Schaden zufügen könnte, und ich ohnedem überzeugt bin, dass der, welcher nur wenig in der Chemie gethan, die Bestandtheile sehr leicht herausholen kann. Er würde solches für eine schöne Entdeckung ansehen; diesen vermeinten Ruhm kann ein solcher nun aber nicht geniessen."

Translation:
> "That I stated [that there is] arsenic in the green colour was because I am worried that someone might do himself harm in preparing it on the [grinding] stone or otherwise. Besides, I am convinced that anyone who has done only a little chemistry could very easily work out its ingredients. He would take that for a fine discovery, but such a person can now not enjoy this supposed fame."

Supporting letters:
- Letter 44 to Gahn, 10 May 1776, p. 177: "Ist ihnen eine grüne [Farbe] bekannt, welche Kupfer und Arsenik enthält?" ("Do you know a green [colour] that contains copper and arsenic?")
- Letter 50, undated, p. 192: "Ich habe aus vitriolo de Cypro und Arsenik eine grüne Farbe bereitet, auch eine Probe davon an den Secretär Wargentin gesandt." ("I have prepared a green colour from Cyprus vitriol and arsenic, and sent a sample of it to Secretary Wargentin [of the Academy].")

The published recipe is "Method of preparing a new green colour" (1778), in *The Chemical Essays of C. W. Scheele* (English translation, 1786; reissued 1901), Essay XIV, pp. 185–186: https://archive.org/details/chemicalessaysof00scheuoft
- He says the colour "has not undergone the slightest alteration in the course of three years." That fits a date of about 1775.
- His only published caution is a footnote: "The water with which the colour is lixiviated contains a little arsenic, and must therefore be thrown out in a place to which cattle have no access."

**Verdict: Confirmed in substance.** Scheele did write, in 1777, that he had disclosed the arsenic so that people would not harm themselves making it. His other reason was to stop anyone else claiming the discovery, which matches Harley's paraphrase. He does not literally say buyers "should be warned it was poisonous." His concern is for people preparing or grinding it.

**Suggested wording:** "A couple of years later, in a letter to a friend, he explained why he'd told people it contained arsenic: he was worried someone would hurt themselves making it." A shorter option is to keep the current line but change "people using it" to "people working with it."

## 2. Matilda Scheurer (ch04)

**Source found.** *Medical Times and Gazette*, 30 Nov 1861, "The Week," p. 560 (vol. 2 for 1861). The journal quotes a newspaper report of the inquest, and its index lists "Arsenic … in artificial flower-making, 560."
https://archive.org/details/medicaltimesgaze23londuoft

Period wording (spelt "Seheurer"/"Scheuier" in the OCR):
> "On Tuesday an inquiry was held by Mr. G. S. Brent, touching the death of Matilda Scheurer, a good-looking girl of 19, who died from poison imbibed into the system during her engagement in the manufacture of artificial flower leaves. Mrs. Louisa Scheurer, a widow, and mother to the deceased, deposed that on Thursday week her daughter was taken ill. She was seized with **vomiting of green stuff**… She had been ill several times before, complaining of pains in the stomach and sickness, **for the last year and a half**. She was engaged as an artificial florist in the warehouse of M. Bergerond, of Judd-street, Brunswick-square… Mr. James Thomas Paul, Surgeon… found that **the lungs gave evidence of the presence of arsenite of copper, the liver being highly impregnated**, as also the mesenteric glands… **A sister of the deceased had died under similar circumstances.** Mr. Bergerond said he employed **ninety-eight girls**… he had suggested the wearing of masks, but it was objected to by them as producing excessive heat. **They, however, wore muslin over their mouths.** It was stated by a juror that the arsenite of copper in such manufactures was prohibited in France… The jury returned a verdict to the effect that the deceased **died accidentally**, from the effects of mortal disease in her stomach and other organs, occasioned by arsenite of copper **used in her employment**."

The editors add: "How can the State hinder ninety-eight young women from destroying themselves for a beggarly livelihood?"

**Verdict: Confirmed** on every point checked: age 19; about 100 workers (98); muslin over the mouth; green vomit; arsenic in the lungs and liver; the sister; "accidentally"; "used in her employment."

**Wording changes:**
- The script says "After a year and a half, she gets sick." The source says she had been sick on and off for a year and a half before the final illness. Suggested: "For a year and a half she's sick on and off. Then she throws up something green. In November 1861, she dies."
- "About a hundred other young women" is fine. Strictly, 98 was the total, including her.

## 3. The Turner children, Limehouse (ch04)

**Sources found.**
- (a) *Medical Times and Gazette*, 26 Apr 1862, "Medical News," p. 446.
- (b) *Medical Times and Gazette*, 3 May 1862, p. 471: the resumed inquest.
- Both are at https://archive.org/details/medicaltimesgaze24londuoft
- (c) T. Orton, "On Poisoning by Green Wall-Paper," *The Lancet*, 8 Nov 1862, pp. 516–518: https://archive.org/details/sim_the-lancet_1862-11-08_2045

Period wording:
- (a) p. 446: "the four children resided with their parents at Tomlin's-terrace, Salmon's-lane, Commercial-road, Limehouse, and the father (Richard Turner), who is a journeyman bricklayer… A surgeon of the district (Mr. Horton) was called in… he pronounced the child to be suffering from **diphtheria**, from which it died, and death was registered accordingly. A few days afterwards another child was seized… A third child also fell a victim… and on Saturday last, **Ann Amelia Turner, aged three years**, also died." This report also calls "Dr. Orton" "the Medical Officer of Health," and says the children had "licked the green colour off the surface."
- (b) p. 471: "touching the death of Amelia Turner, the last of a family of **four children who have all died within a period of two months**… Dr. Thomas Orton, of White Horse-lane, Stepney, said he was called to deceased on April 3… [the mother said the others] had ultimately died of soreness of the throat, which had been **attributed to diphtheria**… He then **noticed the green paper on the wall**… Dr. Letheby… found that the [paper] contained arsenic… three grains to the square foot… He examined the stomach and viscera, but could not find arsenic… [Orton:] At fifty degrees it became quite damp, and **the colour came off on the hand almost like paint**. The Coroner summed up, and the jury, after a brief consultation, returned a verdict of **'Natural Death.'** The Coroner said it was a question whether he could take this verdict. He could not refrain from expressing his entire dissent…"
- (c) Lancet, p. 516: "the three other children had already died within six weeks of measles, but accompanied with such unusual sequelae… The fourth and last child, **aged two years and eight months**… also suffered… from a 'diphtheritic' affection." The mother's sworn deposition says: "**The child sixteen months old that died** used to pick the wall." Orton adds: "Not so the jury; in their judgment the 'death arose from natural causes.'" He blames a juror "who boasted of his control over five-and-twenty houses" and wanted to avoid the cost of stripping green paper.

**Verdict: Confirmed** on:
- the diphtheria diagnosis
- Ann Amelia, about 3
- Thomas Orton noticing the walls
- "like paint"
- the chemist finding arsenic in the paper
- four children in two months
- the natural-causes verdict

The coroner's public dissent, previously unverified, is now confirmed (MTG, 3 May 1862).

**Corrections:**
1. **Ann Amelia was not the youngest.** She was the last of the four to die. A 16-month-old sibling had died earlier, according to the mother's deposition. Change "Then the youngest, three-year-old Ann Amelia, gets sick too" to "**Then the last of them, three-year-old Ann Amelia, falls ill too.**"
2. The inquest was on Ann Amelia only. "The jury… decides the children died of natural causes" is a slight overreach. Suggested: "**The jury hears all of this, and calls it a natural death.**" The closing line ("a jury looked at four children and green walls, and called it natural") can stay.
3. Optional: "The doctors call it diphtheria" is supported. Orton says the doctors first called it measles, then diphtheria, so the line is fine as it stands.
4. Do not imply that arsenic was found in the child's body. Letheby found none, and the script currently doesn't claim it.

## 4. Wallpaper output, 1834 to 1874 (ch02)

**Source found.** A. V. Sugden & J. L. Edmondson, *A History of English Wallpaper 1509–1914* (London: Batsford, 1926), pp. 138–139: https://archive.org/details/historyofenglish00sugd

Period wording:
> "The output of paper-hangings in 1770 was 255,731 pieces; in 1834, **1,222,753**… the output of just over a million pieces in 1834 had increased in 1851 to five-and-a-half million pieces; in 1850 [sic, prob. 1860] to nineteen millions, and **in 1874 to thirty-two millions**."

**The 1834 figure** comes from the paper-staining excise, which was charged per square yard until it was abolished in 1836. The same page gives the duty yield for 1834 as £63,795: £58,851 from England, £436 from Scotland and £4,508 from Ireland. It is therefore a near-official figure for the whole United Kingdom.

**The 1874 figure** has no source given in Sugden & Edmondson. I found no 1870s primary source for it in archive.org full-text searches. W. D. Andrews (Cambridge PhD, 2017, Table 3) gives the same 1834 and 1874 figures, citing Entwisle, *A Literary History of Wallpaper* (1960), p. 165. The OUP JVC blog cites Hoskins (2005), p. 136, for the 32 million figure only.

**About the percentage:** Hawksley (*Bitten by Witch Fever*) says output "rose by more than 2,615 per cent, from 1,222,753 rolls in 1834 to 32,000,000 rolls by 1874." The arithmetic is wrong. 32,000,000 ÷ 1,222,753 = 26.2 times, an increase of about **2,517%**. Do not quote "2,615%" anywhere.

**Verdict: Partly confirmed.** The 1834 number is solid. The 1874 number is a long-repeated round figure with no primary source found. The script's "about 1.2 million… about 32 million" is fine as written. If you want a hedge: "By the 1870s? Around 30 million." "Rolls" and "pieces" are the same unit here.

## 5. "Two tons of arsenic weekly" (ch05)

**Draper's own citation**, from the *Third Annual Report, State Board of Health of Massachusetts* (Jan 1872), p. 20: "In 1860, a manufacturer of paper hangings in England stated that he used two tons of arsenic weekly." His footnote reads "Taylor on Poisons, p. 430."
https://archive.org/details/annualreportofst7071mass

**The original:** Alfred Swaine Taylor, *On Poisons in Relation to Medical Jurisprudence and Medicine*, 2nd ed. (London: Churchill, **1859**), p. 430, section "Arsenite of Copper, Scheele's Green": https://archive.org/details/b2398479x
The American edition (Philadelphia: Blanchard & Lea, 1859) has the same text: https://archive.org/details/onpoisonsinrelat00tayl

> "…and lastly and most abundantly, in the various kinds of green decorative papers for covering the walls of sitting and bed-rooms. **A manufacturer has informed me, that so great was the demand for this 'cheerful' but poisonous colour, that his average consumption of arsenic had amounted to about two tons weekly!**"

The claim is not in the 1848 first edition.

**Verdict: Partly confirmed.** The figure is real and from the period, but:
- (a) it dates from 1859, not 1860;
- (b) Taylor says only "a manufacturer." He could have been a maker of the green pigment rather than of wallpaper. Draper's "manufacturer of paper hangings" is his own reading;
- (c) it is hearsay reported by Taylor.

**Suggested wording:** "Three: money. One manufacturer told a leading poisons expert he was going through about two tons of arsenic a week." Or keep the script's line and drop "wallpaper": "One English manufacturer said he went through two tons of arsenic a week."

## 6. Devon Great Consols and Morris (ch05)

**Morris's directorship.** J. W. Mackail, *The Life of William Morris*, vol. 1 (1899; this copy 1901): https://archive.org/details/lifeofwilliammor01mackuoft
- pp. 216–217: "In 1871 he accepted a place on the directorate of the mining company from which a large portion of the income of his mother and sisters as well as his own was derived… When he resigned his directorship four years afterwards he came home from the last meeting… and solemnly sat down upon his tall hat."
- p. 324 quotes Morris's birthday letter to his mother on "the resignation of his Directorship of the Devon Great Consols Company": "I have just come from the D. G. C. meeting, and, I suppose, ended my business there."
- This supports **1871 to about 1875**.

**Where the fortune came from.** Mackail, vol. 1, p. 14: in 1844 "a company was formed to work certain veins of **copper**… Of these [1,024 shares] Mr. Morris held 272… Mr. Morris's holding rose for a while to the value of over two hundred thousand pounds… It still leads a somewhat struggling existence on the proceeds of the arsenic which, in the high days of the copper-mining industry, was neglected an unimportant by-product." So the family's wealth was made from **copper**. The mine became an arsenic producer from about 1867/68, and it was producing arsenic during Morris's time on the board.

**"Biggest arsenic" in a period source.** *Mining Journal*, 9 Dec 1876 (supplement), letter headed "Devon Great Consols Meeting," signed "One who knows": "**The Devon Great Consols are the largest arsenic producers**, whilst Mr. Field is not a producer at all, but only a refiner."
https://archive.org/details/sim_mining-journal_1876-12-09_46_supplement

No period statement saying "in the world" was found. The claim that it supplied "half the world's arsenic" appears only in modern heritage sources (University of Plymouth, Grace's Guide).

**Verdict: Confirmed**, with one correction. "One of the biggest in the world" is safe, because a period trade paper calls it the largest producer in a British-dominated trade. The board dates are confirmed. However, "His family's fortune came from an arsenic mine" is inaccurate: the fortune came from copper.

**Suggested wording:** "His family's fortune came from a copper mine that became one of the biggest arsenic producers in the world, and from 1871 to 1875 he sat on its board."

## 7. Sweden's ban (ch07)

**Source found.** Henry Carr, "Our Domestic Poisons," with an Appendix on Arsenical Poisoning by Wall Papers, in *The Health Exhibition Literature* (International Health Exhibition, London, 1884), appendix pp. 208–209. The text summarizes replies from foreign governments.
https://archive.org/details/healthexhibition93inte

> "In Sweden, the question of arsenical colours has been the cause of much discussion… **The ordinance of 1876**, which regulated the manufacture, storage, and sale of poisons of all kinds, **unconditionally prohibited the use of arsenic in wall-papers**, blinds, cloth, artificial flowers, and wares of all kinds 'printed or painted in water-colours;' as well as in lamp-shades, sealing-wax, wafers, and candles. A Royal decree amended the clauses of the ordinance of 1876 referring to arsenical goods, removing textile fabrics to the second category, and fixing a limit of permissible impurity…"

The passage later refers to "the decrees of 1879–83" and "the quantity of arsenic prohibited by the law of 1876."

Corroboration: Kulturen (Lund), *Tapeter*: "I Sverige ledde detta 1876 till ett förbud mot handel med tapeter innehållande arsenik… stämpeln 'Arsenikfri'." https://www.kulturen.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Tapeter.pdf

**Verdict: Confirmed, 1876.** The "1879" date comes from the amending decree, which set testing limits. It was not the ban itself. Keep "Sweden in 1876."

## Side findings

- **Orton's title.** *MTG*, 26 Apr 1862, calls him "the Medical Officer of Health." The research file says no source supports that, so this is one period source that does. His own Lancet letter is signed only "Thomas Orton, M.R.C.S." The script's "the surgeon" remains the safe choice.
- **The Lancet quotation** in HISTORY_RESEARCH §3.2 is now verified. "Numerous correspondents from all parts of England made complaints of illness, enclosing green arsenical paper" appears in Orton's letter, Lancet, 8 Nov 1862, p. 517.
