## 0:00 | The Tear Gas Headline
*`ch01_cold_open.txt`*

Tear gas. On Halloween.

This is a real front page from North Dakota, the morning after Halloween, 1933. Look at the headline. "Tear Gas Used to Halt Pranks." The night before, about 300 boys had piled junk cars and garbage in front of the high school. So the police gassed them.

This Saturday, about six in ten American adults will stand at their front door and hand out candy. Around four billion dollars of it.

[curious] So how did *that* turn into *this*?

Here's the short answer. *Trick-or-treating started as a bribe.* And almost everything you think of as Halloween started as a grown-up's plan to stop it.

We'll get to the bribe. And to one Halloween night in Texas that turned parents against the very candy they'd invented to keep the peace.

But first, we need a vegetable. And it's not a pumpkin.

## 0:59 | The Night the Dead Came Home
*`ch02_the_night_the_dead_came_home.txt`*

Ireland, about two hundred years ago. It's the last night of October, and it's one of the biggest nights of the year. The harvest is in. Winter starts tomorrow. And the dead, people believed, are coming home.

So families kept the fire going and left food out. The seats closest to the fire were saved for the family's dead. [dry] Which is a very polite way to be haunted.

But mostly, it was a party. Look at this painting. It's a real Halloween party in County Cork, painted by an Irish artist who was there. People ducking for apples in a tub of water. Games, food, and fortune-telling.

The big fortune of the night was who you'd marry. There was a fruit bread called {barmbrack} with a ring baked inside. Find the ring in your slice, and you'd be married within the year. [wry] Find it with your teeth, and you'd be seeing a dentist.

Kids carried lanterns made from turnips, with a face carved in and a candle inside. Look at this one. It's in a museum in Ireland. Honestly? Way creepier than a pumpkin.

People went out in disguise, faces blacked with soot, using fake voices so nobody knew who they were. Which came in handy, because there was mischief. Gates lifted off their hinges. Cabbages thrown at doors. Chimneys stuffed up so the house filled with smoke.

[light] Hold onto that list. It's about to cross an ocean.

You may have heard that all this goes back two thousand years, to a Celtic festival called Samhain. Historians argue about that. The name comes from a Christian holiday, {All Hallows' Eve}, and the line back to anything ancient is messier than the internet says.

## 2:44 | Coffin Ships and Pumpkins
*`ch03_coffin_ships_and_pumpkins.txt`*

Then, in 1845, a disease hit Ireland's potatoes. The plants turned black, and the potatoes rotted in the ground. And for millions of poor Irish families, potatoes were most of what they ate.

[serious] That's the {Great Famine}. About a million people died. Around two million left Ireland, and more than a million of them crossed the ocean to America, many on ships so crowded and sick that people called them coffin ships.

They landed in cities like Boston and New York, poor, and often not welcome. And they brought their Halloween with them.

Other Americans loved the apple games and the fortunes, and by the end of the 1800s, everybody was throwing Halloween parties. But two things changed along the way.

First, America had pumpkins. Big, cheap, and a lot easier to carve than a turnip. So over time, the {jack-o'-lantern} switched vegetables. If you carve a pumpkin this week, you're doing the American upgrade.

Second, remember those gates off their hinges? The mischief came over too, and in America, it got bigger. Windows soaped. Outhouses tipped over. Sometimes, the stories go, with somebody still inside.

And wagons. Somehow, it was always wagons. One Halloween in Newton, Kansas, people woke up to find a wagon on the roof of the lumberyard. For decades, wagons kept turning up on roofs every November first.

Grown-ups mostly shrugged. Boys will be boys. [dry] Which is not, it turns out, a legal defense.

Then came the Great Depression. And the pranks stopped being funny.

## 4:17 | The Worst Halloween
*`ch04_the_worst_halloween.txt`*

Fargo, North Dakota. Halloween night, 1933. Somebody keeps pulling false fire alarms. Porch lights and streetlamps get shot out. The paper made a point of saying the six boys charged were all from "prominent families."

Across the state, in Bismarck, about 300 boys pile junk cars and garbage in front of the high school. That's when the police bring out the tear gas. That's the headline from the start of this video.

And North Dakota wasn't special. Across the country, Halloween came with smashed windows, pulled-down trolley wires, and streetcar tracks smeared with grease, so the cars slid right past their stops. Some histories call that year *Black Halloween*.

Now stack it up. Teenage boys. No jobs, because it's the Depression. A night when the normal rules don't apply. And towns full of things to break. That means Halloween wasn't really a kids' holiday anymore. *It was a riot in costume.*

Officials in some cities talked about banning it. But how do you ban a night? The kids would come out anyway.

[curious] So if you can't stop Halloween, what do you do?

Some towns came up with a better idea. They decided to pay.

## 5:31 | The Deal
*`ch05_the_deal.txt`*

Here's the plan. If teenagers are going to roam the streets anyway, give them somewhere better to be.

So towns started throwing Halloween parties. Parades. Costume contests with prizes. Haunted houses in school gyms and church basements. Look at this one, from a small town in Washington State. And at home, people started keeping a bowl of treats by the front door.

The kids had a name for the arrangement. The phrase "trick or treat" shows up in Canadian newspapers in the 1920s. In 1927, a paper in Alberta reported kids at the back door and the front, "demanding edible plunder by the word 'trick or treat.'"

[dry] Edible plunder.

And the same report has the town out the next morning, hunting for its wagons. Wagons again. By the 1930s, the phrase was spreading across the United States.

So listen to the words one more time. "Trick or treat" isn't a greeting. *It's a deal.* Give me something, or else. It's a {protection racket}, with better costumes.

And mostly, it worked. A kid busy filling a bag isn't busy tipping over your outhouse.

Then, right as the deal was catching on, America ran out of sugar.

## 6:44 | Sugar Goes to War
*`ch06_sugar_goes_to_war.txt`*

This is a ration book from World War Two. In the spring of 1942, sugar became the first food America {rationed}, meaning each family could only buy a set amount. And it was the last food to come off the list, in 1947. That's five Halloweens with hardly any candy.

So Halloween got pulled way back. Kids were told that pranks helped the enemy. The soap you'd smear on a window, the grease you'd put on the tracks? The war needed those. Some towns held scrap-metal drives on Halloween night instead.

And Chicago went further. In 1942, its city council voted to cancel Halloween for the rest of the war. [wry] Whether anybody actually told the kids is a different question.

Then the war ended. Sugar came back, and so did the soldiers. Then came the {baby boom}: millions of new kids, growing up in brand-new suburbs where the houses sat close together, every front door a few steps from the next. If you're five years old with a pillowcase, that's not a neighborhood. That's a buffet.

Candy companies noticed. Kids' magazines ran trick-or-treat stories. Even Donald Duck got a trick-or-treat cartoon. By the early 1950s, Halloween belonged to little kids.

[warmly] Quick thing before we go on. If you're enjoying this, hit subscribe. It's free, and unlike 1943, I'm not asking for your sugar.

Halloween had never been safer. Which is exactly when parents got scared.

## 8:12 | The Night in Texas (quiet chapter)
*`ch07_the_night_in_texas.txt`*

[serious] Halloween, 1974. A suburb outside Houston, Texas. An eight-year-old boy goes trick-or-treating with his sister and some friends.

That night, before bed, he eats a candy straw from his bag. Within an hour, he's dead. It was laced with cyanide, a deadly poison.

His father told police that a stranger at a dark house must have handed it out. The whole country heard that story.

[pause] But there was no stranger. The poison came from his father. He was deep in debt, and he'd taken out life insurance on his children. He was convicted of murder, and executed almost ten years later.

That's the night in Texas from the beginning of this video.

But the fear had already spread, and it kept growing. Hospitals offered to X-ray kids' candy. Parents threw out apples and anything homemade. Every October, the same warning: check the candy, and don't trust strangers.

[serious] So here's the question. How many kids has a stranger's Halloween candy actually hurt?

How do we know? One sociologist has tracked newspaper reports of poisoned or tampered Halloween treats since 1958. That's almost seventy years, and about 130 reports. Most turned out to be hoaxes, or a pin found before anyone was hurt.

*And he has never found a single child killed or seriously hurt by candy from a stranger.* The one boy who really was poisoned by Halloween candy was poisoned by his own father.

The fear stuck anyway, and it got a fix of its own. Factory-wrapped candy only. Parents walking along behind. Trunk-or-treat in church parking lots. Another scare. Another fix. Another tradition.

So was any of this a good idea? It depends who you ask.

## 9:55 | Treat or Shakedown?
*`ch08_treat_or_shakedown.txt`*

Not everybody liked the deal, even back then. In 1948, a letter to the Washington Post said trick-or-treating was "making gangsters of children." The writer wanted the city to fine parents who sent their kids out to beg.

[wry] And honestly? She had a point. We did just spend this whole video calling it a protection racket.

But to towns that had been using tear gas on teenagers fifteen years earlier, it looked like a miracle. The streets got quieter. Kids got a safe night out. Both of those things were true at once.

[thoughtful] So let's go back to the question. How did the most destructive night of the year turn into a candy holiday for little kids?

The turnip crossed the ocean and turned into a pumpkin. The pranks got worse, until police used tear gas. So towns made a deal: treats at the door, and leave the house alone. The war took the sugar. The suburbs gave it back. And a scare that began with a lie in Texas wrapped it all in plastic.

Every time grown-ups got scared of Halloween, they invented a fix. *And every fix became the tradition.*

So that headline from 1933 isn't the opposite of trick-or-treating. *It's the reason for it.*

[dry] So when a kid knocks on your door this Saturday and says "trick or treat," you might want to pay up.

[warmly] Thanks for watching. If you want more stories like this one, subscribe, and I'll see you next time.

## Fact-check flags

**New in draft 2 (Irish chapters):**
- **Irish Halloween customs** (the dead returning, seats by the fire, food left out; barmbrack with a ring meaning marriage; soot-blackened faces,
  false names and voices; gates off hinges, cabbages thrown at doors, chimneys blocked; bonfires): Marion McGarry, "How Halloween was celebrated in
  Ireland", RTÉ Brainstorm, 20 Oct 2021 (originally Irish Humanities Alliance), https://www.rte.ie/brainstorm/2021/1020/1254854-halloween-ireland-history-traditions-folklore .
  The National Museum of Ireland (folklife) confirms Samhain / Oíche Shamhna as the start of winter with feasting and games.
  "About two hundred years ago" = the 1820s–40s, the period of the painting and of the earliest written folklore. **Add a second source for the
  pranks list** (e.g. the Irish Folklore Commission's Schools' Collection, dúchas.ie, 1937–39).
- **The painting:** Daniel Maclise, *Snap-Apple Night*, exhibited 1833, from a Halloween party in Blarney, Co. Cork, in Oct 1832 that he attended
  (Library Ireland; Blarney history society; the Met's 1845/1853 prints after it). It shows apple-ducking and the snap-apple game. Public domain by age.
  **Image to download** from Wikimedia Commons (rate-limited on 27 Oct).
- **Turnip lanterns:** the museum lantern is in the National Museum of Ireland, Country Life (Castlebar), early 20th century. Carved-vegetable lanterns
  are attested in Ireland and Britain in the 1800s; the word "jack-o'-lantern" for a carved lantern is recorded from 1837 (OED, via Wikipedia).
- **The famine:** potato blight from 1845; about 1 million died; about 2 to 2.1 million left in 1845–55, about 1.5 million overseas, mostly to the US
  (flag [4]). "Coffin ships": the common contemporary name for overcrowded, disease-ridden emigrant ships (period usage; see Irish famine histories).
  "Often not welcome": anti-Irish prejudice in Boston and New York is well documented (avoid "No Irish Need Apply", which is disputed).
- **"For a while … by the end of the 1800s, everybody was throwing Halloween parties":** Nicholas Rogers (*Halloween*, 2002): an ethnic holiday in the
  mid-1800s, a continental one by the end of the century; turn-of-the-century party guides. "Everybody" is loose on purpose (not a statistic).
