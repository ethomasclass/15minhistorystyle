# Paid to Stop: How Halloween Got Bought Off (outline, draft 2, 10-minute cut)

Working title: **Trick-or-Treating Was a Bribe: The Real History of Halloween**
Alts: *Halloween Used to Be a Crime Spree* · *How America Bought Off Halloween*
Target: about **10:00 finished**, about **1,450 words** of narration (165 wpm, plus about a minute of intro, title card,
mark snaps and end screen), **7 chapters**. Written to "Write for someone who knows nothing", "Story loops" and the
series arc beats (skill: `references/script-writing.md`, `references/explainer-craft.md`).

## Driving question (the outer loop)
**How did the most destructive night of the year turn into a candy holiday for little kids?**

**Answer:** The grown-ups paid them off. Then, every time adults got scared of Halloween again, they invented a new fix,
and each fix became the tradition.

**Bigger promise (by ~0:40):** "Almost everything you think of as Halloween started as a grown-up's plan to stop it."

## The long loop (planted in the cold open, closed in chapter 6, about two-thirds in)
"And one Halloween in Texas would turn parents against the very candy they'd invented to keep the peace."

## Hook (recommended version; alternates in draft 1)
On screen at frame 0: an old photo of a farm wagon on top of a barn roof, camera already drifting.
"Trick-or-treating started as a bribe. Look at this. That's a farm wagon. On a roof. Somebody took it apart in the dark,
hauled the pieces up, and put it back together up there. On Halloween, that was normal."

## Chapters (~words · ~time)

### 1 · The Wagon on the Roof (cold open, ~130 words, ~0:50)
- Hook. Describe the photo; turn on the detail.
- Today bridge: millions of kids at doors this Saturday.
- [curious] "So how did *that* turn into *this*?" **(A)** Then the promise.
- Long loop **(B)**.
- Last line: "To get there, we start with a vegetable. And it's not a pumpkin." One-second beat, Chunk intro.

### 2 · Turnips and Tipped Outhouses (~240 words, ~1:30)
- Start in the action: carved turnip lanterns, bonfires, dressing up in Ireland and Scotland.
- **Myth beat** (one sentence pair): the straight line from an ancient festival is messier than the internet says.
- Famine immigrants bring it over. **But** America has pumpkins. **Therefore** the jack-o'-lantern switches vegetables.
- The prank era: gates off hinges, soaped windows, outhouses tipped, the wagon from the opening ("that wagon on the roof").
- Aside [wry]: "boys will be boys". "Which is not a legal defense."
- Last line (opens D): "Then came the Depression. And the pranks stopped being funny."

### 3 · The Worst Halloween (~200 words, ~1:15)
- Greased streetcar tracks, pulled fire alarms, fires in the street (one well-documented city).
- Stack, then "That means": broke, bored young men + a night with no rules + cities full of things to break.
- Some towns talk about a ban. **But** the kids came out anyway.
- Asked aloud (E): "So if you can't stop it, what do you do?"
- Last line: "Some towns found a better answer. They decided to pay."

### 4 · The Deal (~200 words, ~1:15)
- Parades, parties, haunted houses, treats handed out at the door to keep the tricks away.
- "Trick or treat" shows up in print around the late 1920s and spreads in the 1930s (hedged).
- Payoff line: "'Trick or treat' isn't a greeting. It's a deal. Give me something, or else." Closes the bribe.
- Last line (opens F): "It worked. And then, right as it caught on, America ran out of sugar."

### 5 · Sugar Goes to War, Then Comes Home (~210 words, ~1:20)
- A WWII ration book. Candy scarce, Halloween pulled way back (never "cancelled").
- War ends, sugar returns, the baby boom fills the suburbs with kids and houses close together. Candy companies cash in.
  The night moves from teenagers to little kids.
- **Plug (~6:00, about two-thirds in):** [warmly] "Quick thing before we go on. If you're enjoying this, hit subscribe.
  It's free, and unlike 1943, I'm not asking for your sugar."
- Last line: "Halloween had never been safer. Which is exactly when parents got scared."

### 6 · The Night in Texas (the quiet chapter, ~260 words, ~1:35)
- [serious] Slower, no jokes, teal-only quiet mode. 1974: an 8-year-old dies from candy in his own Halloween bag.
  His father blamed a stranger. The poison came from the father. Closes **B**.
- The fear spreads: X-rayed candy, apples thrown out. **G** asked aloud: "So how many kids has a stranger's candy hurt?"
- Say how we know: one sociologist has tracked the reports since 1958. Hoaxes, a few pins, no child killed by a
  stranger's candy.
- The new fix: wrapped candy, supervised trick-or-treating, trunk-or-treat. "Another fix. Another tradition."
- Last line (opens H): "So was any of this a good idea? Depends who you ask."

### 7 · Treat or Shakedown? (~210 words + thank-you, ~1:20)
- Both readings: towns got their streets back and kids got a safe night; some adults at the time called it begging,
  teaching kids to demand. "Both of those were true at once." (Cut the critics line if flag 9 finds no example.)
- [thoughtful] "So let's go back to the question." Answer A through callbacks while the board pulls back: the turnip,
  the wagon, the deal at the door, the ration book, the X-rayed bag.
- Reversal over the opening image: "That wagon on the roof wasn't the opposite of trick-or-treating. It's the reason for it."
- [warmly] "Thanks for watching. If you want more stories like this one, subscribe, and I'll see you next time."
