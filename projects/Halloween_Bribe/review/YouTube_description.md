Why do kids go door to door for candy on Halloween? Because grown-ups were paying them off. In 1933, police in Bismarck, North Dakota, used tear gas on about 300 boys on Halloween night, and the next morning's front page read "Tear Gas Used to Halt Pranks."

This is the real history of Halloween and trick-or-treating: Irish Halloween customs and turnip jack-o'-lanterns, the Great Famine that carried them to America, the prank era of wagons on roofs and tipped outhouses, the 1930s vandalism some call "Black Halloween," the parties and treats towns used to buy the peace, WWII sugar rationing, the postwar candy boom, and the poisoned-candy panic that changed Halloween again. Along the way we look at both sides: critics who said it was "making gangsters of children," and towns that finally got their streets back.

📚 SHOW NOTES
• Ireland, early 1800s: Halloween marked the start of winter. Families left food out for the returning dead, ducked for apples, baked a ring into barmbrack, carried carved turnip lanterns, wore soot disguises, and played pranks: gates off hinges, cabbages at doors, chimneys stuffed up. See Daniel Maclise's "Snap-Apple Night" (1833).
• Samhain: the 2,000-year-old Celtic origin is debated by historians; the name comes from All Hallows' Eve.
• The Great Famine (1845–1852): potato blight; about a million died and about two million left Ireland, more than a million of them for North America, many on overcrowded "coffin ships." They brought their Halloween customs with them, and in America the jack-o'-lantern switched from turnips to pumpkins.
• The prank era: wagons hauled onto roofs (Newton, Kansas, 1892, and many more), soaped windows, tipped outhouses.
• 1933: false alarms in Fargo; tear gas on about 300 boys in Bismarck; greased streetcar tracks elsewhere.
• The deal: town parties kept teens busy, and treats at the door bought the peace. "Trick or treat" appears in Canadian newspapers in the 1920s (Alberta, 1927: kids "demanding edible plunder by the word 'trick or treat'") and spreads across the U.S. in the 1930s.
• WWII: sugar was the first food rationed (1942) and the last off (1947). Kids were told pranks helped the enemy, and Chicago's city council voted to cancel Halloween in 1942.
• After the war: the baby boom and the suburbs made it a little kids' holiday by the early 1950s.
• 1974: an 8-year-old in suburban Houston died from poisoned candy. His father blamed a stranger; the father had done it, for life insurance. Hospitals began X-raying candy.
• The data: sociologist Joel Best has tracked about 130 reports of tampered treats since 1958, mostly hoaxes. He has found no child killed or seriously hurt by a stranger's Halloween candy.
• Both views: a 1948 letter to the Washington Post said trick-or-treating was "making gangsters of children," while towns that had used tear gas fifteen years earlier got quieter streets and a safe night out for kids.
