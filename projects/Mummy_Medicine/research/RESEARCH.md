# When Europe Ate Mummies: research brief and episode outline

**Format:** long episode, about 10–12 minutes (8–9 chapters, ~1,900–2,100 words at the channel's pace).
**Driving question:** *How did eating mummies become medicine, and why was it still for sale in 1924?*
**Short answer:** It started as a mistranslation (a mineral called *mumia* became "mummy"). Demand and fraud kept it going for centuries; science slowly killed it, but it lingered in catalogs into the 20th century, two years after Tutankhamun's tomb was opened.
**Tone:** wry where it's absurd (fakes, Twain), plain and respectful about the dead. These were real people; say so once.

## Key facts (with sourcing status)

**Confirmed**
- *Mūmiyā* (Arabic, from Persian) was a medicinal bitumen; Greek writers called it *pissasphaltus*. "Mummy" in the medicinal sense appears in English around 1400. (Wikipedia "Mummia", citing the OED.)
- Gerard of Cremona (12th c.) translated Arabic medical texts and described mumia in terms of embalmed bodies; natural bitumen supplies were scarce by the 12th century. (Wikipedia; ScienceABC.)
- Egypt banned the export of mummia in the 16th century; afterward apothecaries sold fakes made from recent corpses. Ambroise Paré (d. 1590) exposed the trade. (Wikipedia.)
- Francis I of France reportedly carried mumia mixed with rhubarb. Paracelsus, Oswald Croll and Robert Boyle promoted it. (Wikipedia.)
- England levied an import duty on mumia in the 17th century. (Journal article citing Sugg.)
- 1718: John Quincy calls it "quite out of use in Prescription." (Wikipedia.)
- Merck's price list still offered *Mumia vera aegyptiaca* in 1924, at 12 gold marks per kilogram; Merck's own archive holds a box of it. (Wikipedia; EMD Group / Merck corporate history.)
- Charles II's "King's Drops," a tincture of human skull in alcohol; Thomas Willis mixed powdered skull with chocolate; usnea (moss from skulls); human fat for wounds and gout; blood drunk at executions; last known scaffold attempt in Germany in 1908. (Smithsonian, after Sugg and Noble.)
- Montaigne's "Of Cannibals" contrasted European practices with those Europeans called savage. (Smithsonian.)
- Mummy brown pigment: ground mummy with white pitch and myrrh, sold by London colourmen in the 18th–19th centuries, tubes reportedly sold into the 1960s. (Art UK; FSU Art History.)
- Twain, *The Innocents Abroad* (1869): the locomotives-burning-mummies line, hedged ("stated to me for a fact"); no evidence it happened. (Straight Dope; Benjamin Radford, *Skeptical Inquirer*.)
- Mummy paper: Isaiah Deck's 1847 Egypt trip and his proposal; the Syracuse newspaper "printed on mummy rags" claim traced to a paper that said "Rags from Egypt." (Wikipedia "Mummy paper".)

**To verify before scripting**
- Guy de la Fontaine's Alexandria visit: the often-quoted date 1564 and the dealer's confession (the source is Pomet, 1694; get the passage itself).
- The "King's Drops" origin story (said to be bought from Jonathan Goddard); confirm with Sugg.
- Whether a specific American apothecary sold mumia (a colonial or early U.S. price list would make a strong U.S. tie-in).
- Burne-Jones burying his tube of mummy brown (often told; trace to Rudyard Kipling's or Georgiana Burne-Jones's account).
