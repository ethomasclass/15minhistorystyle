## 1. Guy de la Fontaine in Alexandria (1564): CONFIRMED, details need correcting

- **Original telling:** Ambroise Paré, *Discours d'Ambroise Paré … à sçavoir, de la mumie, des venins, de la licorne et de la peste* (Paris: Gabriel Buon, **1582**), "Discours de la Mumie", ch. VII, ff. 7–8. Scan: https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_06290
- **Quote (Paré, 1582):** "Gui de la Fontaine, Medecin celebre du Roy de Navarre … qu'estant **l'an mil cinq cens soixante quatre** en la ville d'Alexandrie d'Egypte, il ouyt dire qu'il y avoit un **Juif** qui en faisoit grand trafic … luy ouvrit un magazin, où il y avoit plusieurs corps entassez les uns sur les autres."
  - The dealer said that "il n'y avoit point **quatre ans**" since he had prepared all the bodies himself. There were "**trente ou quarante**" of them, and they were "**corps d'esclaves, ou autres personnes**".
  - Fontaine asked whether any had died "de lepre, verole, ou peste". The dealer answered that he did not care "de quelle mort ils estoient morts, ou s'ils estoient vieils ou ieunes, masles ou femelles".
  - The dealer also said he marvelled that Christians were "tant friands de manger les corps des morts".
  - He described his method: he emptied out the brain and entrails, cut deep slits in the muscles, packed them with asphalt, wrapped the body, and left it to dry for two or three months.
- **Retold by Pomet:** *Histoire générale des drogues* (Paris, 1694), Book I of the animals section, pp. 6–7. Scan: https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_pharma_000056. English translation: *A Compleat History of Druggs* (London, 1712). Scan: https://archive.org/details/b30497115_0001. Pomet's version ends: "he took such Bodies as he cou'd get, whether they dy'd of a common Disease, or of some Contagion … which after he had dry'd in an Oven, he sent into Europe; and that he was amaz'd to see the Christians were Lovers of such Filthiness."
- **Corrections to the outline:**
  - Paré names the year as 1564.
  - Fontaine was physician to the **King of Navarre** in Paré's version. Pomet calls him "the King's Physician".
  - The **"criminals"** detail is not in the Fontaine story. In a separate passage of his surgery, Paré says French apothecaries stole **hanged men's bodies** at night, dried them in ovens and sold them as mummy.
  - "Plague" appears only as a cause of death the dealer said he did not care about.
- **Both sources say the dealer was a Jew.** The script should not repeat this as an ethnic detail; see #6.
- **Safe narration:** "In 1564 a French royal physician, Guy de la Fontaine, visited a mummy dealer in Alexandria. The dealer opened a storeroom stacked with thirty or forty bodies and laughed at the idea that they were ancient. He said he had prepared every one of them himself in the last four years, from slaves and anyone else he could get. He didn't care whether they had died of leprosy, the pox or plague. Ambroise Paré published the story in 1582."

## 2. Charles II's King's Drops: PARTLY CONFIRMED (the price is unproven)

- **Confirmed by primary sources:** Charles II kept "King's Drops" and took them on the first morning of his fatal illness (2 February 1685). His physicians later dosed him with **"Spirit of human Skull 40 drops"**.
  - Source: Raymond Crawfurd, *The Last Days of Charles II* (Oxford, 1909), which translates Sir Charles Scarburgh's manuscript. Scan: https://archive.org/details/lastdaysofcharle00craw
  - Crawfurd describes the drops as "the famous 'King's Drops': these were a volatile extract of bone, made up in the King's own laboratory after a formula devised by Dr. Jonathan Goddard".
- **The recipe:** human skull, "of a person hanged or dead of some violent death", plus dried viper, hartshorn and ivory, distilled. Secondary source: J. C. Goddard, *History of Urology*, 2015, https://urologynews.uk.com/features/history-of-urology/post/goddard-s-dropps-a-paradox-of-the-c17th. Goddard's recipes were printed as "Arcana Goddardiana" in the *Pharmacopoeia Bateana* (1691).
  - Martin Lister claimed instead that Goddard's drops were spirit of raw silk, so the skull recipe is not uncontested.
- **The price is reported inconsistently:**
  - Sugg, *History Ireland*: "possibly for as much as £6,000". https://historyireland.com/?p=8852
  - Urology News: "£1500 (some said £6000)".
  - *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, 3rd edition (1797), "Goddard": "sold to Charles II. for 5000l." https://jimclifford.ca/britannica-articles-1/a/EB.5/149981670/6.html
  - No 17th-century source for any figure was found.
- **Safe narration:** "Charles II reportedly bought the recipe from the physician Jonathan Goddard (later writers claimed for thousands of pounds) and made the drops in his own laboratory. They became known as the King's Drops. The key ingredient was distilled human skull." Do not state £6,000 as fact. If used, say "by one account, as much as £6,000".

## 3. Burne-Jones buries his mummy brown: CONFIRMED

- **Primary source 1:** Georgiana Burne-Jones, *Memorials of Edward Burne-Jones*, vol. II (London, 1904), p. 114, under the year **1881**. Scan: https://archive.org/details/memorialsofedwar02burn
  - Quote: "One of these times was remembered by us all as the day of the funeral of a tube of mummy-paint … Mr. Tadema startled us by saying he had lately been invited to go and see a mummy that was in his colourman's workshop before it was ground down into paint. Edward scouted the idea … but when assured that it was actually compounded of real mummy, he left us at once, hastened to the studio, and returning with the only tube he had, insisted on our giving it decent burial there and then. So a hole was bored in the green grass at our feet … and the spot was marked by one of the girls planting a daisy root above it."
  - The scene is the Grange garden in Fulham, and the Alma-Tademas were at Sunday lunch.
- **Primary source 2:** Rudyard Kipling, *Something of Myself* (1937), ch. 1. Scan: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.3472
  - Quote: "once he descended in broad daylight with a tube of 'Mummy Brown' in his hand, saying that he had discovered it was made of dead Pharaohs and we must bury it accordingly. So we all went out and helped—according to the rites of Mizraim and Memphis, I hope—and—to this day I could drive a spade within a foot of where that tube lies."
- **Note:** Kipling, who was Burne-Jones's nephew by marriage, tells it as a childhood memory. Georgiana's memoir places it in 1881, when he was 15. It is fine to cite both without giving a date, or to use "1881, according to his wife's memoir".
- **Safe narration:** "When Burne-Jones learned at Sunday lunch that his mummy brown really was made from mummies, he fetched his tube and buried it in the garden. One of his daughters planted a daisy on the spot."

## 4. America

**4a. Mumia sold by colonial or early-US apothecaries: UNCONFIRMED for the colonial period; CONFIRMED for 1889**

- **Colonial period:** no 18th-century American advertisement, inventory or price list naming mummy was found. Checked:
  - J. Worth Estes's glossary of drugs dispensed in colonial New England, built from physicians' ledgers (Colonial Society of Massachusetts, vol. 57). It has no "mummy" entry; mummy powder appears only inside a Paracelsian recipe. https://www.colonialsociety.org/node/1218
  - Griffenhagen, *Drug Supplies in the American Revolution*.
  - Ford, *The Apothecary in Eighteenth-Century Williamsburg*.
  - This does not prove mummy was absent. Colonial shops stocked English materia medica, and the London lists still carried "Mumia". But there is no source we can cite.
- **Earliest US-market listing found:** Merck's corporate archive says the **first American edition of *Merck's Index* (New York, 1889)** offered the drug "expressly as **Mummy, true Egyptian**". Source: https://www.emdgroup.com/en/stories/powdered-mummies-used-as-medicine.html
  - The 1889 scan (https://archive.org/details/b33764906) has an entry between "Muira puama" and "Murexid" that is consistent with this, but the OCR is garbled.
- **Also documented:** Benjamin West gave a mummified arm and hand to the Library Company of Philadelphia in **1767**, "one of the first mummy specimens to arrive in the colonies". This was a curiosity, not a medicine. Source: S. J. Wolfe, *Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia*, https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/mummies/
- **Safe narration:** "Mummy was still on the market in America in 1889, when the first American edition of Merck's drug index listed 'Mummy, true Egyptian'." Avoid any claim about colonial apothecaries.

**4b. Padihershef, 1823: CONFIRMED**

- Padihershef arrived in Boston in 1823 as a gift of Van Lennep & Co. of Smyrna to Massachusetts General Hospital. The trustees called him "an appropriate ornament of the operating room."
- He was exhibited for profit: first in Boston, then on a tour from 1823 to 1824 through New York, Richmond, Charleston, Augusta, Savannah, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Albany and Rhode Island.
- Admission was 25¢, according to Harvard Magazine.
- He is still in the Ether Dome.
- A November 1823 newspaper called him "the only entire one ever exhibited in the United States". Harvard Magazine says "one of the first complete Egyptian mummies to reach the United States".
- Sources:
  - https://mummymania.omeka.net/exhibits/show/in-the-beginning/an-approprate-ornament-of-the-
  - https://mummymania.omeka.net/exhibits/show/in-the-beginning/padihershef-on-the-road
  - https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2014/01/from-egypt-to-the-ether-dome
- **Safe narration:** "one of the first complete Egyptian mummies in the United States". Avoid saying "the first".

**4c. Mummy paper: PARTLY CONFIRMED (the proposals and claims are real; actual production is disputed)**

- **Isaiah Deck:** "On a Supply of Paper Material from the Mummy Pits of Egypt", *Transactions of the American Institute … for 1854* (Albany, 1855), pp. 83–93. This is a real proposal.
- **Augustus Stanwood of Gardiner, Maine:** the story that he made brown wrapping paper from mummy wrappings rests on one source. Dard Hunter, *Papermaking* (1947), had it second-hand from Stanwood's son. It is oral history.
- ***Syracuse Daily Standard*, 31 July 1856:** the paper said it was printed on stock made from rags imported from Egypt, supplied by G. W. Ryan and milled at Marcellus Falls. Joel Munsell later said the rags came from mummies. Scholars dispute the reading.
- **Skeptic:** Joseph Dane, "The Curse of the Mummy Paper", *Printing History* 17.2 (1995).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummy_paper and https://mummymania.omeka.net/exhibits/show/mummy-paper-no-longer-an-urban/who-came-up-with-the-crazy-ide
- **Safe narration:** "In the 1850s an American geologist seriously proposed pulping mummy wrappings for paper. A Syracuse newspaper boasted it was printed on rags from Egypt, and a Maine papermaker was later said to have turned mummy linen into wrapping paper. Historians still argue over how much of it really happened."

## 5. Recipe using a red-haired man's corpse: CONFIRMED. Croll first, Schroeder copying him

- **First source: Oswald Croll, *Basilica Chymica*** (first edition Frankfurt, 1609), in the recipe "Theriaca de Mumia". Checked in the 1635 edition: https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_pharma_011283
  - Latin: "Eligatur Cadaver hominis rufi, integri, recentis sine macula, viginti quatuor annorum, & suspensi, vel rota contriti, vel hastati, per diem & noctem tempore sereno collectum. Hanc Mumiam, a duobus Luminaribus semel illuminatam ac constellatam, frustulatim … conscinde, & … pulvere Myrrhae & tantillo saltem Aloes … imbibe macerando aliquot dies in Spiritu vini … tunc assimilatur carni fumo duratae sine faetore."
  - Our translation: "Choose the corpse of a red-haired man, whole, fresh, without blemish, twenty-four years old, who has been hanged, broken on the wheel or speared, left out for a day and a night in clear weather … this Mummy, once shone upon by the two Luminaries [sun and moon], cut into small pieces, [sprinkle] with powdered myrrh and a little aloes, steep for some days in spirit of wine … it becomes like smoked meat, without stench."
- **Copied by Johann Schroeder, *Pharmacopoeia Medico-Chymica*** (first edition **Ulm, 1641**, not 1656; later editions followed). Scan: https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_y7Syem6XhHsC
  - Schroeder adds the reason: "quia in hoc sanguis habetur tenuior, adeoque & caro praestantior" (because such a man's blood is thinner and his flesh better). He also generalises the manner of death to "morte violenta" (a violent death).
- **English translation:** *The Compleat Chymical Dispensatory* (London, 1669), Book V. Scan: https://archive.org/details/b30342065
  - Quote: "A fifth sort is the modern Mummy: Chuse the Carcass of a red-hair'd man (whose blood is thinner and flesh better) whole and sound, of twenty four years old, not dying of a disease, but killed; let it lye a day and night in the Air in clear weather, cut the flesh in pieces, and add powder of Mirrh, and a little Aloes, imbibe it by steeping in Spirit of Wine … then hang up the pieces in a dry Air in the shade, and they will be as if smoaked, and not stink. **Croll.**"
  - Schroeder credits Croll himself.
- **On Alphahistory:** its attribution to "Schroeder 1656" is a later edition of a borrowed recipe.
- **Safe narration:** "The German physician Oswald Croll published the recipe in 1609, and it was copied into standard pharmacopoeias for decades afterwards."

## 6. Pomet on European-made fake mummies: CONFIRMED. Flag the antisemitic framing

- **Pomet, 1694** (pp. 6–7). English edition of 1712: "We may daily see **the Jews carrying on their Rogueries** as to these Mummies, and after them the Christians; for the Mummies that are brought from **Alexandria, Egypt, Venice and Lyons**, are nothing else but the Bodies of People that dye several Ways, whether bury'd or unbury'd, that are afterwards embowell'd, and have their several Cavities fill'd with the Powder, or rather Sweepings of Myrrh, Caballine Aloes, **Bitumen, Pitch**, and other Gums … the Bodies being thus prepar'd, are put into **an Oven** to consume all their Moisture … sold here for true Egyptian Mummies to those who know no better."
- **Also from Pomet:** the best grade should be "fine shining Black, not full of Bones or Dirt", but "its greatest Use is for **catching Fish**."
- ***Household Words*, vol. XV, no. 362 (28 February 1857), "Mummy", pp. 196–197**, Dickens's weekly. It quotes this passage and the Fontaine story verbatim from the 1712 English edition. It also quotes Pomet on English druggists bringing moss-covered skulls "from Ireland".
  - Scan: https://archive.org/details/sim_household-words-a-weekly-journal-by-charles-dickens_1857-02-28_15_362
  - The same text is on Dickens Journals Online, Household Words vol. XV, p. 197 (djo.org.uk).
- **Note for the script:** Paré (1582) names the Alexandria dealer as "un Juif". Pomet (1694) frames the whole fake-mummy trade as Jewish "roguery", although his own sentence adds "and after them the Christians". This was a stock early-modern antisemitic trope; Christian apothecaries in France are described doing the same thing (Paré, #1). Recommended wording is "a dealer in Alexandria" and "dealers in Alexandria, Venice and Lyon". Do not say "Jewish dealers".
- **Safe narration:** "The French royal druggist Pierre Pomet complained that 'mummies' arriving from Alexandria, Venice and even Lyon were just recent corpses. They were gutted, stuffed with bitumen and pitch, and dried in ovens."

## 7. John Sanderson, 600 lb of mummy, 1586: CONFIRMED

- **Source:** Samuel Purchas, *Purchas His Pilgrimes* (London, 1625), Part II, Book 9. Reprinted in *Hakluytus Posthumus or Purchas His Pilgrimes* (Glasgow: MacLehose, 1905), vol. IX, pp. 418–419, margin dated **1586**. Scan: https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.21183
- **Quote:** "The Momia, which is some five or sixe miles beyond [the pyramids], are thousands of imbalmed bodies … we were let downe by ropes, as into a Well, with Waxe candles burning in our hands, and so walked upon the bodies … I broke of all the parts of the bodies to see how the flesh was turned to drugge, and brought home divers heads, hands, armes, and feete for a shew: wee brought also **600. pounds for the Turkie Companie in Pieces**: and brought into England in the **Hercules**: together with a whole body … One little hand I brought into England … my brother … gave the same to a Doctor in Oxford."
- **Wording note:** In 1586 it was the **Turkey Company**, which became the Levant Company in 1592. "Levant Company merchant" describes Sanderson's career fine, but the shipment was for the Turkey Company.
- The site described is Saqqara.
- Full edition: W. Foster, ed., *The Travels of John Sanderson in the Levant 1584–1602* (Hakluyt Society, 1931).

## A. Merck's 1924 price list: CONFIRMED (secondary sources; the price list itself was not seen)

- **Merck KGaA corporate history:** "The company continues to sell »Mumia vera Aegyptiaca« into the 20th century. The price list published in 1924 values it at **12 gold marks per kilo**." https://www.emdgroup.com/en/company/history/corporate-history-stories/corporate-history-stories-m1.html
- **The same company also says:** "As late as 1924, a kilogram of mummy powder cost 12 gold marks, according to the price list of Firma E. Merck." https://www.emdgroup.com/en/stories/powdered-mummies-used-as-medicine.html
- **Independent scholarly source:** Beatrix Geßler-Löhr, "Mumia vera aegyptiaca im Abendland" (IBAES): "Noch 1924 wurde 'Mumia vera Aegyptica' von der Darmstädter Firma Merck als Droge zum Kilopreis von 12 Goldmark angeboten." https://ibaes.de/ibaes1/GesslerLoehr/text2.pdf
- **Safe narration:** "As late as 1924, Merck's price list offered 'Mumia vera aegyptiaca', genuine Egyptian mummy, at 12 gold marks a kilo."

## B. Quincy 1718: CONFIRMED

- **Source:** John Quincy, *Pharmacopoeia Officinalis & Extemporanea: or, a Compleat English Dispensatory* (London, 1718), §317, p. 143. Scan: https://archive.org/details/b30540239
- **Quote:** "317. Mummia, Mummy. This is the Flesh of Carcasses which have been embalm'd. But although it yet retains a Place in medicinal Catalogues, it is quite out of use in Prescription."
