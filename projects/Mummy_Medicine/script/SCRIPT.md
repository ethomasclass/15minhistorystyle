# When Europe Ate Mummies
**15 Minute History** · narration script · 1436 words · 8,326 characters (about 8,326 ElevenLabs credits) · about 8:42 with the intro

**Driving question:** How did eating the dead become medicine, and how did it last until 1924?
**Answer:** It began as a translation mistake that turned a mineral into a body. Demand did the rest: when real mummies ran out, dealers made fakes, and doctors wrote recipes for fresh ones. When medicine moved on, mummies became party tricks, a railroad joke and a paint color, and some of that paint may still be hanging in galleries.

## 0:00 | Cold open: While Supplies Last

In 1903, the German drug company Merck printed its price list. Page after page of drugs and chemicals. And right under the musk, there's this line. *Mumia vera aegyptiaca.* Genuine Egyptian mummy. Seventeen marks fifty a kilo. *While supplies last.*

Supplies lasted. In 1924, two years after Tutankhamun's tomb was found, it was still in the catalog.

For centuries, Europeans ate mummies as medicine. Kings did it. Scientists did it. And when the real mummies ran out, they didn't stop. They found fresher ones.

So here's the question. How did eating the dead become medicine? And how did it last until 1924?

It starts with a black, sticky rock.

## 0:59 | The Typo

In Persian and Arabic, {mumiya} was a mineral. A black, sticky pitch that oozed out of the mountains of Persia. Doctors in the Islamic world prescribed it for bruises and broken bones. No one was in it.

But the ancient Egyptians had used black resins and pitch on their dead. So when outsiders saw those dark, glossy bodies, they made a connection. Black stuff in the mountain. Black stuff on the mummy. Same thing, right?

In the 1100s, a scholar named Gerard of Cremona translated Arabic medical books into Latin, the language of Europe's doctors. And he described mumia as the stuff found in embalmed bodies.

Historians still argue over exactly who got it wrong first. But the result was the same. Somebody read pitch and wrote *people.* Mumia stopped meaning a mineral. It started meaning the mummy.

---

## 1:48 | The Trade

Real mountain pitch was rare. Egypt had tombs full of the dead. So the supply chain followed the translation.

Men climbed down into old burial shafts near Cairo, broke the bodies into pieces, and sold them to merchants. The pieces went through Alexandria, onto ships, and across the sea to Europe. In 1586, one English merchant shipped 600 pounds of mummy home in a single load. Plus one whole body. In case anyone wanted the full set.

Back home, {apothecaries}, the pharmacists of the day, ground it into powder. You drank it, or rubbed it on as a paste. Bruises, bleeding, coughs, headaches, poison. Mummy was for pretty much everything.

And this wasn't some back-alley cure. King Francis the First of France reportedly carried a pouch of mummy mixed with rhubarb. Everywhere. Just in case. The famous doctor Paracelsus praised it. So, later, did Robert Boyle, one of the founders of modern chemistry.

## 2:51 | The Fakes

There was one problem. Egypt only had so many ancient dead, and its rulers started to restrict the trade. Supply went down. Demand did not.

So the dealers solved the supply problem the way dealers always do. They made more.

In 1564, a French royal physician named Guy de la Fontaine visited a mummy dealer in Alexandria. The dealer showed him thirty or forty bodies, stacked in a storeroom. Then he admitted none of them was ancient. He'd prepared every one himself, in the last four years. Enslaved people, and others. Filled with pitch, wrapped, and dried.

He said he didn't care what they'd died of. Leprosy, pox, plague. It was all mummy to him. Some researchers have even wondered whether fakes like these carried disease into Europe's pharmacies. Nobody knows. But it's hard to rule out.

*Ancient Egyptian mummy.* Aged a full four years.

And the fakes weren't only made in Egypt. In 1694, the French king's own drug expert, Pierre Pomet, complained that mummies were arriving from Venice and Lyon. Recent bodies, stuffed with pitch and dried in an oven. Genuine Egyptian mummy. Made in France.

Pomet's verdict on the finest grade? Its greatest use, he wrote, was for catching fish.

## 4:03 | Fresh Is Best

Then some doctors decided Egypt was overrated anyway.

A popular theory said a body's healing power was strongest in someone young and healthy who died suddenly. By that logic, a fresh body beat a three-thousand-year-old pharaoh. So in 1609, a German chemist named Oswald Croll published a recipe for making your own.

Take the body of a red-haired man. Twenty-four years old. Hanged, or broken on the wheel. Leave him out for a day and a night, under the sun and the moon. Cut the flesh into small pieces. Soak them in myrrh, aloe and spirit of wine. Dry.

That's a real recipe. It was printed in a medical book, and other doctors copied it for decades.

Why red hair? Because a red-haired man's blood was supposed to be thinner, and his flesh better. Which, honestly, is the least disturbing part of that sentence.

And mummy was only the beginning.

## 4:55 | Beyond Mummies

Historians have a name for this. {Medicinal cannibalism}: eating human remains as medicine. And in early modern Europe, it went far beyond mummies.

Powdered human skull was prescribed for epilepsy. Moss that grew on old skulls was scraped off and sold to stop bleeding. Human fat, bought from executioners, was rubbed into wounds and aching joints. A London pharmacy book from 1718 still listed a rub made with "Man's Grease," "Moss of Human Skull," mummy, and human blood.

In 1685, as King Charles the Second of England lay dying, his doctors gave him forty drops of spirit of human skull. He died four days later.

At public executions, people with epilepsy waited by the scaffold with cups, to drink the blood while it was still warm. They believed it would cure them. In Germany, there's a report of someone trying it as late as 1908.

Around 1580, the French writer Michel de Montaigne wrote an essay about the people Europeans called cannibals. His point was simple. Europe did things just as savage, and called them normal. He was writing about torture and war. He could have added the pharmacy.

## 6:01 | The Party

By the 1700s, doctors were losing faith. In 1718, a London pharmacist wrote that mummy was "quite out of use." Europe took that as a suggestion. It stayed in catalogs for two more centuries.

And in the 1800s, Europe and America fell in love with ancient Egypt. The rich found a new thing to do with mummies. Unwrap them. In front of an audience.

In London, a surgeon named Thomas Pettigrew got so famous for it that people called him "Mummy" Pettigrew. Guests bought tickets. Some went home with a scrap of the bandages. Party favors.

In 1850, a London lord sent out invitations. Monday. 144 Piccadilly. "A Mummy from Thebes to be unrolled at half-past Two." Light refreshments, presumably.

That same summer, a lecturer named George Gliddon brought the show to Boston. Five dollars bought four tickets to three lectures. The poster promised the unopened mummy of the "Daughter of the High Priest of Thebes." Some of the city's top doctors and scientists signed up.

Halfway through the unwrapping, the doctors on stage started arguing. By the last lecture, they agreed. The princess was a man.

Gliddon's explanation? The labels must have been switched during embalming. Three thousand years ago. Boston applauded.

---

## 7:11 | The Tube

Not every mummy ended up in a pharmacy or a parlor. In 1869, Mark Twain wrote that Egypt's railroads burned mummies for fuel. He was joking. People repeated it as fact for the next hundred and fifty years.

And then there was paint. For generations, artists bought a rich brown pigment called {mummy brown}. It was made, at least some of the time, from ground-up mummies. In 1888, a Philadelphia art supply catalog still listed it, right there with the other browns.

In 1881, the British painter Edward Burne-Jones found out what was in the tube. Over Sunday lunch. He went out to the garden and gave it a proper burial. One of his daughters planted a daisy on top.

---

## 7:53 | The Answer

So let's go back to the question. How did eating the dead become medicine? And how did it last until 1924?

It started with a translation. A rock became a body. Then demand did the rest. When the real mummies ran out, dealers made fakes. When the fakes weren't enough, doctors wrote recipes for fresh ones. And when medicine finally moved on, mummies became party tricks, a railroad joke and a paint color.

A box of Merck's mummy still sits in the company's archive.

And mummy brown? Artists rarely wrote down which browns they used, and it isn't easy to tell them apart. Which means that somewhere, on a gallery wall, in a gilded frame, there may be a person.
