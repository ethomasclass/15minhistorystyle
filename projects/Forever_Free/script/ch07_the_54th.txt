About 178,000 Black men served in the Union army, roughly one in ten Union soldiers. Thousands more served in the navy. Many had escaped slavery only months before.

They served in separate units, mostly under white officers. And until 1864, they were paid less than white soldiers. Some regiments refused to take any pay at all until it was equal.

July 1863. The 54th Massachusetts, one of the first Black regiments raised in the North, leads an attack on Fort Wagner, guarding Charleston Harbor. They don't take the fort. Nearly half the men who charged are killed, wounded or captured. But newspapers across the North report how they fought.

Frederick Douglass, who had escaped slavery himself, urged Black men to enlist. Two of his own sons joined the 54th. Once a Black man wore the uniform and carried a musket, Douglass said, "there is no power on earth" that could deny he had earned the right to citizenship.

And Robert Smalls? He went on to pilot ships for the Union navy. After the war, the voters of South Carolina sent him to Congress.
