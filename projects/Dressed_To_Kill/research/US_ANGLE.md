## 1. Robert C. Kedzie, *Shadows from the Walls of Death* (Michigan State Board of Health, 1874)

### What it is
- **Full title:** *"Shadows from the Walls of Death;" Facts and Inferences Prefacing a Book of Specimens of Arsenical Wall Papers, Gathered by R. C. Kedzie, Member of State Board of Health*. "Presented by the State Board of Health, of Michigan." Printed in Lansing by W. S. George & Co. [PRIMARY: scan of the title page]
- **Contents:** a cover letter from the Board, an eight-page essay by Kedzie, and **86 leaves of real wallpaper**. Kedzie tested every sample and found arsenic in each one. The NLM catalog record reads: "8 pages, 86 leaves : 42 cm … Contains 86 leaves of arsenical wallpaper samples." [PRIMARY/SECONDARY: archive.org metadata from the NLM]
  - https://archive.org/details/0234555.nlm.nih.gov
  - Metadata: https://archive.org/metadata/0234555.nlm.nih.gov
- **Where the samples came from:** "they were selected from stores in Lansing, Detroit, and Jackson, and were all on sale in these cities." (Kedzie's preface) [PRIMARY]
- **Size:** NLM gives 42 cm (about 16.5 in) tall. Michiganology says 22" x 30". **Use the NLM figure.** 22 x 30 inches looks like an error, or perhaps the size of an open spread.
- **Epigraph on the title page:** Leviticus 14 on "the plague … in the walls of the house, with hollow streaks, greenish or reddish" [PRIMARY]. This works well on screen.
- **Who Kedzie was:** a chemistry professor at Michigan Agricultural College (now Michigan State University) from 1863 until he died in 1902, and president of the Michigan State Board of Health 1877–81. [SECONDARY: MSU "On the Banks" page https://onthebanks.msu.edu/Object/162-565-3181/robert-c-kedzie]
  - He was an assistant surgeon with the 12th Michigan Infantry from Jan. 15, 1862 (MSU source).
  - He was **captured at Shiloh in April 1862** [SECONDARY: https://kevinforsyth.net/ELMAC/robert-kedzie/]. Other secondary sources agree, but the MSU page I could reach does not mention Shiloh. **Check this before using it.** It would be a nice APUSH hook: a Civil War surgeon turned public-health crusader.

### Print run, distribution, destruction
- **Print run: 100 copies, sent to Michigan public libraries.** Michiganology says so: https://michiganology.org/stories/shadows-from-the-walls-of-death. So does NLM Circulating Now: https://circulatingnow.nlm.nih.gov/2018/05/07/facts-and-inferences-digitizing-shadows-from-the-walls-of-death-part-1/ [SECONDARY]
- **The cover letter from Board Secretary Henry B. Baker** gives the purpose in the Board's own words [PRIMARY: NLM scan, first leaves]:
  > "These books of Specimens are distributed to the leading public libraries throughout this State, in the hope that by so doing the people will be more effectually warned of this source of danger to health…"
  - The letter is dated Lansing, Nov. 1874. The OCR is garbled, so check the date on the page image.
- **Why most copies were destroyed:** librarians did not want to poison patrons, so they threw the books out or destroyed them (Michiganology). NLM puts it this way: "Out of concern for patron health, most copies have been discarded or destroyed." (Circulating Now Part 1) [SECONDARY]
- **The NLM's own copy** was sent in 1874 to John Shaw Billings, then head of the Library of the Surgeon General's Office, which later became the NLM. (Circulating Now Part 1) [SECONDARY]

### The exact preface wording (verified from the NLM scan)
The NLM PDF (https://archive.org/download/0234555.nlm.nih.gov/0234555.pdf) shows this text. The OCR is cleaned up here; check against the page images.

- **The cold-open line, from Baker's cover letter:**
  > "The poisonous nature of the paper of which this book is made will suggest to you the propriety of not allowing it to be handled by children."
- **Main heading of the essay:** "ARSENICAL WALL PAPER. SHALL WE POISON OUR HOMES?"
- **How the poison gets out of the paper:**
  > "Perhaps we could not devise a more effectual way to contaminate the air of our homes with a small amount of arsenical dust, than by the use of wall paper colored with arsenical preparations."
- **The invalid who "goes into a decline":**
  > "…finally succumbs to consumption,—a consumption of arsenic in every breath she inhales!"
- **On a big wallpaper firm's sample book:**
  > "Many of these papers were very beautiful and very dangerous…"
- **Who is to blame:**
  > "…the manufacturers cannot enter a plea of ignorance, for they know the materials employed and the danger of their use."
  - Kedzie cites Draper's Massachusetts report at this point (see section 2). That gives us a direct Massachusetts-to-Michigan link.
- **The law alone won't fix it:**
  > "…any legal enactment on this subject, not sustained by an enlightened public sentiment, will remain a dead letter upon the statute book."
- **Closing of the case list:**
  > "…sufficient of themselves to arouse the attention of all who love life and hate death."
- **Home test:** wet the paper with ammonia water and drop in a crystal of silver nitrate. "If a yellow precipitate forms around the crystal it indicates the presence of arsenic."
- The preface is signed "R. C. Kedzie, Chairman of Committee on Poisons, etc.", Agricultural College, Lansing, Sept. 26 (OCR reads "2fi"), 1874.

### The real Michigan cases in the book [PRIMARY]
1. **"Dr. I. H. B., Lansing, member of House of Representatives for this district in 1873, and his two boys."** The paper held 5.47 grains of arsenic per square foot, "or six ounces of arsenic on the walls of a single room." Everyone recovered once the paper came down.
   - **Probable identity: Dr. Ira H. Bartholomew.** He was a Lansing physician, three-term mayor from 1863, a one-session state representative for Ingham 1, and had sons. Source: Library of Michigan legislator database, https://mdoe.state.mi.us/legislators/Legislator/LegislatorDetail/1863 [WEAK: the initials, town, office and profession all match, but no source names him]
2. **The children of "Hon. L. D. W., of Manchester, formerly State Senator."** Emma, 9, got sick in a green-papered bedroom and recovered after she was moved out. Later her brother Herbert, 6, was put in the same room and showed the same symptoms. He also recovered. (The father's identity is not found.)
3. Mrs. J. of Saginaw, an invalid who improved whenever she was away from home.
4. Mr. H., a prominent lawyer of Mason, whose wife and all his children were chronically ill until the paper was removed.
5. **The death:** a letter of July 2, 1874 from Dr. L. A. Foote of Spring Port, Jackson County.
   - A girl "aged about ten years" died after doctors diagnosed "spinal fever" and then "rheumatism of the heart." The walls "in nearly every room were covered with green paper."
   - Kedzie found arsenic in the samples. He writes: **"A specimen of this paper is bound up in the book of specimens, being the first specimen leaf of the book."** That sample came from the room next to the girl's bedroom.
   - **This is the strongest single fact for the cold open: the first page you turn to in the book is wallpaper from the house where a child died.** Precise wording: "from the room next to hers."

### Is the "book that can kill you" claim accurate?
- **NLM's own testing (2018):** Brian Czarnecki, a U.S. Public Health Service industrial hygienist with the NIH Division of Occupational Health & Safety, sampled the room, the scanner, the operator's gloves and the air. Source: Circulating Now Part 2, https://circulatingnow.nlm.nih.gov/2018/05/09/facts-and-inferences-digitizing-shadows-from-the-walls-of-death-part-2/ [SECONDARY]
  - Arsenic **does** transfer to gloves and equipment.
  - Arsenic in the air was **below detection**, so no respirator was required.
  - Their advice: "the use of gloves is highly recommended to minimize contact with skin, as transfer is cumulative," and wipe down the equipment afterward.
  - The scanning operator wore a hooded Tyvek suit, a dust mask and nitrile gloves.
- **University of Michigan:** "has all leaves encapsulated for safety, and can be handled only with gloves." Source: Historical Bibliography of medicine entry, https://historyofmedicine.com/id/11830 [SECONDARY]
- **MSU:** "each wallpaper specimen has been individually encapsulated." Source: the MSU Special Collections Tumblr text as reposted. 99% Invisible also says the pages are "individually wrapped in plastic": https://99percentinvisible.org/article/shadows-from-the-walls-of-death-studying-poisonous-pages-of-a-lethal-book/
- **Michigan History Center:** in a vault, in a protective enclosure (Michiganology).
- **Verdict:** "It could kill you if you read it" is **dramatic, not literal.** What the evidence supports:
  - It is a real book of real arsenic-laden pages.
  - The people who made it warned not to let children handle it.
  - Today it is handled with gloves, sealed sheets or protective suits.
  - Arsenic does rub off on your hands.
  - There is no evidence a modern reader would be fatally poisoned by leafing through it, and NLM measured no airborne arsenic.
- **Suggested narration:** "a book so poisonous that its makers warned librarians to keep it away from children, and that today's librarians handle only in gloves, sealed sheets, or hazmat suits."
- **Watch out:** 99% Invisible calls him "Robert M. Kedzie." That is wrong; his name is Robert **Clark** Kedzie.

## 2. Massachusetts: Frank W. Draper's 1872 report, the Boston cases, and the first state laws

### The report
- **Title as printed:** "On the Evil Effects of the Use of Arsenic in Certain Green Colors," by Frank W. Draper, M.D.
  - It appears in the **Third Annual Report of the State Board of Health of Massachusetts**, transmitted January 22, 1872, by Secretary George Derby. Printed pages are 18 to about 57.
  - The Board's summary lists it as "The Evil Effects of Arsenic in Certain Green Colors."
  - [PRIMARY] Full text: https://archive.org/details/annualreportofst7071mass. Archive.org mislabels the volume "1870-71," but it contains the Third Report of January 1872. Text file: https://archive.org/download/annualreportofst7071mass/annualreportofst7071mass_djvu.txt
- **Key find: the 1872 Massachusetts report also had real arsenical samples bound into it, two years before Kedzie.**
  - Draper writes: "The sample herewith presented contains … 8.67 grains of arsenic in each square foot" (glossy green box paper) and "Two samples of the [wall] paper are presented herewith."
  - In the scanned copy, which came from the Boston Library Consortium, **bright green sample leaves are visible**: PDF pages about 46 and 53–56 of https://archive.org/download/annualreportofst7071mass/annualreportofst7071mass.pdf. I rendered them and checked. [PRIMARY]
  - The Board's budget for the year even has a line item: "Chemical examination of arsenic wall-papers."
  - **Script angle:** Kedzie didn't invent the poison-sample idea; Massachusetts did it first. Kedzie scaled it up to 86 samples and a whole bound book.
- **Who Draper was:** Frank Winthrop Draper (1843–1909), a Boston physician who taught legal medicine at Harvard Medical School and wrote *A Text-book of Legal Medicine* (1905). [SECONDARY: https://www.amacad.org/person/frank-winthrop-draper]
  - A blog says he helped with the Borden autopsies in 1893 [WEAK].
  - "Boston medical examiner" is **not verified**.

### What the Board's own summary warned [PRIMARY]
> "It should be read by ladies who choose green as a color for the walls of their drawing-rooms, or for their dresses, or who wish to wear bright green flower leaves as ornaments. Guardians of children should beware of green-painted toys. The green-paper covers of Boston confectionery are sometimes deadly. Green lamp-shades are to be suspected."

### American (Boston) cases and data in Draper [PRIMARY]
- **Dress fabric:**
  - MIT's Prof. William Ripley Nichols analyzed **green tarlatan bought in a Boston store** and found 8.21 grains of white arsenic per square foot. "A dress of ordinary dimensions would thus hold feebly in its texture between three and four ounces of pure arsenic."
  - **Boston, 1869:** four dressmakers making "party-dresses" of arsenical green tarlatan all broke out with swollen hands and faces, pimply eruptions, a running nose and headache. The symptoms stopped when the exposure stopped.
- **Children's paint boxes:** MIT's A. H. Pearson found 8.89 grains of arsenic in one light-green tablet weighing 38.26 grains. Draper calls this "hardly less than criminal" of the manufacturers.
- **Candy wrappers:** "One of the most reputable of the confectioners of Boston displays in his windows … rolls of lozenges enveloped in the bright green paper."
- **Boston, 1861:** a young child put a green concert ticket in his mouth and had violent symptoms. Dr. C. T. Jackson analyzed a duplicate ticket and found "nearly two grains of pure arsenic." (Draper cites the *Boston Med. & Surg. Jour.* vol. 64.)
- **Wallpaper cases in Boston:**
  - 1863: a 75-year-old woman, reported by Dr. W. B. Rice.
  - 1859 onward: "the wife of an eminent citizen of Boston," in a room with gilt-and-green flock stripes. She was "almost moribund" and figured out the cause herself. Dr. A. A. Hayes found the paper "loaded with arsenic and extremely unsafe." The workman who took it down was also poisoned.
  - A case from Dr. John Jeffries. The family even pulled out the lead pipes before they suspected the wallpaper.
  - A young lady whose case Dr. John Ware diagnosed.
  - A Boston father and his 5-year-old child.
  - All of these are anonymized. **No famous names.**
- **A visit to a New England wallpaper factory, August 1871:**
  - "In making the green pigment, arsenic was used by the barrel."
  - A 23-year veteran worker described salivation "as bad as from mercury."
  - Of nine paper-hangers Draper interviewed, seven had been poisoned.
- **The APUSH-ready conclusion, laissez-faire stated outright:**
  > "Such repressive legislation would be difficult in a society and under a government organized like our own. The rights of individuals and of industrial pursuits are deemed too sacred to allow of excessive restriction, and a prohibitory law to affect the manufacture or use of arsenical pigments would be of questionable force."
  - Draper is contrasting the U.S. with Germany, France and Sweden, which had already "stringently regulated the traffic."
  - His remedy is the consumer: "The demand ceasing, the supply will cease…"

### "Robert M. Hodges" and "Thomas Orton"
- **Robert M. Hodges: no evidence found.**
  - Draper does not cite him. Nothing turned up linking a "Robert M. Hodges" to arsenic.
  - The name looks like a mix-up. One possibility is the real Boston surgeon *Richard M. Hodges*, but I found no arsenic link for him either. Another is "Robert M. Kedzie," the wrong form 99% Invisible uses.
  - **Don't use it.**
- **"Mr. Orton" is real, but English, not American.** Draper cites him as the attending surgeon in the 1862 London-suburbs case.
  - In that case a child died, "the last of four children who had died within a period of two months," from wallpaper with 3 grains of arsenic per square foot.
  - Dr. Letheby did the chemical analysis.
  - This is the well-known Limehouse (Turner children) case. The first name "Thomas" is not verified from Draper. **Leave him out of the U.S. section.**

### Later Massachusetts work [PRIMARY: USDA Bureau of Chemistry Bulletin 86, *Arsenic in Papers and Fabrics*, J. K. Haywood, 1904, https://archive.org/details/arsenicinpapersf86hayw]
- In 1891 the Legislature authorized the State Board of Health to investigate arsenic in papers and fabrics (W. B. Hills's report).
- **Massachusetts Infant Asylum, 1890 (Dr. J. J. Putnam's case):**
  - The babies were pale, with sores on their fingers.
  - "two children died from inflammation and ulceration of the air passages."
  - The cause turned out to be arsenic-laden **blue** dresses issued to the nurses.
  - When the dresses were washed and reissued, the symptoms came back.
  - This is a strong case, but the color is not green.

### U.S. state laws
- **Massachusetts, Acts of 1900, Chapter 325** ("An act relative to the manufacture and sale of textile fabrics and papers containing arsenic"):
  - Approved **May 18, 1900**; in effect **January 1, 1901**.
  - It banned making or selling "any woven fabric or paper containing arsenic in any form."
  - Exceptions: dress goods with no more than **1/100 grain**, and other materials with no more than **1/10 grain**, of arsenic per square yard.
  - Fine: $50–$200.
  - Full text is in Bulletin 86, pp. 53–54. [PRIMARY]
  - Bulletin 86 (1904): "The United States has no Federal law regulating the amount of arsenic in wall papers, fabrics, etc. One State, Massachusetts, has passed the following law." It also credits the law with cutting high-arsenic papers sharply; most of the remaining offenders were imported, mainly from England.
- **New Hampshire, Laws of 1901, ch. 27:**
  - It uses the same thresholds (1/100 and 1/10 grain per square yard).
  - **It is still on the books today as RSA 339:45**: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XXXI/339/339-45.htm [PRIMARY: current statute and its source note]
  - Bulletin 86 missed it, or counted it differently; it claims Massachusetts was the "only State."
  - **Fun closer:** a Gilded Age arsenic-wallpaper law is still in force in New Hampshire.
- **Michigan:** sources say Kedzie "fought to make such pigments illegal." I found **no Michigan statute** in this research. **Don't claim Kedzie's book led to a ban.** One MSU/Tumblr text implies it did, and that is unsupported.
- **Federal:** there was no federal arsenic-in-wallpaper law (Bulletin 86, 1904). Cooper Hewitt likewise says that "by 1884 the United States had not yet enacted such legislation." https://www.cooperhewitt.org/2015/02/01/toxic-beauty/

## 3. Paris Green on the farm: America's first big insecticide

- **The pest:**
  - The Colorado potato beetle began attacking cultivated potatoes about 1859, "about 100 miles west of Omaha, Nebraska."
  - It spread east and reached "the Atlantic Coast by 1874."
  - [SECONDARY: UF/IFAS EDIS IN303, https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/IN303]
- **The fix:**
  - Farmers in Illinois and Indiana found Paris green worked against the beetle around 1867–68. Wikipedia cites Sorensen, *Brethren of the Net*: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_green
  - A Wisconsin farmer's letter to the *Galena Gazette* (May 28, 1869) recommended Paris green mixed with flour, and the *American Entomologist* repeated it that July. Source: EBSCO Research Starters, https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/american-farmers-increase-insecticide-use
  - USDA ARS training slides call it "Widely considered the first insecticide" and note "1870s- wide use against Colorado Potato Beetle." https://ars.usda.gov/ARSUSERFILES/47494/MODULE1.PDF [SECONDARY]
  - **Script wording:** "widely considered America's first mass-market chemical insecticide." The "first" claim is common but hedged in the sources.
- **What it looked like in the newspapers** [PRIMARY: Chronicling America]:
  - Kenosha (Wis.) *Telegraph*, June 23, 1870: "Procure a common tin pepper-duster, fill it with Paris green, and dust it sparingly on the center of each hill… about five pounds of Paris green to the acre." https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85033123/1870-06-23/ed-1/?sp=7
  - *West-Jersey Pioneer* (Bridgeton, N.J.), July 21, 1881, ad: "STRICTLY PURE Paris Green KILLS Potato Bugs," sold at a drug and stationery store. https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83032103/1881-07-21/ed-1/?sp=3
  - Mineral Point (Wis.) *Tribune*, Apr. 22, 1875. A skeptic claims the potato-bug losses of the past decade "exceed the whole cost of the late war." This is period hyperbole: quote it as the paper's claim, not as fact. https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn86086770/1875-04-22/ed-1/?sp=6
- **Twist: the same Kedzie defended Paris green on crops.**
  - The *Vermont Phoenix* (Brattleboro), June 8, 1877, quotes Kedzie's report in the Michigan State Board of Health report for 1875 (as cited by the paper).
  - He tested potatoes whose vines were heavily dusted, "but in no instance could I find a trace of arsenic in the tubers."
  - He also wrote that the small amounts used do not injure "human food."
  - The occasion was a dispute: Massachusetts State Assayer Hayes had warned against Paris green on potatoes.
  - https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn98060050/1877-06-08/ed-1/?sp=2 [PRIMARY, but secondhand quotation of Kedzie; the 1875 Michigan report is the original. OCR is rough.]
  - **The idea:** the same chemist, the same element. On the bedroom wall it was a menace; on the potato field it was progress.
- **Accidental poisonings:**
  - **Easton, Pa., June 21, 1885** (*New-York Tribune*, June 22, 1885, p. 1): "STRAWBERRIES POISONED WITH PARIS GREEN."
    - Fifteen people at a party in Freemansburg were poisoned, and so was the Jesse Abel family in Easton.
    - They had eaten strawberries from patches next to potato fields, and "the wind had carried the fine particles of Paris green upon the berries."
    - "Prompt medical aid prevented fatalities."
    - https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030214/1885-06-22/ed-1/?sp=1 [PRIMARY]
  - Paris green was also a common means of suicide in 1870s papers. Example: *New-York Tribune*, June 28, 1875, Brooklyn. We probably won't use this; it isn't suitable for the audience.
- **The mosquito war:**
  - M. A. Barber and T. B. Hayne, "Arsenic as a larvicide for anopheline larvae," *Public Health Reports*, Dec. 9, 1921. Later PHS literature says Paris green dust "has been used since 1921 as a larvicide against anophelines" after Barber's pioneering work. [SECONDARY: CDC Stacks search results]
  - **Malaria Control in War Areas (MCWA)**, created in 1942 around Southern military bases, was the ancestor of the CDC. Its July 1943 monthly report says: "30,399 pounds of paris green was applied by airplane to 24,921 acres." https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/48163 [PRIMARY]
  - DDT took over from 1943–44. "By 1951, malaria was considered eliminated from the U.S." https://www.cdc.gov/malaria/history/index.html [SECONDARY]
- **The line to regulation:**
  - **The Insecticide Act of 1910** was signed by Taft on **April 26, 1910** and took effect Jan. 1, 1911. Statute: https://www.govinfo.gov/link/statute/36/331
    - Its long title targets "adulterated or misbranded Paris greens, lead arsenates, and other insecticides."
    - It defined Paris green chemically: Paris green was adulterated if it had less than 50% arsenious oxide, or water-soluble arsenic above 3.5%.
  - **The twist:** the law protected *farmers' wallets* (weak, fake product), **not human health.**
    - farmdoc daily (Univ. of Illinois, July 2026) says the uniformity goal was "tied only to pesticide efficacy," not "safety for humans or other animals."
    - FIFRA in 1947 was the first time pesticide law took up danger to human health.
    - https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/07/pesticide-problems-part-i-legislative-history.html [SECONDARY]
  - Arsenic residue tolerances on food came in the 1920s–30s (EBSCO).

## 4. Period U.S. newspaper stories (Chronicling America via loc.gov) [PRIMARY]

1. **"Poisoned by Wall Paper"** (from the *Worcester (Mass.) Spy*), reprinted in the *Canton (Ill.) Register*, **Nov. 4, 1886**, p. 8.
   - Text: "One of our best-known Boston families has been severely afflicted during the past week by the death of its youngest child, undoubtedly from arsenic inhaled from wall paper at their summer residence at Swampscott."
   - A Harvard Medical School chemist "found more or less of the poison in nearly every paper in the house." The child was moved to another town "but did not re[cover]"; the OCR breaks off there.
   - https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85033474/1886-11-04/ed-1/?sp=8
   - Also printed in the *Irish Standard* (Minneapolis), Dec. 4, 1886: https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn90059959/1886-12-04/ed-1/?sp=7
2. **"Poison in the Arts"** (from the *St. Albans Messenger*), *Bellows Falls (Vt.) Times*, **Mar. 21, 1873**, p. 2.
   - A father tests his family's wallpaper after hearing that "a young lady who wore a green tarlatan dress to a party was carried home in a fainting fit."
   - He ends: "So we commence with the baby, clothe him in gay and poisonous apparel, feed him with poisoned candy…"
   - https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84022549/1873-03-21/ed-1/?sp=2
3. **"Strawberries Poisoned with Paris Green,"** *New-York Tribune*, **June 22, 1885**, p. 1. See section 3.
4. **"Poisoned by Wall Paper. Three Women Found to Have Met Death in Same Manner,"** *Daily Kennebec Journal* (Augusta, Me.), **Apr. 9, 1907**, p. 2.
   - A Chicago Tribune wire from Evansville, Ind.: Mrs. Zachariah Watson died after cleaning parlor wallpaper "impregnated with virulent poisons," and "Watson's first two wives died in the same way."

## Best 3 U.S. beats for a sub-10-minute video

1. **COLD OPEN: "The book that came with a warning label" (Michigan, 1874).**
   - In 1874, Michigan's Board of Health sent 100 libraries a book made of real poisoned wallpaper, with a note: "The poisonous nature of the paper of which this book is made will suggest to you the propriety of not allowing it to be handled by children."
   - The first sample page came from the house where a ten-year-old girl died.
   - Most copies were destroyed. The few left, about four or five, are sealed in plastic or vaults. When the NLM scanned its copy in 2018, the operator wore a Tyvek suit.
   - Images: NLM, public domain.
2. **APUSH beat: "A government organized like our own" (Massachusetts, 1872 to 1900).**
   - Boston's Dr. Frank Draper documented arsenic in Boston candy wrappers, kids' paint boxes, party dresses and bedroom walls, and bound real green samples into a state report.
   - He then argued that America *couldn't* ban it the way Germany and France had, because "the rights of individuals and of industrial pursuits are deemed too sacred."
   - It took 28 years: Massachusetts Acts 1900, ch. 325, effective 1901, America's first state arsenic-in-fabric-and-paper law. New Hampshire followed in 1901, and that law is **still on the books**.
   - This is Gilded Age laissez-faire versus Progressive Era regulation in one story.
3. **Pivot: "From the parlor to the potato field" (1860s to 1940s).**
   - As the Colorado potato beetle swept east to the Atlantic by 1874, the same arsenic green became farmers' first mass insecticide, dusted from pepper shakers.
   - The same Kedzie who warned about walls said potatoes were safe.
   - Paris green drifted onto strawberries (Easton, Pa., 1885). Airplanes dropped it on Southern swamps to fight malaria (1943, MCWA, the forerunner of the CDC).
   - The 1910 Insecticide Act regulated it, but to protect farmers from *weak* poison, not people from *strong* poison.

**Optional fourth beat (about 20 seconds):** the 1886 "Poisoned by Wall Paper" newspaper story of a Boston family's child dying at a Swampscott summer house. It shows the fear reached the nation's elite.

## UNVERIFIED, conflicting, or do-not-use
- **Exact number of surviving Kedzie copies:** 4 (NLM 2018), 5 (Michiganology; Wikipedia 2026, with different holders). Wikipedia's claim that NLM has two copies is not confirmed. MSU's "only complete copy" claim is self-reported and dated.
- **Physical size "22 x 30 inches"** (Michiganology). It conflicts with NLM's 42 cm.
- **The exact date of Kedzie's preface** (OCR "Sept. 2fi, 1874") and of Baker's letter (Nov. 1874). Check against the images.
- **Kedzie's capture at Shiloh:** secondary sources only; the MSU page I reached doesn't mention it.
- **"Dr. I. H. B." = Ira H. Bartholomew:** a strong match on initials, town, office and profession, but no source names him. "Hon. L. D. W. of Manchester" is not identified; possibly Lucius D. Watkins, but his service as state senator is not confirmed.
- **"Robert M. Hodges":** no evidence he existed in this context. Don't use.
- **"Thomas Orton":** "Mr. Orton" is the English surgeon in the 1862 London case. He is not American, and his first name is not verified here.
- **That Kedzie's book "led to a ban" or "quickly ended arsenical wallpaper":** stated by MSU Tumblr text and a fan site; no Michigan statute found. Bulletin 86 (1904) says only Massachusetts had a law.

- **Draper as Boston "medical examiner"** and his role in the Borden autopsies: not verified or weakly sourced.
- **The 1907 Indiana "three wives" wallpaper deaths:** a single sensational wire story; arsenic not named.
- **The 1879 Newark, Vt. "Paris green brook" deaths:** almost certainly diphtheria. Use only as a panic or misinformation example.
- **The exact first year Paris green was used on beetles** (1867 vs. 1868) and "first insecticide" wording: use "late 1860s" and "widely considered."
- **Tonnage of Paris green used on U.S. potatoes in the 1870s:** the 1877 OCR reads "from 300 to [illegible] tons" a year. Not usable.
