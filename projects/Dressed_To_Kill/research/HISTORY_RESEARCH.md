# Arsenic Green: History Research Dossier

Project: 15 Minute History, "Arsenic Green" (the 19th-century craze for Scheele's Green and Emerald/Paris Green)
Compiled: 14 Oct 2026
Scope: verified facts, corrections to the outline, quotes, myths, and hook material. Each claim has a URL. Confidence: **High** = primary source read directly or several strong sources agree. **Medium** = one reputable secondary source, or a primary source seen only second-hand. **Low / UNVERIFIED** = repeated online but not traced to a source.

## 2. The science: how people were actually poisoned

**Proven routes (period evidence):**
1. **Dust and flakes.** The pigment was fixed only loosely with size (glue) or starch.
   - Kedzie (1874) explains it this way: "the feeble adhesive power of the size… the currents of air… sweeping, dusting… all combine to dislodge the pigments… and to scatter them in the form of a fine dust in the room."
   - Dr Halley tested the air of his own library and got "distinct crystals of arsenic… from the dust" (Draper 1872).
   - Torn green tarlatan dress fabric released "a plentiful cloud of light green arsenical dust" (Draper 1872).
2. **Mouth contact (children).** The Turner children were found tearing off and licking the green wallpaper (Hawksley). An infant in Peckham died after "sucking a piece of arsenical wall-paper" (Carr 1880). Children also licked green sweet wrappers, toys "painted with Schweinfurt green loosely applied… mixed with water only," and "sweetie watches" backed with Scheele's-green paper (Draper 1872; Carr 1880).
3. **Skin contact and breathing at work.** Artificial-flower makers dusted the powder onto leaves. Dr Guy's Privy Council survey of a works employing about 100 young women found skin disease that "hardly any of them escaped." Of 25 he examined, "nearly all showed signs, often highly developed, of chronic arsenical poisoning" (quoted in Carr 1879).
4. **Eating.** Arsenic green was used directly in sweets and cake ornaments. Hassall found it in 9 of about 100 sweet samples. Draper reports 18 people seriously ill and 1 child dead in Edinburgh in 1857 after eating a green pastry ornament.

**The "toxic gas" story: what is actually true**
- **1839, Gmelin:** reports a "mouse-like" smell in damp green rooms and blames a volatile arsenic compound. A Prussian chemist, Basedow, blamed "arseniuretted hydrogen," which is **arsine** (Draper 1872, p.48–49). Sources: PMC120786; Draper.
- **1891–92, Gosio:** moulds on arsenic-pigmented material gave off a garlic-smelling gas that killed lab rats and mice quickly. He thought the gas contained arsine; his chemists suggested diethylarsine. He was looking because people were reportedly still being poisoned **after** arsenical paper had been papered over, which seemed to rule out dust. Sources: PMC120786; Res Medica (Haslam 2013) https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/view/182
- **1933, Challenger:** "Gosio gas" is **trimethylarsine** (Challenger, Higginbottom & Ellis 1933). *Wikipedia's "1921" and some sites' "1925" are wrong.* Source: https://doi.org/10.1039/jr9330000095
- **Modern toxicity data:**
  - Yamauchi et al. (1990): trimethylarsine "proved to be an arsenic compound of low toxicity," with an oral LD50 of 7,870 mg/kg in mice. "TM-As is far less toxic than arsine." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2318361/
  - W. R. Cullen & R. Bentley, "The toxicity of trimethylarsine: an urban myth," *J. Environ. Monit.* 7 (2005): 11–15. https://doi.org/10.1039/b413752n
  - Caveats: the main data are oral doses in animals, and inhalation data are thin. Methylarsines were genotoxic in cell tests (Andrewes et al. 2003). Gosio's lab deaths may have involved other volatile arsenic species.
  - Meharg measured the air in a Scottish cottage with about 20 layers of wallpaper, including arsenic-green layers, and found no arsenic gas. https://spectroscopyworld.com/system/files/pdf/X-ray_16_5.pdf
- **Arsine (AsH₃)** really is very toxic. But no one has shown that wallpaper produced it.

**On-air verdict (plain English):**
> "In damp rooms, mould growing on the wallpaper could turn the arsenic into a garlic-scented gas, and Victorian and later scientists blamed it for deaths. But that gas wasn't the deadly arsine of the history books. It's trimethylarsine, and modern tests suggest it's surprisingly weak. The real danger was simpler: arsenic dust shed into the air, onto fingers, and into the mouths of children."

Do **not** say "arsine gas." It is safe to say: "a garlicky arsenic vapour that scientists argued about for a century."

## 3. Vivid cases

### 3.1 Matilda Scheurer, artificial-flower maker (d. Nov 1861) — High (core) / Medium (details)
- **Age, job and death:** 19 years old. She had worked 18 months making artificial flowers with emerald green and "died in London with all the characteristic symptoms of chronic poisoning by arsenic… arsenic in the internal organs" (Draper 1872, citing *Medical Times & Gazette*, 30 Nov 1861).
- **Inquest (Northampton Mercury, 30 Nov 1861):**
  - Employer: M. Bergerond of Judd Street, Brunswick Square, who employed 98 girls. The girls wore muslin over their mouths instead of masks.
  - She vomited matter "of a very greenish colour."
  - Surgeon James Thomas Paul found arsenite of copper in her lungs and her liver "highly impregnated."
  - **A sister had died in similar circumstances.**
  - Verdict: she "died accidentally" from disease caused by "arsenite of copper that was used in her employment." A juror said the substance was banned in France.
  - Source: transcription at https://www.jack-the-ripper-tour.com/generalnews/poisoned-by-flowers/ (a secondary website, so Medium).
- **"Green whites of the eyes," green fingernails, "everything she looked at was green":** reported by Alison Matthews David (*Fashion Victims*, 2015) and repeated widely. We have **not** seen the primary wording. **Medium.** Good for narration if attributed: "according to accounts…"
- **Privy Council report:** the 1862 report describes one flower-maker's death in detail. Its line "The tortures which that poor girl must have endured…" (quoted in Carr 1879) is **probably** about Scheurer, but the report does not name her. Medium.

### 3.2 The Turner children, Limehouse (Feb–Apr 1862) — High (core) / Medium (inquest wording)
- **The family:** Richard Turner was a bricklayer. Over about six weeks the family lost three children to a "mysterious illness." The first was diagnosed with diphtheria. Thomas Orton was called on Thursday 3 April 1862 to the youngest, **Ann Amelia, aged 3** (Hawksley, *Telegraph* 2016 excerpt): https://www.lucindahawksley.com/could-this-wallpaper-kill-you-victorian-britains-lethal-obsession-with-the-perfect-shade-of-green/
- **Draper (1872, citing *Med. Times & Gazette*, 3 May 1862):**
  - "the last of four children who had died within a period of two months."
  - Throat symptoms.
  - The paper was loosely coloured and damp, and "the stain came off on the hand like paint."
  - "Three grains of arsenic were found in a square foot of the paper."
  - Orton and Letheby both attributed the deaths to arsenic.
- **Henry Letheby** (1816–1876): lecturer in chemistry at the London Hospital and Medical Officer of Health for the City of London. He did the chemical examination. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Letheby
- **Twist:** the inquest jury reportedly **did not** accept arsenic and returned a natural-causes verdict (Hawksley pp.166–67, via Victorian Web). The claim that the coroner publicly disagreed is **UNVERIFIED** (blog-level only).
- **Orton's follow-up** in *The Lancet* (Nov 1862) is verified by DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(02)41836-3. A quotation from it ("Numerous correspondents from all parts of England…") appears in a student essay; we have **not** checked it against the original: Medium.
- *Error to avoid:* Haslam (2013) calls 3 grains per sq ft "a lethal dose." That is a careless framing, because the paper is not eaten whole.

### 3.3 Bradford, 1858 — context only (not green pigment)
- About 20–21 dead and 200+ ill. White arsenic went into peppermint humbugs: druggist's assistant William Goddard took it from a mislabelled cask when the maker wanted "daff," a cheap filler.
- The deaths fed into the Pharmacy Act 1868. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1858_Bradford_sweets_poisoning
- **Use only to show how freely arsenic was sold.** Do not imply green dye was involved.

### 3.4 Ball gowns and wreaths
- **BMJ, 15 Feb 1862** (phrases checked against the indexed BMJ page text):
  - 20 yards of green tarlatan "would contain about 900 grains of white arsenic."
  - "A lady in full dress… carries in her skirts poison enough to slay the whole of the admirers she may meet with in half a dozen ball-rooms." **High.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2324313/
- **Hofmann for the Ladies' Sanitary Association (1862):** "a lady wears in her hair more than forty grains of white arsenic… sufficient to poison twenty persons" (*Chicago Medical Examiner*, Sept 1862). **High** (phrases checked). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9983972/
- **Draper (1872):** "Hofmann found in a single twig of twelve leaves, ten grains of pure arsenic." A Boston tarlatan sample tested at MIT held 8.21 grains of white arsenic per sq ft: "A dress of ordinary dimensions would thus hold… between three and four ounces of pure arsenic."
  - Draper himself thought that **occasional wearing** would not seriously harm the wearer. The victims were mostly **the dressmakers**: four Boston dressmakers in 1869, and dancers playing water-nymphs at a Hamburg ballet, whose costumes "nearly cost the lives" of the makers and wearers. **High** (primary; these are period estimates, not modern measurements).
- **Real surviving dress:** the 1860s green silk dress at Toronto Metropolitan University (FRC2014.07.406) tested positive for arsenic on 5 Sept 2014 and was displayed in the Bata Shoe Museum's *Fashion Victims* exhibition. https://www.torontomu.ca/content/dam/fashionresearchcollection/research-articles/Arsenic_Green_Dress.pdf

### 3.5 Punch
- **"The Arsenic Waltz. The New Dance of Death. (Dedicated to the Green Wreath and Dress-Mongers)"**, *Punch*, 8 Feb 1862. It shows a skeleton gentleman asking a skeleton lady to dance. Artist **uncertain** ("possibly John Leech" per Wellcome). The image is public domain at Wellcome. https://wellcomecollection.org/works/awbr7whm
- "The Great Social Evil" (1857) is **not** about arsenic (§0). https://victorianweb.org/periodicals/punch/49.html

### 3.6 Napoleon, St Helena (d. 5 May 1821)
- **The wallpaper:** David Jones (chemist, BBC radio, May 1980) asked listeners about the colour of Napoleon's wallpaper. Shirley Bradley of Norfolk produced a scrap from a family scrapbook. X-ray fluorescence at Glasgow (Ken Ledingham) found arsenic, about 0.12 g/m² per Jones's own later account.
  - Published as Jones & Ledingham, "Arsenic in Napoleon's wallpaper," *Nature* 299 (1982): 626–627. https://doi.org/10.1038/299626a0
  - Their conclusion: enough to make him ill, probably not to kill him.
  - 0.12 g/m² figure: Medium (Jones's recollection). https://chemistryworld.com/opinion/the-last-retort/3005618.article
- **Hair study (INFN, 2008):**
  - Hair from Napoleon's boyhood, his Elba exile, and the day of and after his death, plus his son and Josephine.
  - All samples had high arsenic, about 100× modern levels (roughly 10 ppm vs 0.1 ppm), with **no significant rise at the end of his life**.
  - Conclusion: steady environmental exposure, not poisoning. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/02/080211131357.htm
- **Cause of death:** Lugli et al. (2007) read the autopsy reports as advanced gastric cancer (at least stage IIIA). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17203089/
- **On-air verdict:** "His wallpaper really did contain arsenic. But it almost certainly didn't kill him. Stomach cancer did."

### 3.7 Other verified household cases (pick one or two)
- **Dr Halley, London:** fell ill in his library newly papered in emerald-green flock, nearly 60 grains of Scheele's green per sq ft. He suffered partial paralysis of his left side and recovered whenever he stayed away (Draper 1872). High.
- **Robert Brudenell Carter FRCS** had "reason to believe that two of his children died from an arsenical wallpaper in their nursery." https://rcseng.ac.uk/library-and-publications/library/blog/arsenic-a-domestic-poison
  - Probably the same case the 1883 BMJ report describes: "two children of an eminent consulting surgeon died from enteritis, while the nurses escaped" until the nursery papers were tested. High.
- **Hinds's Birmingham couple:** the couple fell ill **and so did their parrot**, and all recovered once the paper was removed (Res Medica; RCS). Medium-High.
- **Doctors as victims:** "one-fourth of the cases of poisoning reported to the Medical Society had occurred in the persons of the medical men themselves… or in members of their families" (BMJ 1883). High.

### 3.8 Sweets, toys and odd objects
- **Sweets:** 9 of about 100 coloured sweet samples contained arsenic green (Hassall 1855).
- **Toys:** "sweetie watches" in Glasgow with a paper back of Scheele's green; children were poisoned (Carr 1880). Toys painted with Schweinfurt green mixed with water (Draper).
- **Food:** a Vienna family poisoned by strawberries kept in a box painted green (Draper). Professor Taylor found Scheele's green on the underside of his breakfast loaf (Draper).
- **Other items:** green paper on lozenge rolls in "one of the most reputable" Boston confectioners' windows (Draper 1872).

### 3.9 Factory workers
- **Artificial-leaf works:** Dr Guy's survey (§2). Of 25 workers, 20 had fever, and dimness of sight affected two-thirds (Privy Council 1862, via Carr 1879).
- **Paper-stainers:** a printer could not work with arsenical pigments for more than two or three weeks at a stretch (Draper, as reprinted; Medium).
- **Devon Great Consols miners:** suffered arsenic skin lesions ("pock") (Wired via Backspace; Medium).

## 4. The alarm-raisers vs. the industry

**Verified alarm-raisers**
- **Leopold Gmelin**, 1839 (newspaper warning).
- **Arthur Hill Hassall**, *Lancet* commission, 1851–54 (sweets).
- **Alfred Swaine Taylor**, toxicologist (Guy's). He measured 28–70 grains of pigment per sq ft in papers (Draper). The famous line "wallpaper now furnishes arsenic to the millions" (1858) is attributed to him via Whorton: Medium. A pastry-ornament inquest (*Times*, 29 Jan 1853) quotes him on "ten fatal cases" of children eating cake ornaments in two years. Those ornaments were probably arsenical, but the colour is not stated: Medium.
- **William Hinds** (Birmingham, 1857); **Dr Halley** (London).
- **Thomas Orton** (surgeon) and **Henry Letheby** (chemist / MOH), 1862.

**The industry's position**
- In Paris, manufacturers protested that banning arsenic "would necessitate the absolute suspension of their works" (Draper 1872).
- In 1891, manufacturers argued in Massachusetts that paper-hangings "cannot be made without arsenic" (American Architect, via search). Medium.
- At Boston hearings, an analyst who pushed for the law was accused of self-interest because he earned fees from testing papers (BMSJ 1887 discussion). https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-101759884-bk
- Some manufacturers reportedly offered to eat their own paper (Haslam): Low/Medium.

**Why Britain never banned it.** Laissez-faire politics, money from arsenic mining, and genuinely mixed medical evidence. Doctors still prescribed arsenic, and arsenic-eating peasants in Styria seemed fine (Bartrip 1994, via Waterloo HR). The 1883 report admitted that "the number of cases may appear very small." It argued that this was because doctors did not recognize the cause.

## 5. William Morris

- **The letter.** Verified from the manuscript images in the William Morris Archive (Univ. of Iowa, MA 4669). https://morrisarchive.lib.uiowa.edu/items/show/3365
  - Written on Kelmscott House, Upper Mall, Hammersmith letterhead and dated **"Oct 3rd"** with no year.
  - The catalogue's "October 3, 1868" and "Southwold" are **wrong**. Morris only moved to Kelmscott House in 1878. Hawksley, Meharg (*Nature* 2003) and the Smithsonian all date it **3 October 1885**. That date fits the letterhead. We did not check Kelvin's *Collected Letters* directly.
  - Recipient: **Thomas Wardle**, Morris's dyer in Leek, Staffordshire. (Some sites say "Leeds"; that is wrong.)
  - Exact wording: "Thanks for your note about the papers. I cannot imagine it possible that the amount of lead which might be in a paper could give people lead-poisoning: still there should not be lead in them: especially, by the way in the red one; I can understand Chromate of lead being in the green ones but surely in very small quantities. **As to the arsenic scare a greater folly it is hardly possible to imagine: the doctors were bitten as people were bitten by the witch-fever.** I will see Warner next week and try to get to the bottom of the matter. My belief about it all is that doctors find their patients ailing don't know what's the matter with them and in despair put it down to the wall papers, when they probably ought to put it down to the water closet, which I believe to be the source of all illness. And by the by as Nicholson is a tea-totaller he probably imbibes more sewage than other people: though you mustn't tell him I said so." (A P.S. blames lead-tinned cooking pots.)
  - **Correction:** the version usually printed online, "the doctors were bitten by witch fever," is a misquote. One website even says the line was Wardle's reply to Morris; that is wrong too.
  - "Warner" is almost certainly **Metford Warner** of Jeffrey & Co., the firm that printed Morris's papers (Medium, inference). "Nicholson" was the customer who complained (Meharg, *Nature* 2003).
- **Money.**
  - Morris's father was a founding investor in Devon Great Consols in 1844 (272 shares). DGC became one of the world's largest arsenic producers, refining arsenic from 1867/68 and producing about 72,000 tons in all.
  - Morris was a **director in 1871–75** and a shareholder until about 1877.
  - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devon_Great_Consols ; https://www.abdn.ac.uk/news/21299/ ; Hawksley via Victorian Web.
- **Did Morris papers contain arsenic? Yes, the early ones.**
  - Meharg tested a scrap of *Trellis* (1864) from the home of Morris's foreman George Campfield: "The green branch was an arsenic-copper salt, the red rose vermilion."
  - In the Sanderson/Morris archive, "nine out the first eleven designs contained colourways that used arsenic pigments, with the use of arsenic stopping somewhere around 1872."
  - A scrap of wallpaper found under the floorboards of Morris's **childhood home** (now the William Morris Gallery) was also arsenic green.
  - The NGV (Melbourne) found **no** arsenic in its *Daisy*, only lead. The V&A found no arsenic in the later stand-book greens.
  - Sources: https://spectroscopyworld.com/system/files/pdf/X-ray_16_5.pdf ; https://ngv.vic.gov.au/explore/collection/work/60399 ; V&A blog.
- **Fairness check.**
  - Fair to say: he was an arsenic heir and mine director, his early papers were arsenical, and he dismissed the danger in 1885. Even then, it was in a **private** letter.
  - Not supported: "factory magnate resisting regulation."
    - He did not print his own wallpapers.
    - We found no evidence that he lobbied.
    - He had resigned from the mine board and, per O'Sullivan via Philip Ball, sold his shares before his socialist period.
    - His firm had already dropped arsenic colours by the time he wrote the letter.
  - Suggested framing: *"Even the most famous designer of the age, a man whose family fortune came out of an arsenic mine, called the panic 'folly.'"*

## 7. Myths and common errors to avoid

1. **"Arsine gas from damp wallpaper."** The gas is trimethylarsine, and its toxicity is now disputed or considered low (§2). Even Meharg's 2004 article repeats "highly toxic trimethylarsine… had killed many," which shows how persistent the claim is.
2. **Napoleon killed by wallpaper.** Not supported (§3.6).
3. **"The Victorians knew nothing."** False.
   - Scheele warned about the toxicity in 1777.
   - Hassall tested sweets in the 1850s.
   - *Punch* mocked arsenic dresses in 1862.
   - Draper (1872) says tarlatan was "known popularly as an 'arsenical' green."
   - Kedzie mailed actual poison samples to libraries.
   - The real problem was **disputed evidence plus no regulation** in Britain and the US.
4. **Death tolls.** There is **no reliable total.** Deaths were often misdiagnosed as diphtheria, cholera and similar illnesses, and wallpaper deaths were hard to prove.
   - Documented cases number in the low hundreds of illnesses: the Medical Society of London committee (1880) worked from "a little over one hundred" cases.
   - Headlines like "Left Thousands Dead" (e.g., Esquire Philippines) have no source.
   - On air: *"Nobody knows how many died. The cases we can document run to hundreds of illnesses and a scattering of deaths, and many more were probably never recognized."*
5. **"100 million square miles of green wallpaper in Britain (1858)"** (Haslam / Res Medica). This is impossible: Great Britain is about 89,000 square miles. It is probably a garbled "square yards." Do not use.
6. **"Britain banned arsenic wallpaper."** It never did. Sweden (1876) and Germany (1882/1887) did. Massachusetts set limits in 1900.
7. **Bradford 1858 as an "arsenic green" case.** It wasn't. It was white arsenic in humbugs.
8. **"The Great Social Evil" as an arsenic cartoon.** It is about prostitution.
9. **Morris quote wording, date and recipient.** Use §5. Not "Leeds," not 1868, not Wardle's reply.
10. **Turner case "proved" by the inquest.** The jury rejected arsenic. The doctors blamed it.
11. **Buckingham Palace, 1879:** Queen Victoria supposedly had green paper stripped after a visiting dignitary fell ill. **UNVERIFIED.** It is often credited to Carr's 1879 pamphlet, but we searched Carr 1879/1880/1883 and found **no** mention of the palace or the Queen. Do not use.
12. **"Scheele's green was invented in 1778."** Discovered in 1775, published in 1778. Either date can be defended if labelled.
