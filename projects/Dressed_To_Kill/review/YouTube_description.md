The most fashionable color of the 1860s was made with arsenic, and people knew it. Punch drew a skeleton asking a lady in a green ball gown to dance. In 1874, Michigan mailed 100 libraries a book made of real poison wallpaper, with a note asking librarians not to let children handle it. So how did a poison end up on the walls, the dresses and the candy, and why did it take so long to get it off?

This is the history of arsenic green: Carl Wilhelm Scheele's copper-arsenic Scheele's Green (1775) and the brighter Paris green (1814), the Victorian craze for green wallpaper, ball gowns, hair wreaths, candy wrappers and toys, the BMJ's warning about ball gowns, Punch's "Arsenic Waltz" (1862), the artificial-flower maker Matilda Scheurer (1861), the Turner children of Limehouse (1862) and the "natural death" verdict, the dust vs. gas debate (and Napoleon's wallpaper), William Morris, Devon Great Consols and his 1885 "witch fever" letter, Frank Draper's 1872 Massachusetts report and laissez-faire, Robert Kedzie's "Shadows from the Walls of Death" (1874), "arsenic-free" wallpaper, Sweden's and Germany's bans, Britain's lack of one, the 1900 Massachusetts law, and Paris green on America's potato fields.
