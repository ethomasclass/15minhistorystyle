Meanwhile, in America, the same green was on the same walls.

Massachusetts, 1872. A Boston doctor named Frank Draper writes a report for the state's {board of health}, a government office that tracks disease. His first line: "Everybody knows that arsenic is a deadly poison."

Then he shows where it's hiding. Bedroom walls. Children's paint boxes. Candy wrappers in the window of one of Boston's best shops. He even bound real samples of the poisoned paper into his report.

So, ban it? Some European governments were already restricting these colors. Draper didn't think it would work here. That would be difficult, he wrote, "under a government organized like our own. The rights of individuals and of industrial pursuits are deemed too sacred."

Translation: he didn't think Americans would let the government tell a business what it couldn't sell. That's {laissez-faire}, French for "leave it alone." The government stays out, and buyers look out for themselves. Stop buying it, Draper said, and they'll stop making it.

Two years later, in Michigan, a chemistry professor named Robert Kedzie decided people needed to see it for themselves.

He collected cases. A nine-year-old girl named Emma got sick in her green bedroom, and got better when she was moved out.

And in a little town called Spring Port, the girl from the first page. Her family's walls were green in nearly every room.

So Kedzie went shopping. He gathered wallpaper from ordinary stores and tested every sheet himself. All 86 had arsenic. Then he bound them into a book, and mailed it to the libraries. This book.

His headline was a question: "Shall we poison our homes?"
