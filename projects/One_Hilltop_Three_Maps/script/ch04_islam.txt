In the early 600s CE, in the trading city of Mecca on the Arabian Peninsula, a merchant named Muhammad said he began receiving revelations from God. Those revelations became the Quran. Muslims see Muhammad as the final prophet in a line that includes Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. And quick note: "Allah" is simply the Arabic word for God.

Islam is a universalizing religion with about 2 billion followers. It's the second largest religion in the world, and one of the fastest growing.

And it spread fast. Within about 100 years of Muhammad's death, Islam stretched from Spain to Central Asia. Part of that was hierarchical diffusion, as new Muslim rulers and empires took over territory.

But a huge part of the story isn't about armies. It's about trade. Muslim merchants crossed the Sahara with camel caravans and sailed the Indian Ocean on the monsoon winds. In port after port, local people adopted their faith. That's contagious diffusion.

One famous example: Mansa Musa, ruler of the West African empire of Mali, and possibly the richest person who ever lived. In thirteen twenty-four, he made the pilgrimage to Mecca with a caravan so loaded with gold that he crashed its value in Cairo for years. Trade brought Islam to Mali, its rulers embraced it, and cities like Timbuktu became centers of Islamic learning.

Islam has two main branches. Most Muslims, around 85 to 90 percent, are Sunni. About 10 to 15 percent are Shia, concentrated in countries like Iran and Iraq.

Muslim life is built around the Five Pillars: declaring faith in one God, praying five times a day, giving to charity, fasting during Ramadan, and making a pilgrimage to Mecca at least once, if you're able.

The five daily prayers happen at set times from dawn to night, always facing Mecca. In much of the world, you'll hear the call to prayer echo from mosques. And yes, there are apps that point toward Mecca from wherever you are.

During the month of Ramadan, many Muslims don't eat or drink anything, not even water, from sunrise to sunset. Picture a full school day, or football practice, without a single sip. At sunset, families and friends break the fast together with a meal called iftar, often starting with dates and water. When Ramadan ends, there's a huge celebration, Eid al-Fitr, with new clothes, gifts, and a lot of food. Many Muslims also eat only halal food, which means no pork and no alcohol.

Now, back to our puzzle. Which country has the most Muslims in the world? Not Saudi Arabia. Not Egypt. It's Indonesia, in Southeast Asia, with roughly 240 million Muslims. And Islam got there mostly through traders, not conquest. In fact, only about 1 in 5 Muslims lives in the Middle East and North Africa.

And every year, nearly 2 million of those Muslims, from Indonesia to Nigeria to Michigan, travel to Mecca for the hajj. Many men wear the same two pieces of plain white cloth, so you can't tell a billionaire from a farmer. Everyone stands equal.
