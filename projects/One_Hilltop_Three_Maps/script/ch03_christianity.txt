About 2,000 years ago, in that same small region, a Jewish teacher named Jesus of Nazareth began preaching. His followers came to believe he was the Son of God, and that he rose from the dead after being crucified. Their holy book became the Bible, combining the Hebrew scriptures with the New Testament.

From the start, Christianity was a universalizing religion: its message was meant for everyone. Today it's the largest religion in the world, with about 2.4 billion followers. And its spread uses every type of diffusion in the book.

First, person to person. Early missionaries like Paul traveled the Roman Empire's roads and shipping routes, starting small communities in city after city. That's contagious diffusion.

Then, from the top. In 313, the emperor Constantine legalized Christianity, and by 380 it was the official religion of the Roman Empire. When the emperor converts, the empire follows. That's hierarchical diffusion.

Then, across oceans. Starting in the 1500s, European colonizers and missionaries carried Christianity to the Americas, Africa, and Asia. That's relocation diffusion, and it's why about 8 in 10 people in the Philippines are Catholic today.

Today, Christianity has three major branches. Catholics are concentrated in Latin America, southern Europe, and the Philippines. Protestants dominate northern Europe, the United States, and much of Africa. Orthodox Christians are mostly in Eastern Europe and Russia.

And a church service can look completely different depending on where you are. In Lagos, Nigeria, a Pentecostal service might run for hours, with a full band and people dancing in the aisles. In Greece, an Orthodox service is all incense and golden painted icons. In Manila, millions fill the streets for processions during Holy Week.

In Mexico, Día de los Muertos blends Catholic holidays honoring the dead with Indigenous traditions that are far older. That kind of blending is a big deal in geography, and you'll see much more of it soon.

But here's the part most people don't know. In 1900, most of the world's Christians lived in Europe. Today, more Christians live in sub-Saharan Africa than in any other region on Earth. The typical Christian today is more likely to live in Lagos or São Paulo than in London or Paris. Christianity's center of gravity has moved south.
