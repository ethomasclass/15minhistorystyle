Judaism is the oldest of the three, going back about 4,000 years in the land along the eastern Mediterranean that includes modern Israel. It was one of the first religions to worship a single God. Its central text is the Torah, the first five books of the Hebrew Bible.

Judaism is an ethnic religion. Being Jewish is tied to a people and a shared history, not just a set of beliefs, and Judaism doesn't seek converts. It's also small: about 15 million people, most of them living in Israel or the United States.

But look at the map. Why are there Jewish communities on nearly every continent?

Because, again and again, Jews were forced to leave. The Babylonians conquered Jerusalem and exiled its people. The Romans destroyed the Temple in 70 CE. In fourteen ninety-two, Spain expelled its entire Jewish population. In the late 1800s and early 1900s, more than two million Jews fled poverty and violence in Eastern Europe for the United States. And after the Holocaust, many moved to the new state of Israel, founded in 1948.

This scattering has a name: the diaspora. It's relocation diffusion, and much of it wasn't by choice. That's why the map of Judaism looks like scattered dots instead of one solid block.

Wherever they landed, Jewish families carried their traditions with them. Every Friday at sunset, Shabbat begins and lasts until Saturday night. Many families start it with a special dinner: candles, blessings, and braided bread called challah. Some observant Jews take a full break from work, driving, and even phones for the whole day. Imagine a 25-hour, every-week break from your screen.

Many Jews also keep kosher, a set of food laws: no pork, no shellfish, and no mixing meat with dairy. So, no cheeseburgers at a strictly kosher deli.

At 13, boys celebrate a bar mitzvah, and girls celebrate a bat mitzvah, usually at 12 or 13. The teen reads from the Torah in Hebrew in front of the whole community, which is about as nerve-wracking as it sounds, and then everyone celebrates.

And here's where geography gets really interesting. Because Jewish communities landed in so many places, each one developed its own culture. Ashkenazi Jews in Central and Eastern Europe spoke Yiddish, and when they came to New York, they brought the bagel with them. Sephardic Jews from Spain developed their own language, Ladino. Mizrahi Jews built communities across the Middle East and North Africa. And in Ethiopia, the Beta Israel practiced Judaism for centuries in near isolation.

In 1991, Israel airlifted more than 14,000 Ethiopian Jews out of a country in civil war, in about 36 hours. It was called Operation Solomon. One plane carried more than 1,000 people, a world record. Same religion, completely different cultures, all shaped by where the diaspora landed.
