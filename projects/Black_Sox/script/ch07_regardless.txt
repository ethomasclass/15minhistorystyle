For almost a year, it's a rumor. Then, in September 1920, a {grand jury} in Chicago, a panel that decides if there's enough evidence for a trial, starts looking into gambling in baseball. Cicotte breaks down and confesses. So does Jackson. He says he took 5,000 dollars.

Outside the courthouse, the story goes, a little boy looks up at Jackson and begs, "Say it ain't so, Joe." It's a great line. Jackson said it never happened.

The owners are scared. A fixed World Series can't be handled quietly. So they hire a federal judge, Kenesaw Mountain Landis, as baseball's first {commissioner}, with almost total power over the game.

Summer 1921. The trial. The players take back their confessions. On August 2nd, the jury finds them all *not guilty*. Some jurors reportedly celebrate with the players afterward.

The next day, Landis bans all eight anyway. For life.

Even third baseman Buck Weaver, who took no money and played hard. He knew about the fix and didn't report it. That was enough.

This time, nobody came back. That's the wall. Gambling goes from an open secret to the one thing baseball *will not forgive*.
