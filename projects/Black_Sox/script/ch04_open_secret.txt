But a rule is only as strong as the people enforcing it. And for the next 40 years, almost nobody wanted to look.

Baseball booms. By the 1900s there are two major leagues, big new ballparks and a World Series. The betting comes right along with it. Bettors work openly in the stands. Players bet. Club officials bet. Usually on their own team to win. Not always.

Why didn't anyone stop it? Because enforcing the rule meant admitting there was a problem. A fixed game is bad for business. So is telling your customers about one. Clubs usually handled suspicious players *quietly*. A trade. A release. No headlines.

Umpires weren't off limits either. In 1882, an umpire named Dick Higham was caught writing letters to a gambler, tipping him off on which way to bet. He's still the only umpire ever banned for life.

And in October 1908, the Giants and the Cubs play one game to decide the pennant. Before it, a man offers umpire Bill Klem thousands of dollars to make the close calls go New York's way. Klem says no, and reports it.

The man turns out to be the Giants' own team doctor. He's banned from every ballpark for life. Who sent him? Nobody really asks.

Which seems like the obvious next question.
