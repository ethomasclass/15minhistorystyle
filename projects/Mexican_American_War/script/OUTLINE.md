## The hook: "Where is the spot?"

**Cold-open artifact:** Abraham Lincoln's handwritten **Spot Resolutions**, December 22, 1847.

A 38-year-old congressman from Illinois, in his first month in Washington, stands up and asks the President of the United States a
very specific question. President Polk had told Congress that Mexico "shed American blood upon the American soil." Fine, said Lincoln.
*Show me the spot.* Was it really American soil? Or was it someone else's land, where the American army had just shown up?

**Why this works:**
- **It's a mystery with an answer.** Viewers get a question they can hold for 12 minutes, and the video pays it off: the spot was in the
  land between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande, which Texas claimed and Mexico said was never Texas.
- **It's the lesson's opener in disguise.** "Where does Texas end?" (the card's two-river map) is exactly Lincoln's question. Students
  get the same thing the class starts with; general viewers get a story.
- **It's a little bit funny**, which suits the channel's voice. A first-term congressman picks a fight with a wartime president, and his
  hometown papers start calling him "Spotty Lincoln." Lincoln isn't the hero of the video, though. He's one of the critics, and the
  video gives the war's supporters their say as well.
- **It sets up the ending.** Lincoln asks where the war *started*. The ending asks where it was *going*: every new acre forced the
  question "slave or free?"

**Driving question (said out loud at the end of the cold open):**
> How did an argument over a strip of land between two rivers turn into a war that moved the map by half a million square miles,
> and why did winning it make America's biggest problem worse?

**Answer (the last chapter says it):** Texas broke from Mexico over self-rule *and* slavery. Polk wanted California and the Southwest,
tried to buy them, then sent the army into the land both countries claimed. When the shooting started, Congress voted for a war that had
already begun. Supporters saw a defense of American soil and the country's destiny; critics, Lincoln and the young Ulysses S. Grant among
them, saw a war of conquest. The treaty won the land, and the people already living there paid for it. Every new acre then raised the
question the country had been avoiding: slave or free? The Wilmot Proviso showed the answer would split the country by region, not by
party.

**Possible titles** (short, per your packaging rule):
1. **The Exact Spot** · *Texas, the Mexican-American War, and the Question It Reopened* (recommended)
2. **American Blood, American Soil?** · *How the U.S. Went to War with Mexico*
3. **Mexico Will Poison Us** · *The War That Won the West and Reopened Slavery* (from Emerson; see chapter 9)

Dates on the title card: **1821 – 1848**.

## 1 · The Exact Spot (cold open) · 0:00 · ~160 words

- Open on the document: Lincoln's Spot Resolutions, in his own hand. December 1847. The war has been going for 19 months.
- Short sentences: a freshman congressman. Eight resolutions. Basically one question asked eight different ways: *where is the spot?*
- Back up to Polk's war message, May 1846: Mexico "has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil."
- Lincoln's point: if it happened on Mexican land, the story changes.
- Cut to the **two-river map**: the Nueces and the Rio Grande, about 150 miles apart. Texas said its border was the Rio Grande. Mexico
  said Texas stopped at the Nueces, if it was Texas at all.
- Driving question.
- Hook out: "To answer Lincoln's question, we have to go back 25 years, to when Mexico was the one inviting Americans in."

*On screen:* the Spot Resolutions manuscript with "the particular spot" circled in teal; Polk's message with the key line marked; the
two-river map with the strip between them tinted coral.

## 2 · Come On In · 1:02 · ~220 words

- 1821: Mexico wins independence from Spain and has a huge, thinly settled north. Its answer is to *invite* settlers. {empresario}:
  a contractor who brings in settlers for a land grant. Stephen F. Austin's first 300 families.
- The deal: become Mexican citizens, follow Mexican law, and (officially) become Catholic. Land is cheap; cotton grows well.
- Many settlers come from the South and **bring enslaved people**. Name this plainly.
- Tejanos: the Mexican Texans already there, who mostly welcomed the newcomers at first.
- The problem grows: Americans soon outnumber Tejanos many times over.
- 1829: Mexico **abolishes slavery**. Texas talks its way into an exemption, and settlers use "contracts" to keep enslaved people
  in all but name.
- 1830: Mexico **bans new American settlement** and new enslaved people coming in. It doesn't stop either one.
- One dry aside: "So Mexico's plan to fill Texas worked. A little too well."
- Hook out: "And then a general named Santa Anna decided to take charge of everything."

*Vocab:* empresario, Tejano.

## 3 · Remember the Alamo · 2:15 · ~300 words

- Santa Anna throws out Mexico's federal constitution and **centralizes power**: decisions come from Mexico City, not the states.
  {centralism}. Several Mexican states revolt, not just Texas. That matters: it wasn't only an American story.
- 1835: fighting starts. March 2, 1836: Texas declares independence.
- **March 6, 1836: the Alamo.** Fewer than 200 defenders killed. Name **Juan Seguín**, a Tejano officer who had ridden out of the
  Alamo days earlier as a messenger and survived. He's this video's thread; he comes back in chapter 8. Tejanos fought on both sides.
- **April 21, 1836: San Jacinto.** Houston's army wins in about 18 minutes and captures Santa Anna the next day. Mexico's government
  never recognizes the treaty he signs as a prisoner. Hold that thought: Mexico still says Texas is Mexico.
- The Republic of Texas's constitution makes slavery permanent: Congress can't free enslaved people, and free Black people can't live
  there without permission.
- **The card's question, said plainly: freedom, slavery, or both?** Texans saw a fight against a dictator for the rights they'd been
  promised. Opponents, then and now, point to the constitution they wrote. The video lets the evidence speak: both were true at once.
- **Nine years** as an independent country (the card's "Watch for"). The U.S. holds off on annexing because of slavery and fear of
  war with Mexico. 1845: Congress annexes Texas. {annexation}. Mexico breaks off relations.
- Optional: {Manifest Destiny}, coined in 1845, the belief that the U.S. was meant to spread across the continent. One sentence. Not
  on the card, but most students are expected to know it. Your call.
- **Mid-video subscribe plug** at the end of this chapter (about 3:45, a third of the way in). Draft: "Quick thing before we get to the
  war. If you're enjoying this, hit subscribe. Unlike Texas, you don't have to wait nine years."

*Vocab:* centralism, Republic of Texas, annexation, (Manifest Destiny).

## 4 · Polk Acts First · 3:54 · ~300 words

- James K. Polk takes office in 1845 and wants California and the Southwest, especially the harbor at San Francisco.
- **He tries to buy them first.** Late 1845: he sends John Slidell to Mexico City with an offer of up to $25 million. Mexico refuses
  even to meet him. To Mexico, Texas is still stolen land.
- January 1846: Polk orders General Zachary Taylor's army to the Rio Grande, into the land between the rivers. Taylor sets up across
  from the Mexican town of Matamoros.
- **April 25, 1846**: Mexican cavalry attack an American patrol in the disputed land. Soldiers killed; the rest captured.
- **The key detail:** on **May 9**, Polk's cabinet agrees to ask Congress for war *anyway*, before any news of a fight. That same evening,
  Taylor's report of the attack arrives. Polk rewrites the message. It comes from his own diary.
- May 11: "American blood upon the American soil." The bill says war *already exists* "by the act of the Republic of Mexico."
- Congress votes yes by huge margins; Polk signs **May 13**. Many who doubted the war voted yes anyway: voting no could look like
  abandoning soldiers who were already fighting.
- **Card's "Watch for": Congress didn't start the war; it voted after the fighting began.** Say it in exactly those terms.
- ASK beat: "So who actually decided this country would go to war?" Leave it open to come back to in chapter 6.

*On screen:* Polk portrait (coral); a diary page with May 9 marked; the two-river map again, Taylor's arrow moving into the strip.
*Vocab:* Nueces Strip.

## 5 · The War, Fast · 5:32 · ~120 words · CUT FIRST

The card marks battles as "often taught, not essential," so this is a quick-moving map, not a battle chapter.
- Three arrows: Taylor wins in the north (Palo Alto, Buena Vista). Forces take New Mexico and California. Winfield Scott lands at
  Veracruz and marches inland to **capture Mexico City, September 1847**.
- One human beat: Chapultepec and the cadets Mexico remembers as the **Niños Héroes**.
- A list of young American officers: Grant, Lee, Jackson, Longstreet, McClellan. "Remember some of those names. They'll be fighting
  each other in about 14 years." (Sets up the ending.)

## 6 · The Spot · 6:13 · ~280 words

- Back to the cold open's document. **Lincoln's Spot Resolutions**, December 1847: was the spot inside land Mexico had always
  governed? Had the people there ever accepted Texas's or America's rule?
- Payoff: the attack happened in the strip Texas claimed but had never really governed. Lincoln's point lands.
- January 1848: the House of Representatives votes, narrowly, that the war was "unnecessarily and unconstitutionally begun by the
  President." The war is still on. A dry beat.
- Lincoln pays for it: his hometown papers call him "Spotty Lincoln." He'd already promised to serve only one term and went home.
- **Grant**, who fought in the war as a young lieutenant, later called it "one of the most unjust ever waged by a stronger against
  a weaker nation."
- Optional aside (10 s): Henry David Thoreau spends one night in jail rather than pay a tax that supports the war, and writes "Civil
  Disobedience" about it. Someone paid the tax for him the next morning.
- **Supporters' side, given fairly:** Mexico refused to negotiate, owed Americans unpaid claims, had attacked first in land Texas
  claimed, and many Americans believed in expanding west. It was a popular war in much of the country, and volunteers signed up
  faster than the army could use them.
- Close: "Historians have argued about this for over a hundred years. Some blame Mexico. Others blame Polk." (No historian names, per
  your rule, so the card's two historians become this one line.)

*On screen:* the "two views" layout, supporters left, critics right. Grant's quote card.

## 7 · The Man Who Was Fired · 7:45 · ~250 words

The story hook for the treaty chapter.
- Polk sends a diplomat, **Nicholas Trist**, with the army. Talks go badly; Polk recalls him.
- Trist decides the chance for peace is now or never, **ignores the recall**, writes Polk a letter of about 65 pages explaining why,
  and keeps negotiating. Dry aside: "Which is a very long way to say 'no.'"
- **February 2, 1848: the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo**, signed by a man who technically no longer works for the government. Polk is
  furious, but it's what he wanted, and he sends it to the Senate.
- Terms, with a map that fills in: Rio Grande is the border. The **Mexican Cession**: about 530,000 square miles (today's California,
  Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming). The U.S. pays **$15 million**.
- Promises to people living there: **Article VIII** protects their property; they can become U.S. citizens.
- But the **Senate cut Article X**, which protected Mexican land grants. Hold that thought.
- Optional one line: the Gadsden Purchase, 1853, the last strip of land in the southwest. (Card: not essential.)

*On screen:* the cession map tinted coral, square-mile counter. The treaty's signature page.
*Vocab:* Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, Mexican Cession.

## 8 · Who Paid · 9:08 · ~220 words · HEAVY (no jokes, slower voice, quiet mode)

ASK beat from the card: *Who lost when the land changed hands?*
- **The cost in lives.** About 1,700 Americans killed in battle. More than 11,500 died of disease. Most soldiers who died never saw
  a battle. Mexico's losses were larger and were never fully counted (hedged).
- **About 80,000 Mexicans** in the ceded land become foreigners overnight without moving. On paper, their property was protected.
- **The 1851 Land Act** (California) makes landowners prove their titles in court. Cases take years; lawyers' fees and squatters take
  much of the land anyway.
- Native nations living in the Cession had no part in the treaty at all. (Not on the card. This follows your "say who was left out"
  rule; one sentence.)
- Enslaved people in Texas, from chapter 3: annexation and the war locked their status in. They had no say.
- **Callback: Juan Seguín.** The Tejano who fought for Texas at the Alamo and became mayor of San Antonio is driven out by newer Anglo
  settlers in the 1840s. In his memoir, he called himself "a foreigner in my native land." Let that line sit.

*On screen:* quiet mode, all teal. The numbers as a simple chart; a land-case document; Seguín's portrait with his quote.
*Vocab:* Californios (optional).

## 9 · Poison · 10:29 · ~300 words

- Go back to **August 1846**, three months into the war. A Pennsylvania congressman, **David Wilmot**, attaches one sentence to a
  war-money bill: no slavery in any land taken from Mexico. {Wilmot Proviso}.
- It **passes the House 83 to 64**, then **dies in the Senate**. It comes back again and again and **never becomes law** (card "Watch for").
- **The vote map:** the yes votes come from the North and the no votes from the South, *in both parties*. {sectionalism}: loyalty to
  your region over your party. Show it as a split map, not a party chart. "Same party labels as today in name only" if needed.
- Why winning made it harder (card ASK): Texas had been settled as slave territory before it joined. This land was new, and every
  acre forced Congress to answer "slave or free?"
- Ralph Waldo Emerson, writing in 1846: "The United States will conquer Mexico, but it will be as the man swallows the arsenic, which
  brings him down in turn. Mexico will poison us."
- **"So let's go back to Lincoln's question."** The answer, pulling the threads together: the spot was in land both countries claimed.
  Supporters saw defense and destiny; critics saw conquest. Both readings were there from the start. The question nobody could
  settle wasn't where the war started. It was what the new land would be.
- Callback to chapter 5's officers: Grant later wrote that the Civil War was largely the outgrowth of the war with Mexico.
- **Closing line options** (pick one):
  - A: "Lincoln wanted to know where the war started. The bigger question turned out to be where it was going to end."
  - B: "Emerson said Mexico would poison us. It took 13 years to work."
  - C (series tease, bridge to Lesson 3): "Congress was going to have to answer that question. And the answer it came up with in 1850
    was a compromise. That's a story for next time."
- **End-screen line** (after the closing line lands): "Thanks for watching. If you want more stories like this one, subscribe, and
  I'll see you next time."

*Vocab:* Wilmot Proviso, sectionalism.
