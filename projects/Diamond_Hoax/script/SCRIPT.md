## 0:00 | Paid for Dirt (cold open)
*`ch01_cold_open.txt`* · 156 words · Hook

Two cousins from Kentucky walked into a rich man's office with a bag of rocks. By the time they were done, they'd walked away with about eight million dollars in today's money.

Their customers were some of the richest men in America. Bankers. Generals. The most famous jeweler in the country. [wry] Not the kind of people who usually lose money to two guys in slouch hats.

And what did those men buy? A diamond field. A hill out West where diamonds and rubies were just lying on the ground. Some of them were sitting in anthills.

There was just one problem. The diamonds were real. *The diamond field wasn't.*

[curious] So how do two cousins get the richest men in America to pay millions for dirt?

We'll get to those anthills. They're how the whole thing fell apart. But first, a knock on an office door, late at night, and a bag nobody wanted to talk about.

## 1:06 | Too Late for the Bank
*`ch02_too_late_for_the_bank.txt`* · 202 words · Act I · setup

San Francisco, about 150 years ago. It's evening, and a mining promoter named George Roberts is still at his desk when two scruffy men show up at his door.

They're cousins. Philip Arnold and John Slack. Arnold had been chasing gold out West since the Gold Rush. And he's holding a small leather bag like it might bite him.

They explain, a little nervously, that they'd meant to leave it at the Bank of California. But it was too late in the day. Could Mr. Roberts keep it safe?

[pause] Naturally, Roberts asks what's in it.

The cousins hesitate. They look at each other. Finally, Arnold lets it slip. {Rough diamonds}. Uncut, the way they come out of the ground.

From where? They won't say. Somewhere out West. Somewhere secret. And Roberts has to swear he won't tell a soul.

[dry] He kept that promise for about as long as it took to find a banker.

That banker was William Ralston. He ran the Bank of California, the same bank the cousins had been too late for, and he was the biggest money man on the West Coast.

[light] Which raises a question. Why would a man like that believe two guys with a bag?

## 2:21 | Everybody Wants a Diamond Mine
*`ch03_everybody_wants_a_diamond_mine.txt`* · 199 words · Act I · setup

Because he wanted to.

Ralston had helped make a fortune from the silver mines of Nevada. But men like him were always hunting for the next big strike. And right then, the hottest thing in the world was diamonds. A few years earlier, diamonds had turned up in South Africa, and a rush had started there. *So why not here?*

Then the cousins came back from another secret trip with a bigger bag. They said it was worth $600,000. A local jeweler reportedly took a look and said yes, these are real.

And they were. That's the trick. Arnold had worked as a bookkeeper at a company that made diamond drills, which use cheap, flawed diamonds as cutting tips. [wry] He knew exactly where to find a diamond nobody wanted.

So Ralston and his partners bought in. They agreed to pay the cousins $100,000 for a piece of the claim, half of it up front.

And the cousins spent some of that money on a trip. To London. Under fake names, they bought about $20,000 worth of rough diamonds and rubies. Low-grade stuff. Rejects.

[light] Now they had plenty of stones. What they needed was somebody famous to call them treasure.

## 3:36 | The Tiffany Test
*`ch04_the_tiffany_test.txt`* · 225 words · Act II · escalation (ends on the midpoint twist)

New York. A fancy house on Madison Avenue. In the room: two famous Civil War generals. The most famous newspaper editor in the country. A few nervous investors. And Charles Tiffany, of Tiffany and Company, the most famous jewelry store in America.

The stones go out on the table. Tiffany picks through them and holds them up to the light. And then he says this.

"Gentlemen, these are beyond question precious stones of enormous value."

Two days later, his number comes in. That sample, he says, is worth $150,000. And if that was just a sample? One of the investors did the math on the whole bag. *One and a half million dollars.*

That's called an {appraisal}: an expert's estimate of what something is worth. And an appraisal is only as good as the expert.

Here's what nobody asked. Tiffany sold diamonds that were cut and polished. He and his gem cutter had very little experience with rough stones straight out of the ground. [dry] Which is a bit like asking a chef to judge a cow.

The cousins had paid about $20,000 for their London stones. [wry] Tiffany had just made them sound like a fortune.

Now the money men were hooked. But they weren't stupid. Before they put in serious money, they wanted one more thing. *Somebody had to go stand on that diamond field.*

## 5:00 | The Long Way Around
*`ch05_the_long_way_around.txt`* · 268 words · Act II · escalation

And that was a problem. Because there was no diamond field. There was a bag.

[pause] So the cousins went out and made one.

Picture a flat, empty mesa in the far corner of northwest Colorado, near the Wyoming line. Sandstone, sagebrush, a lot of wind. Before any experts showed up, someone walked that ground with a bag of cheap stones. Pressing them into the dirt. Tucking them into cracks in the rock. Poking them into anthills.

That's called {salting}: planting something valuable where a buyer is going to dig, so the ground looks richer than it is. Miners had salted gold claims for years. [wry] Nobody had ever salted a desert with diamonds.

June 1872. The investors' expert arrives. Henry Janin, one of the best-known mining engineers in the country. The group rides the train to Rawlins, Wyoming. Then four days on horseback, with Arnold leading the way. And Arnold keeps getting lost. Doubling back. Going in circles. By one account, Janin was blindfolded for part of the ride. Either way, nobody was finding that place again without the cousins.

Then they arrive. And almost right away, the men start finding stones. Diamonds. Rubies. Right there in the dirt.

Janin was thrilled. He staked out about 3,000 acres and wrote a glowing report. The company's shares, he said, were easily worth $40 each.

[dry] And Janin would know. He'd been allowed to buy 1,000 of those shares at $10 each. He sold them at $40. That's about $30,000 on top of his fee, for saying yes.

But he'd seen it with his own eyes. What more could anyone ask for?

## 6:39 | Cash Out (+ plug)
*`ch06_cash_out.txt`* · 170 words · Act II · escalation (plug)

Now the real money moved. A new company was set up in New York with ten million dollars in stock. The names on the list were huge. One of those generals. The Rothschild banking family. Tiffany himself.

There was even a law to settle. Lawyers worried that America's new mining law didn't cover diamonds. So that summer, the U.S. Attorney General ruled that it did. [wry] The federal government officially agreed you could stake a claim on a diamond field. It just never asked to see one.

And the cousins cashed out. The investors bought them out completely. Altogether, Arnold collected about $550,000. Slack took about $100,000. That's around eight million dollars today.

Then they left. Arnold went home to Kentucky, a rich man. Slack just disappeared.

[warmly] Quick thing before we go on. If you're enjoying this, hit subscribe. It's free, and unlike Henry Janin, nobody's paying me in stock to say so.

[light] Because a few months later, in California, a geologist got on the wrong train. Or the right one.

## 7:43 | The Anthills
*`ch07_the_anthills.txt`* · 284 words · Act III · climax

October 1872. A train in California. On board are men from a government survey, geologists who'd spent five years mapping a long strip of the West, from California to the Rockies.

Their leader was Clarence King. Thirty years old, brilliant, and very sure of himself. And on that same train was Henry Janin, showing off diamonds.

[curious] That bothered King's team. They'd mapped that part of Colorado. They hadn't seen any diamonds. And something else didn't fit. Diamonds, rubies and sapphires form in very different kinds of rock. In nature, you basically never find them lying around together.

So King went to look for himself. Five days on horseback, in the cold. They found the mesa, and Janin's claim notices posted on it. And then they started finding stones.

Too many, in a way. Every time they found a diamond, there were about a dozen rubies near it. Every time. [dry] Nature is not that tidy.

Then they looked at the anthills. Some had rubies in them. And around those anthills, the ground had boot prints. The anthills without boot prints? No rubies. One of King's men figured someone had pushed the stones in with the end of a stick.

They dug a trench, ten feet long, down where real gems would be. Nothing.

Then a stranger rode up. A diamond dealer from New York, out hunting for the field himself. One of King's men blurted out that the whole thing was a fraud. And the dealer said:

"What a chance to sell short on the stock."

To {sell short} is to bet that a price will crash. [wry] He didn't want to warn anybody. He wanted to get paid.

[light] Which meant King was in a race.

## 9:28 | Not Enough Money
*`ch08_not_enough_money.txt`* · 175 words · Act III · climax

If the wrong people heard first, they could quietly sell their stock, and everyone else would be left holding paper worth nothing.

So King left before dawn. He rode about 45 miles to the nearest train station. He got to San Francisco on November 10th, went straight to Janin's hotel, and spent the whole night convincing him.

The next morning, he walked into Ralston's bank and read his report to the company's directors. The diamond field, he told them, was a fraud.

According to one of King's own men, a director suggested King might wait a little before he went public. Long enough for a few people to sell. King's answer:

"There is not enough money in the Bank of California to make me delay the publication a single hour."

[pause] The company sent its own man out to check. He came back and agreed. On November 26th, 1872, the story hit the papers. One San Francisco paper called it "the most gigantic and barefaced swindle of the age."

[light] So where did all that money go?

## 10:34 | Where the Money Went
*`ch09_paid_for_dirt.txt`* · 254 words · Act IV · fallout + takeaway

Not back to the investors. Ralston alone reportedly lost about $250,000.

Philip Arnold was charged with fraud in California. But he was home in Kentucky by then, and he never stood trial. He reportedly said he'd already hired a lawyer: "a good Henry rifle." [dry] A Henry rifle is not a lawyer.

He did settle one investor's lawsuit, for $150,000. Then he took his money and became a banker. [wry] Of course he did. A few years later, an argument with a rival banker turned into a shootout. Arnold was hit in the shoulder. He died of pneumonia about six months later.

And John Slack? You may have heard that he vanished. For a long time, that's what everyone thought. He didn't. He made coffins in St. Louis, then moved to a small town in New Mexico and became the local undertaker. He died there in 1896, leaving about $1,600.

[thoughtful] So let's go back to the question. How do two cousins get the richest men in America to pay millions for dirt?

They didn't do the convincing. The experts did. A famous jeweler who'd never judged a stone straight out of the ground. A mining engineer who made money by saying yes. And investors who wanted a diamond field so badly that nobody wanted to be the one to say no.

The diamonds were real. *The field was dirt.* And the richest men in America paid for it.

[warmly] Thanks for watching. If you want more stories like this one, subscribe, and I'll see you next time.
