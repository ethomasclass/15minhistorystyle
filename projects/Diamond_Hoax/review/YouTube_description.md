## Description
```
How do two cousins from Kentucky get the richest men in America to pay millions for dirt? In the Great Diamond Hoax of 1872, Philip Arnold and John Slack walked away with about $18 million in today's money, and they got Charles Tiffany to vouch for the stones.

This is the true story of the Great Diamond Hoax: a secret bag of rough diamonds, a banker who wanted to believe, a mesa in northwest Colorado salted with cheap stones, the diamond fever that followed, and the geologist who raced to expose the fraud before it could bring down the Bank of California. Along the way you'll learn the six moves almost every con still uses.

📚 SHOW NOTES
• Every dollar figure is in today's money, at about $27 for each 1872 dollar. The cousins took about $660,000 at the time.
• San Francisco: Arnold and Slack bring a bag of rough diamonds to a mining promoter's office, then to William Ralston of the Bank of California.
• London: under fake names, the cousins buy cheap rough diamonds and rubies.
• New York: Charles Tiffany appraises a sample in front of two Civil War generals and the editor Horace Greeley. Neither Tiffany nor his gem cutter knew much about uncut stones.
• Colorado: mining engineer Henry Janin inspects the salted field, writes a glowing report and profits on company shares.
• The Golconda company: more than a quarter of a billion dollars in stock, backed by names including the Rothschilds and Tiffany.
• Clarence King and the Fortieth Parallel Survey: too many rubies, boot prints around the anthills, and a diamond dealer who wanted to sell short.
• November 26, 1872: the fraud hits the papers.
• Afterward: the shootout that led to Arnold's death, Slack's life as a New Mexico undertaker, the Panic of 1873, and the 1875 run on the Bank of California.
• The six moves: the bait, play hard to get, borrowed trust, the proof, the rush, the exit.
```
