## Description

How did two cousins from Kentucky get the richest men in America to pay millions for dirt? They walked into a San Francisco office with a bag of rough diamonds, and walked away with about $18 million in today's money.

This is the Great Diamond Hoax of 1872: a secret diamond field out West, the banker William Ralston and the Bank of California, Charles Tiffany's famous appraisal, a salted mesa in northwest Colorado, diamond fever, and the geologist Clarence King, who found rubies in the anthills and blew the whole thing open. Along the way: the six moves almost every con still uses, and how you can spot them.

📚 SHOW NOTES
• Money: a dollar in 1872 was worth about 27 of today's dollars, so every figure in the video is in today's money.
• The bait: Philip Arnold and John Slack bring a bag of rough diamonds to San Francisco, "too late for the bank." Arnold had worked as a bookkeeper for a diamond-drill company and knew where to get cheap, flawed stones. In London, under fake names, the cousins bought low-grade rough diamonds and rubies.
• The investors: William Ralston of the Bank of California, two Civil War generals (George McClellan and Benjamin Butler), the newspaper editor Horace Greeley, and later the Rothschild banking family.
• The Tiffany test: in New York, Charles Tiffany called the sample "precious stones of enormous value." He sold cut diamonds and knew little about rough stones.
• The proof: in June 1872 the mining engineer Henry Janin rode four days to the field, found stones himself, and wrote a glowing report. He also bought shares cheap and sold them at full price.
• The rush and the exit: a company worth more than a quarter of a billion dollars in stock, "diamond fever" in the papers, and the cousins bought out before anyone checked.
• The anthills: Clarence King's 40th Parallel Survey found the mesa. Diamonds, rubies and sapphires lying together, about a dozen rubies by every diamond, and rubies only in the anthills with boot prints around them.
• Short selling, explained: a diamond dealer who found King's camp saw "a chance to sell short on the stock."
• The dominoes: insiders, the crash, a run on the Bank of California, everyone else. King: "There is not enough money in the Bank of California to make me delay the publication a single hour." The hoax hit the papers on November 26, 1872.
• What happened next: Arnold never stood trial, settled one lawsuit and became a banker; Slack became a small-town undertaker in New Mexico; the Bank of California ran out of cash in 1875, and Ralston drowned the next day.
