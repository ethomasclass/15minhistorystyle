## How to read this file

- **VERIFIED**: confirmed by two or more independent sources, or by an authoritative primary or official source plus one other.
- **SINGLE-SOURCE**: one reliable source only. Usable, but say it carefully or attribute it ("according to...").
- **DISPUTED/MYTH**: sources disagree, or the claim is wrong or overstated.

## 1. How barrage balloons worked

### The basic idea

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| A barrage balloon is a tethered, lighter-than-air balloon. Its steel cable can damage or tangle an attacking plane. Flying at irregular heights, the balloons force enemy planes to fly higher, so their attacks are less accurate. | VERIFIED | NPS ("flew at irregular heights and intervals... steel cables that could damage or entangle enemy aircraft... forced enemy planes to fly higher"); NASM |
| The balloons were filled with **hydrogen**. Trainees were taught to keep sparks and static away from the gas so it would not ignite. | VERIFIED | NASM; Hervieux/NBC ("filled with hydrogen") |
| The cable of the D-Day balloons carried a **small explosive charge**. If a plane hit the cable, the cable snagged the wing and pulled the charge into it. | SINGLE-SOURCE (official) | NASM. Dabney's description, "It was something like a canister bomb," fits this, but I found it only as a video quote reported secondhand (see section 6). |
| The commander, Lt. Col. Leon Reed, described the purpose as forcing enemy planes above or around the barrage, where anti-aircraft guns could reach them. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Military.com, Nov 11, 2015: https://www.military.com/daily-news/2015/11/11/army-medic-all-black-world-war-ii-unit-backed-medal-of-honor.html |

Narration tip: the balloon does not have to hit anything. It is a "keep out" sign in the sky. Low-flying dive bombers and strafing planes have to climb into the guns or risk hitting an invisible steel wire.

### The D-Day balloons: "Very Low Altitude" (VLA)

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| The 320th used **VLA (very low altitude)** balloons, about **35 feet long**, which flew **below 2,000 feet**. | VERIFIED | NASM (35 ft long, below 2,000 ft); Military.com 2015 ("about 2,000 feet of tether"); H.R. 10110 (118th) says "up to 2,000 feet" |
| Each balloon held **over 3,000 cubic feet** of hydrogen. | SINGLE-SOURCE (official) | NASM |
| Each balloon weighed about **125 pounds**. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Military.com 2015. (Hervieux/NBC says they were "anchored by 125-pound gasbags", which is garbled wording; don't use it.) |
| **How high they actually flew at Normandy**: H.R. 4621 says "about 200 feet". NASM says "below 2,000 feet". | DISPUTED | H.R. 4621 vs NASM. They may not contradict each other: 2,000 ft was the ceiling, and 200 ft may describe how they were often flown over the beach. Safe wording: "a few hundred feet up, never more than about 2,000." |
| Soldiers refitted RL-31 Signal Corps winches as balloon reels; they weighed about 35 pounds and were mounted on jeeps. | SINGLE-SOURCE | H.R. 4621 |
| **Getting them ashore**: balloons were inflated in England and tied to ships for the Channel crossing. Teams of four or five men then walked the inflated balloons from the ships to the shore. | VERIFIED | NASM; NARA photo 111-SC-191713 shows a crew walking a balloon to its winch (section 7). Wikipedia (citing Hervieux p. 236) says normal 5-man crews were cut to 3 or 4 for Normandy. |
| The battalion was spread across **more than 100 landing craft** bound for Omaha and Utah. | SINGLE-SOURCE (official) | NASM |

### Compared with British balloons in the Blitz

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| The standard British (RAF Balloon Command, "LZ") barrage balloon was about **64 feet long**, held about **19,000–20,000 cubic feet** of hydrogen, and was meant to fly up to about **5,000 feet**, sometimes higher. | VERIFIED as approximate (sources differ slightly) | Wikipedia, "Barrage balloon": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrage_balloon (64.2 ft, 19,150 cu ft, up to 5,000 ft, often 6,500); Pennsylvania Military Museum blog: https://pamilmuseum.org/blog/barrage-balloons-by-dr-andrew-vavreck (about 66 ft, about 20,000 cu ft) |
| By mid-1940 Britain had about **1,400** balloons, around a third of them over London. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Wikipedia, "Barrage balloon" |
| **Takeaway**: a D-Day VLA balloon was roughly **half the length** and held about **one-sixth of the gas** of a London Blitz balloon, and it flew far lower. | VERIFIED (arithmetic on the figures above: 35 ft vs ~64 ft; 3,000 vs ~19,000 cu ft) | NASM + Wikipedia |

Narration tip: "London's balloons were the size of a house. The D-Day balloons were small enough for four men to walk up a beach."

## 2. The 320th Barrage Balloon Battalion (VLA)

### Formation and training

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| All-Black unit, **formed in 1942**. | VERIFIED | NPS; NASM; H.R. 4621 |
| Exact activation date: **September 21, 1942** (H.R. 4621 and Wikipedia). Another bill text gives **December 10, 1942**. | DISPUTED (minor) | H.R. 4621; the December date was reported from a 2024 bill in a search summary that I could not confirm. Just say "1942." |
| Trained at **Camp Tyson, near Paris, Tennessee**, the nation's only barrage balloon training center. Camp Tyson trained 30+ battalions, four of them all-Black: the **318th, 319th, 320th and 321st**. | VERIFIED | NPS; NASM; Tennessee Encyclopedia: https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/?p=490 |
| A full balloon battalion had about **1,100 men and 50+ balloons**. The Army.mil article says the 320th had **about 700** soldiers in five batteries plus a headquarters battery. | VERIFIED (1,100 standard size) / SINGLE-SOURCE (700) | NPS and NASM (1,100); Army.mil 2014 (700) |

### D-Day

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| Landed on **both Omaha and Utah** beaches on June 6, 1944. | VERIFIED | NASM; NPS; H.R. 4621; Army.mil 2014 |
| **More than 600** men in the assault force, **most on Omaha**. The usual exact figure is **621**. | VERIFIED (600+) / SINGLE-SOURCE-ish (621) | NASM ("more than 600"); 621: Military.com 2015 and Wikipedia (no inline cite). Safe: "about 600 men." |

### How long they stayed

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| Battery A moved to Cherbourg in late July 1944; the rest stayed on Omaha and Utah until early October. Service in France ended **October 24, 1944**. | VERIFIED | H.R. 4621; Wikipedia (citing Hervieux p. 251) |
| Length of stay: **110 days** (H.R. 4621), **140 days** (Wikipedia/Hervieux), **almost 150 days** (NASM). | DISPUTED | June 6 to Oct 24 is 140 days, so 140 fits the dates best. Say "**more than four months**." |

### "The only African American combat unit at D-Day": precise wording

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| The 320th was **the only Black *combat* unit** in the D-Day landings. | VERIFIED | Army.mil 2014; AP 2024 ("the only African American combat unit at Normandy that day"); History.com ("the lone African American combat unit on D-Day"); Daily Beast (Hervieux) |
| It was **not the only Black unit there.** About **2,000 African American troops** landed on Omaha and Utah by nightfall on D-Day, mostly in service and labor units. | VERIFIED | Hervieux/TIME ("nearly 2,000"); AP 2024 ("about 2,000"); Hervieux/NBC |

## 4. Segregation context (APUSH)

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| The WWII Army was **segregated by race**. Most Black soldiers were put in service and labor units. Black officers faced quotas and could not command white officers. | VERIFIED | Hervieux/NBC; Hervieux/TIME; NPS (Woodson's OCS story) |
| Official justification: the **1925 Army War College study, "The Use of Negro Manpower in War,"** which claimed Black soldiers were inferior and recommended white leadership and strict segregation. | VERIFIED | Army Heritage and Education Center bibliography: https://armyheritage.org/wp-content/uploads/ref-bibs/subjects/Ethnic%20Groups/Black%20Americans/Blacks%20in%20the%20Army%20Interwar.pdf ; CUNY Social History for Every Classroom: https://shec.ashp.cuny.edu/items/show/808 ; Hervieux/NBC (quotes its claim that the Black soldier "naturally believes himself inferior to the white") |
| More than **1 million** African Americans served in WWII (AP says about 1.2 million). | VERIFIED | AP 2024; Hervieux/NBC |
| **German POWs treated better than Black GIs**, example 1: Cpl. **Rupert Trimmingham**'s letter in *Yank* magazine (**April 28, 1944**). Stuck overnight at a Louisiana railroad station, he and eight other Black soldiers had to eat in the kitchen while about two dozen German POWs with two guards ate in the lunchroom. He asked why the country allowed this when Black men were fighting for it. He later said he got 287 letters in reply, 183 of them from white readers. | VERIFIED | Library of America: https://www.loa.org/news-and-views/1345 ; Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Trimmingham |
| Example 2: on a USO tour, **Lena Horne** refused to perform for an audience where German POWs sat in front of Black soldiers. She walked off the stage and sang to the Black soldiers, with the POWs behind her, then complained to the NAACP. Usually placed at **Fort Riley, Kansas**. | VERIFIED (story) / location best-attested as Fort Riley | NPS: https://www.nps.gov/features/malu/feat0002/wof/Lena_Horne.htm ; Army.mil (Fort Knox article, site blocked to me, from search excerpt): https://www.army.mil/article/217981/fort_knox_general_club_director_host_black_entertainers_ease_tensions_in_pre_integrated_army |
| Example 3 (the 320th's own): at Camp Tyson, Tennessee, POWs could eat in town restaurants that would not serve the men of the 320th. | VERIFIED | Military.com 2015; History.com |
| Woodson on the beach: "At that time, they didn't care what color my skin was." | SINGLE-SOURCE | Military.com 2015 |
| **Double V campaign**: began with **James G. Thompson**'s letter in the *Pittsburgh Courier*, **January 31, 1942**: "Should I Sacrifice to Live 'Half American'?" He proposed a second V: victory over the Axis abroad and over racism at home. The *Courier* launched the campaign in **February 1942** (Double V logo first ran Feb. 7, 1942). Thompson was a 26-year-old cafeteria worker at an aircraft plant in Wichita, Kansas, kept off the factory floor. | VERIFIED | NPS Port Chicago: https://home.nps.gov/poch/learn/historyculture/the-double-v-campaign.htm ; PBS: https://www.pbs.org/wnet/african-americans-many-rivers-to-cross/history/what-was-black-americas-doub ; Dickinson College transcription: https://blogs.dickinson.edu/hist-118pinsker/?p=6325 . (The Feb. 7 date is from the Wikipedia article, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_V_campaign . Say "February 1942".) |
| The *Courier* that pushed Double V is the same paper that called Woodson the "No. 1 Invasion Hero." | VERIFIED | NPS (Woodson); Double V sources above. A good link for the script. |

## 8. Myths to avoid

1. **"There were no Black soldiers at D-Day."** False. About 2,000 Black troops landed on June 6 (Hervieux/TIME; AP 2024).
2. **"The 320th were the only Black soldiers at D-Day."** False. They were the only Black *combat* unit; port, truck and quartermaster units were there too (NNPA; theGrio).
3. **"Dabney (or Parham) was the last survivor."** Each was called that, and each claim was shaky (Boston Globe 2016; Veterans Breakfast Club).
4. **"Woodson got the Medal of Honor."** He has not. He got the Bronze Star (1944), and the Distinguished Service Cross posthumously (2024).
5. **"Woodson saved 200 lives."** The sources say he *treated* about 200 men; how many survived is unknown. H.R. 4621 says "believed to have saved 200". Say "treated."
6. **"Barrage balloons were huge, like the ones over London."** The D-Day VLA balloons were small, about 35 ft long and hand-carried.
7. **Eisenhower "personally praised them on the beach."** It was a written commendation, not a beach visit.
8. **"EO 9981 desegregated America."** It covered the armed forces only, and was carried out slowly (fully by about 1954).
9. **Lena Horne at "Camp Robinson."** Best-attested location is Fort Riley, Kansas.
10. **"The DSC was given at Arlington."** No. The 2023 Arlington ceremony was the Bronze Star; the DSC was laid at Omaha (June 3, 2024) and presented to the family on Capitol Hill (Sept 24, 2024).

## Best story beats for narration

- **The small balloon.** London's balloons were giants. The D-Day ones were 35-foot hydrogen bags that four men walked up the beach by hand, dodging mines, to hang steel wire over 150,000 men (NASM).
- **The men who weren't supposed to be there.** Woodson was a pre-med student who aced the Army's test and went to officer school, and then was told the anti-aircraft branch "could not house" a Black officer. They made him a medic instead (NPS). It is the reason he was on the beach at all.
- **Thirty hours.** Wounded on his landing craft ("They raked the whole top of the ship and killed all the crew"), he treated himself, then set up an aid station under fire and worked about 30 hours, treating about 200 men, before he collapsed. After three days he asked to go back (NPS; AP 2024).
- **Better than us: the POWs.** At Camp Tyson the men of the 320th watched German prisoners eat in restaurants that would not serve them (Military.com; History.com). Trimmingham's 1944 *Yank* letter told the same story from a Louisiana station. This is Double V in one image.
- **"At that time, they didn't care what color my skin was."** On Omaha, under fire, segregation briefly didn't matter. Then the 320th came home to slurs at a Georgia base (History.com).
- **The medal that went missing.** He was recommended for the Medal of Honor; the paperwork vanished; a 1973 fire destroyed his records. Eighty years later, in June 2024, an Army general laid a Distinguished Service Cross in the sand at Omaha Beach where he had bled (NPS; AP). He still has not received the Medal of Honor.
- **"I've been waiting for someone to call me for 50 years."** (Wilson Monk to Hervieux). The theme of being forgotten, and a way to end the chapter.
- **The balloons are back.** Tethered aerostats watch the US border today, and in 2026 both sides in Ukraine are hanging nets and cables to snag drones, the same idea the 320th carried up Omaha Beach (CBP; Militarnyi; NPR).
