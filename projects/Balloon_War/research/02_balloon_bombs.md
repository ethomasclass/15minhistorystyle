# Chapter 2 research: The Deadly One (Japan's Fu-Go balloon bombs, 1944–45, and Bly, Oregon)

Research for "The Balloon War: three balloons that fought World War Two" (15 Minute History).
Compiled 2026-10-17.

## How to read this file

- **VERIFIED**: confirmed by two or more independent sources, or by the authoritative primary or near-primary source plus one other.
- **SINGLE-SOURCE**: one reliable source only. Usable, but say it carefully or attribute it.
- **DISPUTED/MYTH**: sources disagree, or the claim is wrong or overstated.

### The key source

The anchor source is **Robert C. Mikesh, *Japan's World War II Balloon Bomb Attacks on North America*, Smithsonian Annals of Flight no. 9 (Smithsonian Institution Press / National Air and Space Museum, 1973).** It is the Smithsonian's own official monograph. It draws on postwar interviews with Japanese officers and on US Army records, and it includes a full appendix of 285 incidents.

- Smithsonian repository record: https://repository.si.edu/handle/10088/18679 (DOI https://doi.org/10.5479/si.AnnalsFlight.9)
- Full-text scan on the Internet Archive, marked Public Domain Mark 1.0: https://archive.org/details/japanese-balloon-bombs-1943
- OCR text, which is what I quoted from: https://archive.org/download/japanese-balloon-bombs-1943/japanese-balloon-bombs-1943_djvu.txt

Below, "Mikesh p. X" means the printed page number in that monograph.

**Ross Coen, *Fu-Go* (Univ. of Nebraska Press, 2014)** is the main modern book. I could not read it directly. Where I cite it, the page numbers come from the Wikipedia article's citations (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fu-Go_balloon_bomb), so treat those as one step removed. **Bert Webber's *Silent Siege*** was not available online, and no claim below depends on it.

### Altitude control: the "clever" part

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| By day, sunlight heated the gas and the balloon rose. At night the gas cooled to about -50°C and the balloon sank. Without a control system it would burst or fall into the sea. | VERIFIED | Mikesh p. 12; Wikipedia summary of Mikesh/Coen https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_balloon |
| A **gas relief valve** at the bottom vented hydrogen if the balloon rose too high (about 38,000 ft). | VERIFIED | Mikesh p. 12; Smithsonian *Air & Space* |
| A **cast-aluminum wheel** about 24 in across held **32 sandbags**. **Aneroid barometers** (four in all, three wired in parallel for redundancy) closed a battery circuit when the balloon sank to about 30,000 ft. That fired **blowout plugs** (small black-powder charges) that dropped **two sandbags at a time**, and the balloon rose again. | VERIFIED | Mikesh p. 12 and pp. 45–46 (detailed description); Smithsonian *Air & Space* ("Thirty-two paper sandbags … released by small explosive charges … triggered by barometric switches"); Coen pp. 33–40 via Wikipedia |
| When the sandbags ran out, after about three days of up-and-down cycles, the same mechanism **dropped the bombs** and then a **demolition charge destroyed the balloon** so it would leave no evidence. The number of cycles was calculated from forecast winds. | VERIFIED | Mikesh p. 12; Wikipedia/Fire balloon summary |
| The system **switched on about 52 minutes after launch**. | SINGLE-SOURCE (via Wikipedia, citing a secondary source) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_balloon |
| The device was developed under **Technical Major Otsuki** of the **Noborito Research Institute**. | SINGLE-SOURCE (primary) | Mikesh p. 12 |

### The jet stream and Wasaburo Oishi

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| **Wasaburo Oishi (Ooishi)**, director of Japan's **Tateno** Aerological Observatory, made about **1,200–1,300 pilot-balloon observations (1923–1925)**. They showed a persistent, very fast west-to-east wind high over Japan, which we now call the jet stream. He published in **Esperanto**, so the world mostly ignored it. | VERIFIED | Smithsonian *Air & Space* (Rebecca Maksel, 2018): "almost 1,300 observations," "published in Esperanto" https://www.smithsonianmag.com/air-space-magazine/as-next-may-unbelievablebuttrue-180968355/ ; Cosmos Magazine https://cosmosmagazine.com/history/wasaburo-ooishi-and-the-jet-stream ; Wikipedia (1,228 observations, Mar 1923–Feb 1925) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasaburo_Oishi |
| Oishi "**discovered**" the jet stream. | DISPUTED in nuance | Most sources credit him as the first to document it scientifically. Cosmos notes earlier hints, such as Glaisher in the 1860s. | The military planners got their wind data from meteorologist **Hidetoshi Arakawa** of the Central Meteorological Observatory, who built on Oishi's work. | VERIFIED (Arakawa) / SINGLE-SOURCE (the link to Oishi) | Mikesh pp. 9–10 names Arakawa and seven radiosonde stations but **does not mention Oishi**. Smithsonian *Air & Space* (Maksel 2018) states the Arakawa–Ooishi link. |
| The jet stream is strongest **November–March**, which is why the launches were in winter. | VERIFIED | Mikesh p. 10; Nippon.com |
| **Travel time:** the Japanese estimated **30–100 hours, averaging about 60 hours** (about 2.5 days), over roughly 6,200 miles of ocean. "About three days" is a fair round figure. | VERIFIED | Mikesh p. 10 ("thirty to one hundred hours with an average time of sixty hours"); AHF ("about 60 hours"); Wikipedia/Fire balloon ("about three days"); Nippon.com ("about two days if released in November") |

### The Hanford incident (worth a beat)

## 4. The US response

### The press blackout

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| On **4 January 1945** the **Office of Censorship** asked newspapers and radio to give "no publicity whatsoever" to balloon incidents. Compliance was **voluntary**, and it held "from coast to coast." | VERIFIED | Mikesh p. 26–27; AHF timeline ("Jan 4, 1945"); Coen pp. 162, 169 via Wikipedia |
| **Why:** to stop Japan from learning whether the balloons arrived or where they landed, and to prevent panic. Japan screened US, Chinese and Soviet press for reports. The only report it got was the Thermopolis story, repeated in the Chinese paper *Takungpao* in late December 1944. | VERIFIED | Mikesh pp. 25–26; AHF |
| An Office of Censorship note to editors (about April 1945): "your refusal to publish or broadcast information about these balloons has baffled the Japanese, annoyed and hindered them." | SINGLE-SOURCE (primary, quoted in Mikesh) | Mikesh p. 27 |
| Japanese propaganda (Domei, 17 Feb 1945) falsely claimed big fires and casualties in America. | VERIFIED | AHF timeline; Mikesh discusses Japanese broadcasts (pp. 36–37) |
| The blackout made it **hard to warn the public**. After Bly, a **joint War and Navy Department statement on 22 May 1945** described the balloons and warned people not to touch them, saying "the possible saving of even one American life … would more than offset any military gain" to Japan. | VERIFIED | Mikesh p. 27–28; Smithsonian Magazine 2019 ("On May 22, 1945, the War Department confirmed…"); Coen pp. 177–179 via Wikipedia |
| Was the blackout fully "lifted" on 22 May? | NUANCE | The 22 May statement ended the silence about the *existence* of the danger. Mikesh says Bly "caused the government to abandon its campaign of silence." Details stayed restricted, and in 1945 the deaths were first reported only as an explosion of "undetermined origin" or an "unidentified object." (Mikesh p. 50; Oregon Encyclopedia summary via search) |

## 5. Bly, Oregon, 5 May 1945 (handle with care)

### What happened

| Claim | Status | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|

### The six who died

| **Jay Gifford** | 13 | 12 |
| **Edward "Eddie" Engen** | 13 | 13 |

## 9. Myths and pitfalls to avoid

1. **"The balloons carried germs or poison."** MYTH as stated.
   - The **fear** was real and official. In March 1945 a War Department list ranked "bacteriological or chemical warfare" as the #1 possible use. The "Lightning Project" quietly alerted vets, 4-H clubs and agricultural colleges to watch for strange livestock and crop diseases, and stockpiled decontamination chemicals (Mikesh p. 29).
   - Mikesh himself wrote: "Had this balloon weapon been further exploited by using germ or gas bombs, the results could have been disastrous" (p. 1).
   - But **no recovered balloon carried a biological or chemical payload**, and a postwar US investigation found no such plans had been carried out (Coen pp. 137–139, 191–193, via Wikipedia).
   - Claims that Noborito proposed **rinderpest** (cattle plague) as a payload and that Tojo vetoed it are SINGLE-SOURCE/unverified. They trace to Amanda McVety's *The Rinderpest Campaigns* via a blog. If used, say "some historians report Japan considered it."
2. **"Japan stopped because of the sand detectives / B-29s hit the launch sites."** Overstated. See section 4. Japan stopped mainly because of hydrogen-plant damage and no feedback. The sand result arrived too late to matter operationally.
3. **"Thousands hit America."** No. About 9,300 were launched, about 300 incidents were recorded, and perhaps 1,000 arrived.
4. **"Only deaths on US soil."** Say **"on the US mainland"** or "continental United States."
5. **"Seven family members killed"** (Nippon.com): wrong. The victims were a pastor's wife and five church children from several families. You may note the unborn child.
6. **Ages of the children:** these vary by source (see section 5). Use the plaque or "11 to 14."
7. **"The schoolgirls apologized."** The women expressed regret through 1,000 cranes. Avoid "apology."
8. **"Oishi's research was used directly to plan the attacks."** This is plausible and stated by Smithsonian *Air & Space*. Mikesh credits Arakawa and does not name Oishi. Phrase it as "built on."
9. **"Balloons nearly stopped the Manhattan Project."** Overstated (see section 3).
10. **Mikesh says "near Lakeview, Oregon"** in one passage (p. 27). The site is near **Bly** (Lake County). Lakeview is the county seat.

## Best story beats for narration

- **The weapon made of paper and potato paste:** a 33-foot hydrogen balloon of mulberry-bark paper glued with konnyaku. The paste was so food-like that hungry workers stole it to eat (Mikesh).
- **Schoolgirls in a sumo hall:** thousands of teenage girls, told nothing and sworn to secrecy, pasted panels with no hairpins and trimmed nails. The balloons were inflated inside Tokyo's Nichigeki Music Hall and the sumo arena (Mikesh; Nippon.com).
- **A flying computer with no electronics:** barometers, a battery and black-powder plugs dropped two sandbags at a time for three days. Then it dropped its bombs and blew itself up to hide the evidence.
- **The wind discovered in Esperanto:** Oishi mapped the jet stream in the 1920s, and almost nobody read it. Japan used that wind to aim at America from 5,000+ miles away, launching first on Emperor Meiji's birthday.
- **The silence was a weapon too:** America's voluntary press blackout left Japan blind, while geologists matched ballast sand to a Japanese beach. Japan, hearing nothing and with its hydrogen plants bombed, quit in April 1945.
- **Bly (slow down, serious tone):** a pastor's pregnant wife and five children on a Saturday picnic found something in the woods, a balloon that had probably lain there for weeks after Japan stopped launching. Name all six.
- **The cost of secrecy:** the blackout that protected the country also meant those children had never been warned. Seventeen days later the government finally told the public.
- **Paper cranes and a modern echo:** in 1987 the girls who made the paper, now grandmothers, sent 1,000 cranes to Bly, carried there by a Japanese American who had been interned 50 miles away. Bombs still turn up in Canadian forests (2014). In 2023 a fighter jet shot down a balloon from Asia riding the same high winds, and those winds still make your eastbound flight faster.
