## 1. What the unit was

### Formation and size
| Claim | Status | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Activated **January 20, 1944, at Camp Forrest, Tennessee**, as the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops, commanded by Col. Harry L. Reeder. | VERIFIED | NWWII; GALP |
| Authorized strength **82 officers and 1,023 enlisted men**, so about **1,100 men**. | VERIFIED | NWWII; GALP; Smithsonian 2013 |
| The idea came from planners including Ralph Ingersoll (a magazine editor turned staff officer) and Billy Harris. | VERIFIED | NWWII (Ingersoll); Wikipedia lists both https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_Army |
| Its job: make about 1,100 men look like a force of two divisions, roughly **30,000 men**. | VERIFIED | NWWII; WUNC 2024 |

### Components (all part of the "Ghost Army")
The statute lists the 23rd as made up of its HQ Company, the 603d Engineer Camouflage Battalion, the 406th Combat Engineer Company, the 3132d Signal Service Company, and the Signal Company, Special. The 3133d Signal Service Company was a separate sister unit that served in Italy. (PL 117-85, finding 1) **VERIFIED (primary)**

| Unit | Job | Men | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **603rd Engineer Camouflage Battalion** | Visual deception: inflatable tanks, trucks, jeeps, artillery, planes. Mostly artists. | 379 | NWWII; GALP (VERIFIED) |
| **Signal Company, Special** (ex-244th Signal Co.) | "Spoof radio": fake radio traffic imitating real units. | about 296-300 | GALP (296); NWWII (~300) (VERIFIED) |
| **3132nd Signal Service Company** | Sonic deception: recorded sounds played from huge speakers on half-tracks. | 145 | GALP (SINGLE-SOURCE for the number) |
| **406th Engineer Combat Company** | The only real fighters: guarded the perimeter, posed as MPs, did construction. Led by Capt. George Rebh. | 168 | NWWII; GALP (VERIFIED) |
| **3133rd Signal Service Company** (Italy) | Sonic deception on the Gothic Line, April 1945 (Operation Craftsman), working with a platoon of British inflatable-decoy specialists. | small | PL 117-85 findings 3, 23-24 (VERIFIED, primary) |

- The 3132nd and 3133rd were **the only "sonic deception" ground combat units the US had in WWII**. Both were activated at Pine Camp (now Fort Drum), NY, in 1944. (PL 117-85, finding 16) **VERIFIED (primary)**
- **Naming trap:** some sites (even NWWII and the GALP page title) mix up "3132nd" and "3133rd". The 3132nd went to France with the 23rd. The 3133rd went to Italy. The Gold Medal law names the **3133rd** because it was separate from the 23rd. **VERIFIED** (PL 117-85)

### The four tools of deception
1. **Visual**: inflatables, fake tank tracks, fake gun positions, flash canisters to fake artillery fire. (NWWII; GALP-Ops) **VERIFIED**
2. **Sonic**: sounds of tanks, trucks, bridge-building. The sounds were recorded at **Fort Knox**, Kentucky, with help from **Bell Labs** engineers. They were first put on records, later on wire recorders. They were played from speakers weighing about **500 pounds** mounted on half-tracks. **VERIFIED** for Fort Knox and the 500-lb speakers (WBUR 2012, where veteran Jack McGlynn describes recording at Fort Knox; Smithsonian 2013; Smithsonian 2022). **SINGLE-SOURCE** for the Bell Labs and wire-recorder details (Task & Purpose, https://taskandpurpose.com/history/ghost-army-wwii).
   - Range: **about 15 miles** is the common figure (NWWII; Smithsonian 2013; WUNC 2024). It varied in practice. At Brest an officer estimated **2.5 miles** (GALP-Ops). Claims of 50 miles are not credible.
3. **Radio ("spoof radio")**: operators posed as the radio staff of real divisions, copying their Morse code styles and sending fake but realistic traffic. **VERIFIED** (Smithsonian 2022; WUNC 2024; PL 117-85 finding 15)
4. **"Special effects" / atmosphere**: fake shoulder patches sewn on, fake markings painted on trucks, fake headquarters with **fake generals**, and loose talk in bars and cafes where spies might hear. **VERIFIED (primary)**: PL 117-85 finding 17 says they "impersonated other, larger Army units by sewing counterfeit patches onto their uniforms, painting false markings on their vehicles, and creating phony headquarters staffed by fake generals." Café talk: History.com; Smithsonian 2022 (Bluestein); GALP-Ops (Bettembourg, men "on leave" in cafes).

### How many operations?
| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **21 full-scale tactical deceptions**, starting with Operation Elephant (July 1-4, 1944). | VERIFIED (primary) | PL 117-85 findings 13-14 |
| "More than 20" | VERIFIED (consistent) | GALP; NWWII; History.com |
| "22 operations" | Minor variant | WUNC 2024 |

**Use "more than 20" or "21."** The law is the best citation.

- Active in battle **June 1944 through March 1945**. (PL 117-85 finding 2) **VERIFIED**
- **First members ashore on D-Day**: "the first 4 members ... landed on D-Day and 2 became casualties while creating false beach landing sites." (PL 117-85 finding 7) **VERIFIED (primary)**
- **Task Force Mason**, 16 men under 1st Lt. Bernard Mason, reached Normandy **June 14, 1944** and set up dummy artillery to draw fire away from the real 980th Field Artillery. (PL 117-85 findings 10-11) **VERIFIED (primary)**
- Lt. Fred Fox's radio men were meant for a deception called Operation Troutfly, which was cancelled. They were instead given to the 82nd Airborne, which had lost 95% of its radios. (PL 117-85 findings 8-9) **VERIFIED (primary)**. A good surprise detail.
- **Battle of the Bulge**: fake radio traffic hid the movement of Patton's Third Army toward Bastogne. (PL 117-85 finding 18) **VERIFIED (primary)**

### Key operations
**Operation Brest (Aug 20-27, 1944), Brittany, France** (GALP-Ops; NWWII)
- The first time all three methods (visual, sonic, radio) were used together. **VERIFIED** (GALP-Ops; ghostarmy.org activation archive)
- Task Force X (about 200 men under Capt. Oscar Seale) set up **53 dummy tanks** near Kerlin. Flash canisters faked artillery and drew German counter-fire away from the real 37th Field Artillery Battalion, which "received no shells". **SINGLE-SOURCE** (GALP-Ops, from the unit's own history)
- Sound half-tracks drove to "just a few hundred meters from German lines." (GALP-Ops) **SINGLE-SOURCE**
- Honest result: it partly worked. 20 to 50 German 88mm guns were reportedly shifted to the flanks. But the larger goal, scaring General Ramcke into surrendering, failed: he had about 38,000 troops, not 21,000, and held out 27 more days. A real tank company (Co. D, 709th Tank Bn) attacking nearby lost five tanks. (GALP-Ops) **SINGLE-SOURCE**
- Jack Masey called Brest "the biggest piece of action I ever saw in World War II." (GALP-Ops)

**Operation Bettembourg (Sept 15-22, 1944), Luxembourg** (GALP-Ops; GALP archive https://ghostarmy.org/thearchive/operation-bettembourg-14-22-sept-1944-)
- Patton's Third Army had a thinly held gap in its line. The Ghost Army pretended to be parts of the **6th Armored Division** to make it look strong. **VERIFIED** (GALP; 99% Invisible https://99percentinvisible.org/episode/show-of-force/)
- About **500 men posed as about 8,000**. Planned for 2 days, it lasted 8 because the real relief unit (83rd Infantry Division) was late. (GALP-Ops) **SINGLE-SOURCE**. The size of the gap is quoted as 50, 70 or 75 miles in different places, so **don't give a number** (DISPUTED).
- Lt. Bob Conrad: nothing stood between them and the Germans "but our hopes and prayers." (GALP-Ops)
- The first Ghost Army historical marker stands in Bettembourg. (GALP; Wikimedia Commons photos, see section 7)

**Operation Viersen (March 18-24, 1945), Rhine crossing, Germany** (GALP-Viersen, the official history; PL 117-85 findings 19-21; Simpson)
- The last and biggest operation. The unit pretended to be **two whole divisions, the 30th and 79th Infantry**, in the XIII Corps zone near Viersen. The real Ninth Army crossing was farther north, in the XVI Corps zone. **VERIFIED (primary)**
- Each fake division had **nearly 100 rubber vehicles plus 5 liaison planes**, and there were 64 rubber 40mm and 16 rubber 90mm anti-aircraft guns, plus artillery dummies, radio nets and sound. (GALP-Viersen) **VERIFIED (primary)**. The popular figure "**600 inflatables**" (NWWII; History.com) is a round total. It is plausible, but the official history doesn't state it.
- Results: the 30th Division's intelligence officer called the attack a "complete surprise to the enemy." A captured German map placed the 79th where the fakes said it was and lost track of the 30th altogether. The Ninth Army G-2 said it "materially assisted in deceiving the enemy." (GALP-Viersen; PL 117-85 finding 21 quotes the same line) **VERIFIED (primary)**
- Lt. Gen. William Simpson's commendation, 29 March 1945, praised "careful planning, minute attention to detail, and diligent execution of the tasks." (Simpson) **VERIFIED (primary document copy)**
- Funny detail: the commendation letter couldn't say what the unit had actually done, because that was secret. (WUNC 2024) **SINGLE-SOURCE**
- **Caveat on effect**: one researcher, cited in a review of Jonathan Gawne's *Ghosts of the ETO*, argues German records "show no sign of the deception," perhaps because German intelligence had "rather collapsed" by 1945. (https://www.historyofwar.org/bookpage/gawne_ghosts_ETO.html) **DISPUTED.** Say "helped" or "appears to have helped," not "won the Rhine crossing."

### Casualties
- **3 killed, dozens wounded**: "3 soldiers ... gave their lives and dozens were injured." (PL 117-85 finding 22) **VERIFIED (primary)**. History.com says "about 30 wounded." The NWWII Museum says one operation that simulated the 80th Infantry Division cost 2 killed and 15 wounded.
- The 3133rd in Italy lost almost 70% of its 35 dummy tanks in 19 days of combat. (NWWII) **SINGLE-SOURCE**

### "Lives saved" figure
- "15,000 to 30,000 American lives saved" is widely repeated (History.com: "military estimates"), but I found no primary document for it. **SINGLE-SOURCE / treat with caution.** The safer wording, from PL 117-85 finding 4, is that it "reduced casualties."
- The official Army analysis quoted in the law: "Rarely, if ever, has there been a group of such a few men which had so great an influence on the outcome of a major military campaign." (PL 117-85 finding 5) **VERIFIED (primary)**. This is the best line in the chapter.

**Inflatables were not a US invention.** The British used dummies earlier (North Africa, Operation Fortitude). The 3133rd worked alongside British inflatable specialists in Italy. (PL 117-85 finding 24; History.com) **VERIFIED**

### The famous "four soldiers lift a tank" story
- **What's documented:** Ghost Army artist **Arthur Shilstone** (603rd, Co. B) told the story and painted it. Two Frenchmen on bicycles got past the perimeter and saw four GIs pick up what looked like a 40-ton Sherman. Shilstone said: **"They looked at me, and they were looking for answers, and I finally said: 'The Americans are very strong.'"** (Smithsonian 2013; GALP roster https://ghostarmy.org/roster/Arthur-Rudolf-Shilstone/; GA.com press kit, where his painting is credited "Courtesy: Arthur Shilstone")
- **Status: SINGLE-SOURCE (one eyewitness, Shilstone).** It is widely repeated, but every version goes back to him. Narrate it as "one artist, Arthur Shilstone, remembered..."
- **Where and when is unclear:** Smithsonian 2013 says shortly after D-Day, in France. Another account says rural Luxembourg. **Don't name a place.** "France" is probably fine if hedged.
- The story first reached the public in *Smithsonian* magazine in April 1985, when Shilstone painted it. (Smithsonian 2013; GALP roster)
- Weight math for the script: 93 lb split among 4 men is about 23 lb each, roughly a heavy backpack.

## 5. Modern-day connections (sourced)

1. **Inflatable decoys are back, in Ukraine.** Czech company **Inflatech** makes more than 30 kinds of inflatable decoys, including **HIMARS** rocket launchers, M1 Abrams tanks and jets. They weigh about 25-90 kg (55-198 lb), take 2-4 people to handle, are inflated by an electric or petrol blower whose engine heat helps fool infrared sensors, and cost $10,000-$100,000. CEO Vojtech Fresser: "If I force the enemy to destroy my product with something that is at least four times as expensive..." and "Of course I would prefer to produce toys for children. But first of all we must ensure a safe world for them." (AFP via The Defense Post, March 6, 2023 https://www.thedefensepost.com/2023/03/06/inflatable-weapon-decoys-ukraine ; AP via ClickOnDetroit https://clickondetroit.com/news/world/2023/03/06/inflatable-tanks-missiles-czech-firm-makes-decoy-armaments) **VERIFIED** (AFP and AP).
   - Inflatech **would not confirm** selling to Ukraine. Fresser's claim that a third of the HIMARS Russia "destroyed" were decoys is his own claim. **SINGLE-SOURCE / unverified.**
   - Note the weight: about the same as the Ghost Army's 93-lb Sherman, 80 years later.
2. **Ukraine's wooden HIMARS decoys.** The Washington Post reported (Aug 2022, citing Ukrainian officials) that wooden HIMARS fakes drew at least 10 Russian Kalibr cruise missiles. A US diplomat said: "They've claimed to have hit more HIMARS than we have even shipped." (Reported secondhand: The Defense Post https://www.thedefensepost.com/2022/08/30/ukraine-himars-decoys-russia/ ; Babel https://babel.ua/en/news/83659-wp-the-ukrainian-military-use-wooden-himars-mockups-to-decoy-russian-missiles) **SINGLE-SOURCE** (all from the WaPo report; I couldn't open the original).
3. **Russia uses them too.** Russian inflatable T-72s (likely from maker Rusbal), and a Ukrainian General Staff claim that Russian decoys near Zaporizhzhia deflated. (Forbes via Euromaidan Press; Business Insider https://www.businessinsider.nl/ukraine-says-russias-putting-inflatable-tanks-on-the-battlefield-but-the-decoys-deflated/) **SINGLE-SOURCE.** Use lightly, if at all.
4. **Today's Army still learns from them.** Retired Brig. Gen. Ed Burley, a former Army psychological operations commander, said the modern PSYOP force uses the Ghost Army's mixed-media approach: "The Ghost Army was unique in that they integrated all these techniques into a process that was highly believable." Veteran George Dramis was photographed with a modern PSYOP unit in 2022. (WUNC 2024) **SINGLE-SOURCE**
5. **Spoof radio and today's fake accounts and deepfakes.** This is **an analogy, not a sourced fact.** Spoof radio faked who was talking on the network (copying operators' Morse "styles" and making up chatter like promotions). Dramis: "Things like so-and-so got another stripe today" (WUNC 2024). That is the same idea as a bot account or a cloned voice. Present it as the narrator's comparison.
6. **The fashion brand.** Bill Blass, a name students may know from department stores, served in the 603rd. His wartime notebook "B" doodles became his logo. (GALP) **VERIFIED** for service; **SINGLE-SOURCE** for the logo.
7. **The photograph.** Art Kane's "A Great Day in Harlem" (1958) is one of the most famous jazz photos ever, and the photographer was a Ghost Army camoufleur. (GALP roster) **VERIFIED**
8. **Hollywood.** A **Ben Affleck** Ghost Army film was announced at Universal in April 2019 (script by Nic Pizzolatto), based on the Beyer & Sayles book. An earlier version was at Warner Bros. with Bradley Cooper (2015). ## 8. Myths to avoid

1. **"The Ghost Army ran the fake army in England before D-Day (FUSAG / Operation Fortitude, Patton's phantom army)."** MYTH / DISPUTED. FUSAG was a separate Allied deception, mostly British-run and done through radio and double agents. The 23rd's operations were almost all **after** D-Day, in France, Luxembourg and Germany (PL 117-85 findings 2, 10-14; Mental Floss https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/30447/fusag-ghost-army-world-war-ii). NWWII says the unit "worked with the British on Operation Fortitude"; keep that vague if you use it at all.
2. **"They fooled Hitler / won the war / the Germans never caught on."** Overstated. Veteran Jack McGlynn said "the Germans never wised up" (WBUR 2012), but the unit often had no feedback on whether the Germans noticed, sometimes they clearly didn't notice, and one researcher found no sign of Viersen in German records (Gawne review). Brest only partly worked. Say "often fooled the enemy" and "helped save lives."
3. **"Saved 15,000-30,000 lives."** No primary source found. Hedge it ("some estimates say") or skip it.
4. **"Four men lifted a tank in front of French farmers," told as a confirmed fact with a place and date.** It comes from one eyewitness (Shilstone), and the location varies. Attribute it to him.
5. **"Firestone made the tanks."** Not supported. Say U.S. Rubber and Goodyear.
6. **"The famous ghost patch was the unit's insignia."** DISPUTED. Wikipedia's caption says it was "not used by the actual unit, but associated with the unit after the war." But the Gold Medal reverse shows "emblems" of the 23rd and 3133rd (US Mint). Avoid claiming the patch was worn in combat.
7. **"Sound carried 50 miles."** No. About 15 miles at most, often much less.
8. **"Every member was an artist."** No. The 603rd was mostly artists, but the unit also had radio men, sound engineers and the 406th combat engineers. (GALP; NWWII)
9. **"3133rd" used for the French sound unit.** The France unit was the 3132nd. The 3133rd was in Italy. (PL 117-85)
10. **"It was all classified until 1996 and nobody knew."** Smithsonian published the story in 1985, and a few articles slipped out after the war (History.com). Say "secret for about 40 years; fully declassified in 1996."
11. **"The Ghost Army was its official name."** It was the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops. "Ghost Army" is the popular name (PL 117-85: "popularly known as the 'Ghost Army'").

## Best story beats for narration (ranked by surprise)

1. **"The Americans are very strong."** Two French cyclists watch four GIs pick up a 30-ton Sherman. It's a 93-pound balloon. (Shilstone's own account; attribute to him.)
2. **The fake army was full of future celebrities.** A fashion legend (Bill Blass, who "read Vogue in his foxhole"), one of America's great abstract painters (Ellsworth Kelly), and the man who took "A Great Day in Harlem" (Art Kane) were all inflating rubber tanks.
3. **They even faked the gossip.** Fake patches, fake generals walking the streets, fake promotions on the radio, and men posing as MPs who "closed" 19 bars in Rennes for a division that wasn't coming.
4. **1,100 men pretending to be 30,000**, and an official Army analysis said "Rarely, if ever, has there been a group of such a few men which had so great an influence on the outcome of a major military campaign."
5. **Sound as a weapon.** 500-pound speakers on half-tracks playing tanks recorded at Fort Knox, including a sergeant yelling at someone to put out a cigarette. One veteran thought he'd "shoot the Germans with sound!"
6. **The thank-you note couldn't say thank you for what.** Simpson's 1945 commendation couldn't describe the mission, the men had to stay quiet for about 40 years, and the Gold Medal came in 2024, when only 7 were still alive.
7. **Same trick, 80 years later.** Ukraine-era inflatable HIMARS from a Czech firm weigh about what the Ghost Army's Sherman did. As the CEO put it, make the enemy waste a missile "at least four times as expensive" on a balloon.
8. **A colonel who missed the briefing**, looking at the fake division at Bettembourg: "Well, son, you certainly could have fooled me."
