## 1. Tatsuo Ogawa (the launch-crew boy, Japan)

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| He was **16** and "helping launch experimental 'balloon bombs' with other youths his age" at **Ichinomiya Beach** (Chiba) in **early spring 1944**. He was **97** in 2025. | SINGLE-SOURCE (oral history) | Nippon.com 2025 |
| After middle school he joined the **Noborito** institute as a **lathe operator**; one day he was told to go to Ichinomiya Beach for a "special assignment." | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 |
| "Six boys would inflate each paper balloon, inspect it for leaks, and release it into the sky." His words: "Our job was to carry the balloons to the beach and inflate them with hydrogen gas." | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 |
| **"We'd get a sweet potato for each hole we found, so that was a great incentive."** | SINGLE-SOURCE (quote) | Nippon.com 2025 |
| The boys "returned to their room laughing, saying, 'There's no way we can win with these things.'" Their older team leader slipped away from a party saying flatly, **"Japan's gonna lose,"** and didn't come back that night. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 |
| "On the morning of August 15, the Noborito Institute received orders to destroy evidence"; Ogawa helped **burn documents and bury the ashes**. He heard the surrender broadcast in a vegetable patch in front of his home, and "even as the adults around him wept, he was not surprised." | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 |
| "After seeing the kind of bombers America had, it was obvious even to a kid that you can't win with balloons." | SINGLE-SOURCE (quote) | Nippon.com 2025 |
| **Careful:** his Ichinomiya work was the **1944 test flights**, before the November 1944 offensive. Ichinomiya was later one of the three real launch sites (Mikesh pp. 16–17), and it was the USGS's second pick for the sand (Mikesh p. 35). Say "the beach where he'd launched them" (true for the test flights), not "he launched the balloon that hit Bly". | | |
| Note: the v1/v2 draft 1 line "You really think we can beat America with this?" is also Ogawa (research 02 §2). Draft 2 uses the Nippon.com 2025 quotes instead. | | |

## 2. Rei Minamimura (the schoolgirl, Japan)

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A graduate of a **Futaba** girls' school who helped make the balloons. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 |
| Girls (unmarried, **12 and up**, under the August 1944 Women's Volunteer Labor Order) were summoned to venues like the **Takarazuka Theater** and **Yūrakuza** in Tokyo, sat on **cushions**, and glued washi with **konnyaku paste tinted blue** into **tatami-sized panels**. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 (blue tint also in Mikesh pp. 13–14: coloring made thin spots visible) |
| **Military police** told the girls it was a top military secret and forbade them to speak of it. Many died without ever telling. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 |
| After the war she married and raised children. **"One day she spotted a photograph in a bookstore window—an image just like the balloons she had helped assemble. Only then did she realize what she had been making. The shock was profound."** | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 |
| She went to the Defense Agency to request records, gathered other women's testimonies, and **self-published her account (2000)**. "It was an act of resistance against being erased for so long." | SINGLE-SOURCE | Nippon.com 2025 |
| **Careful:** nothing links her to the 1987 cranes. The cranes came from other former schoolgirls (Takeshita's friend's wife and her classmates; research 02 §6). Say "women who'd done the same work", never that Rei folded them. When she saw the photo is not given ("years later"); say "decades later". | | |

## 3. Byron Price (the newspaperman who ran the silence, USA)

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A newsman for about **29 years** (United Press 1912, then the **Associated Press**, its Washington bureau chief, executive news editor from 1937). Director of the **Office of Censorship**, 1941–45, its first and only director. | VERIFIED | Wikipedia (citing Sweeney); Hanyok |
| The press code was **voluntary**; he won a special **Pulitzer Prize (1944)** for "creation and administration of the newspaper and radio codes." | VERIFIED | Wikipedia (Pulitzer site) |
| On **4 Jan 1945** the Office of Censorship asked editors and broadcasters to give "no publicity whatsoever" to balloon incidents; it held "from coast to coast, a remarkable self-restraint in a free-press-conscious country." | VERIFIED | Mikesh pp. 26–27 |
| About three months later, a confidential note to editors: "your refusal to publish or broadcast information about these balloons has **baffled the Japanese, annoyed and hindered them**." | SINGLE-SOURCE (primary) | Mikesh p. 27 |
| "The voluntary censorship, ironically, made it difficult to warn the people of the bomb danger. **The risk seemed justified as weeks went by and no casualties were reported.**" | SINGLE-SOURCE (primary) | Mikesh p. 27 |
| Military intelligence, "with initial acquiescence by the Office of Censorship," refused to release information. "**Price subsequently asked for at least a partial release of information on the potential danger of the balloon bombs; however, the Navy refused.** The news clampdown may have contributed to some of the (few) casualties." | SINGLE-SOURCE (a CIA review summarizing Sweeney) | Hanyok |
| **DATE UNKNOWN.** Neither source says whether Price's request came before or after Bly. Draft 2 says it without a date and places it before the turn to Bly. **Check Sweeney's chapter on the balloons before voicing.** If the request came after 5 May, move the line to ch 9 ("Price had argued for a warning; it came seventeen days too late" must then be cut too). | | |
| "At 5:28 p.m. on 15 August 1945, Price canceled the censorship code and placed an **'Out of Business'** sign on his office door." | SINGLE-SOURCE | Hanyok |
| Note the calendar: Ogawa's document burning was the morning of 15 Aug in Japan; Price's sign was 5:28 p.m. 15 Aug in Washington, about a day later in real time. "On the same date" is true; "at the same moment" is not. | | |

## 4. Bly (the Mitchells and the Patzkes)

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Elsie (Elsye) Mitchell, 26**, pregnant, had baked a **chocolate cake the night before** and **almost stayed home** because she hadn't been feeling well. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Smithsonian 2019 |
| Archie was moving the car, about **40 feet** away; he shouted a warning "for he had **heard rumors** of Japanese balloons." | SINGLE-SOURCE (primary) | Mikesh p. 67 |
| Archie: "I…hurriedly called a warning to them, but it was too late. Just then there was a big explosion. I ran up – and they were all lying there dead." | VERIFIED | Smithsonian 2019; FHS |
| Dick (14) and Joan "Sis" (13) **Patzke** were brother and sister. Their sister **Betty** was the eldest of **ten** children; another brother died fighting in the war. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Smithsonian 2019 |
| Their sister **Dottie McGinnis** came home to cars in the driveway: **"I ran to one of the cars and asked is Dick dead? Or Joan dead?"** | SINGLE-SOURCE (quote) | Smithsonian 2019 |
| For decades, families were sometimes not believed: "They would be telling someone about the loss of their sibling and that person just didn't believe them" (filmmaker Ilana Sol). | SINGLE-SOURCE | Smithsonian 2019 |
| Germany's surrender (8 May) overshadowed the news of the deaths. | SINGLE-SOURCE | Smithsonian 2019 |
| In **1949** Congress paid **$20,000** to the families; supporters said "the public had not been warned of the danger… although the armed services knew that several had reached this country." | SINGLE-SOURCE (primary) | Mikesh p. 68 |
| Takeshita learned in the late 1980s that **the wife of a childhood friend** had built the bombs as a girl. | VERIFIED | Smithsonian 2019; research 02 §6 |
