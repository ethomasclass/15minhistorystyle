# Reconstruction: a three-part series

**15 Minute History** · series plan · standard **8.1: Identify legal, political, and social dimensions of Reconstruction**
(Presidential and Congressional plans, Freedmen's Bureau, tenant farming/sharecropping, 13th–15th Amendments, Black Codes, KKK,
military districts).

Source notes: [`reference/`](reference/) (Lesson 1 Political Reconstruction, 10.2 Realities of Reconstruction, 10.3 End of
Reconstruction).

---

## The throughline

**Series question:** What does it take to make freedom real?

**Answer, built across three episodes:** freedom needs three things: **the law, the land, and someone willing to protect it.**
Reconstruction gave freedpeople the first, never delivered the second, and then took away the third.

**The thread in the freedpeople's own words.** In October 1865 a committee of freedmen on Edisto Island, South Carolina, summed up
their situation in three words: *"Landless, Homeless. Voteless."* The series follows those words: Episode 1 shows how they got
there, Episode 2 crosses out *Voteless* and shows what *Landless* still costs, and Episode 3 shows the vote taken back.

**Recurring motif card** (like the BANNED ledger and the Swatch): either **LAW / LAND / PROTECTION** or the petition's three words.
It fills, gets crossed out, and gets stamped across the series. *(Your pick; see the Episode 1 outline.)*

## The story checklist (from the story-arc research)

Run it on every episode outline and draft.

1. **Who wants what, and what's in the way?** One sentence. A person or group at ground level, not only presidents and Congress.
   Stories are remembered better than explanations because each event hangs on a cause (Willingham's four Cs: causality, conflict,
   complications, character).
2. **But / therefore.** Every beat links to the next with *but* or *therefore*, never *and then*. A list beat gets rewritten or cut.
   The causes students need are said out loud with the linking word ("The Black Codes are *why* Congress wrote the 14th Amendment").
3. **The hook carries the standard.** The opening story is the lesson's content, not decoration.
4. **Shape.** Mark where the episode rises and falls. The three episodes use three different shapes.
5. **Loops.** Every chapter ends on a small question the next one answers; close one loop as you open the next. A gap works only if
   viewers know enough to care (give a 15–20 s recap in Ep 2 and 3) and the gap isn't too big.
6. **Every required term rides on a story beat**, defined where it appears.
7. **Segments.** One idea per chapter, 1–2 minutes each; the logo breaks are pause points for class.

### Script rules: written for people who don't know history (and people who do)

From the scriptwriting research. These sit on top of the skill's `explainer-craft.md` checklist.

1. **Keep a "what they don't know" list for every episode.** The "curse of knowledge": once you know something, you assume
   everyone does, and trying harder to imagine the reader doesn't fix it. A written list does. Before drafting, list every name,
   term and event a viewer would need (Union, Confederacy, veto, amendment, ratify, pardon, Dred Scott…). Each gets a plain
   explanation the first time it's said. The list goes in SCRIPT.md. (Pinker)
2. **Define in passing, not in a lecture.** One clause, right where the word appears: "a pardon: an official 'you're
   forgiven.'" History viewers barely notice it; new viewers need it.
3. **Write for the ear.** Listeners can't rewind. One idea per sentence; most sentences about 8–12 words; fragments are fine;
   lead with the point; read every chapter aloud and rewrite anywhere you stumble or run out of breath.
4. **And, But, Therefore at every scale.** Each paragraph sets something up (*and*), turns it (*but*), and lands a consequence
   (*therefore*). If two paragraphs only join with "and then," one of them is a list. (Olson's ABT, from the South Park rule)
5. **Move up and down the "ladder of abstraction."** Alternate scene (one person, one place, one moment: Frazier in Savannah,
   Howard on Edisto) with summary (the rule, the law, the numbers), and always come back down to a person. This is how long-form
   explanatory narratives work. (Hart, *Storycraft*)
6. **Narration says what the picture can't.** Don't describe what's on screen; point at it ("Look at the handwriting") and add the
   meaning.
7. **Never "today's party."** Every time Republican or Democrat comes up for the first time, say it was a very different party.

## Corrections to the source notes (before scripting)

- The Reconstruction Act of 1867 made **5** military districts, not 7, and they were Congress's doing, not Lincoln's.
- Reconstruction sent **2** Black senators (Revels, Bruce) and about **14–16** representatives to Congress, not 7 in total.
- The Radicals were a faction, not a majority of Congress. The 1866 election gave Republicans the votes to override vetoes.
- The 1867 Act barred the Confederacy's leaders from voting and office, not every ex-Confederate.
- Charles Caldwell: most accounts say he was lured into a store for a Christmas drink, not dinner at a friend's home. Check details.
- "60,000 kept from voting in Mississippi, 1875" needs a source or a hedge.
- Hayes pulled troops from the Southern statehouses, not from the whole South. "Secret deal" needs a hedge ("no proof of a written deal").
