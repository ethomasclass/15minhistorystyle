In February 1870, Mississippi was let back into the Union. Two days later, Hiram Revels was sworn in as a U.S. senator. He had been born free in North Carolina. He was a minister and a teacher, and during the war he served as a chaplain for Black Union soldiers.

He wasn't alone. During Reconstruction, about 2,000 Black men held public office. Sheriffs. Mayors. Judges. State lawmakers, like Charles Caldwell. Sixteen served in Congress, including two senators, both from Mississippi.

There's a phrase from the time for this. Freedpeople said the {bottom rail} was on top. Picture a rail fence. The bottom rail was always the one in the mud. Now it was on top. You'll find the phrase in their mouths, and in the cartoons of people who hated the idea.

So what did these new governments do?

They built the first public school systems in the South. They built hospitals and roads, and rebuilt railroads wrecked by the war. They got rid of the Black Codes.

They also raised taxes. And some officials were corrupt. Critics, then and later, made that the whole story.

But corruption wasn't a Southern thing in the 1870s. It was everywhere. In New York City, a political boss named William Tweed stole tens of millions of dollars. Nobody said New Yorkers weren't fit to govern themselves.
