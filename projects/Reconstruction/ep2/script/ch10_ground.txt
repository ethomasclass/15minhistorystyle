So let's step back, look at the wall, and go back to the question. Five years after slavery ended, a Black minister from Mississippi was in the U.S. Senate. How did it change so fast?

Because Congress forced it. The army held the South while new state governments were written. Black men got the vote, and they used it, sending about 2,000 of their own to office. And the 15th Amendment wrote the vote into the Constitution.

To Republicans, this was finally a South rebuilt on equal citizenship. To most white Southerners, it was a world turned upside down by outsiders and soldiers. Bottom rail on top. They meant it as a warning.

And what was all of it resting on? Two things. Votes, and the soldiers who protected them.

Not land.

Look at the card from Edisto. *Voteless* is crossed out. *Landless* is still there. Freedpeople could vote. They could hold office. But most of them still didn't own the ground they stood on. And people who don't own the ground can be pushed off it.

As for the soldiers, they were only there as long as the North wanted them there.

Charles Caldwell was still in the state senate. In 1873, the governor made him a colonel in the state militia, the state's own part-time army. Two years later, that's what would get him killed.
