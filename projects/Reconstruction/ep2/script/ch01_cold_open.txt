In 1861, Jefferson Davis walked out of the United States Senate to lead the Confederacy. Nine years later, his home state sent a new senator to Washington. A Black minister named Hiram Revels.

This is a print made to celebrate him and six Black congressmen. Look at the man seated on the left. The first Black senator in American history.

But before he could sit down, some senators tried to stop him. The Constitution says a senator has to have been a citizen for nine years. And until 1866, they argued, no Black man had been a citizen at all. Remember the Dred Scott decision? They were still using it.

It didn't work. The Senate voted 48 to 8, and Revels took the oath.

Five years earlier, not one Black man in Mississippi could vote. So how did that change so fast? And what was all of it resting on?

We'll find out. And along the way, we'll meet a blacksmith from a small town in Mississippi. Hold onto him.
