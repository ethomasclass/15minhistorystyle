That fall, 1868, the country elected a new president: Ulysses S. Grant, the general who had won the war. He was a Republican. And in the South, Black voters helped put him there.

Republicans noticed. The vote was what made everything else possible. So they wanted to make it permanent.

In 1869, Congress sent the states the {15th Amendment}. It says the right to vote can't be denied "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude."

Translation: you can't be kept from voting because you're Black, or because you used to be enslaved.

It was ratified in 1870. And on that card from Edisto, one word gets crossed out. *Voteless.*

But read it closely. It says what you can't use to stop someone voting. Race. Color. Having been enslaved. It doesn't say anything about a tax to vote, or a reading test, or a rule that excuses anyone whose grandfather could vote. Hold onto that. It's going to matter.

And it says nothing about women, Black or white. Women who had fought slavery alongside the abolitionists were left out, and the fight over it split the women's movement in two.

Still. Five years after the war, freedmen could vote across the whole country. In parts of the South, they were most of the voters.
