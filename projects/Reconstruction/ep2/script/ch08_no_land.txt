Because there was one thing Reconstruction never delivered. The land.

Remember Garrison Frazier in Savannah? "The way we can best take care of ourselves is to have land." Remember Edisto? Thaddeus Stevens's plan died with him. Most freedpeople were still farming land owned by someone else.

And everybody had a problem. Planters had land but no money to pay workers. Freedpeople had their labor, but no land and no money. The Freedmen's Bureau pushed them to sign work contracts with planters, sometimes the same men who had owned them. Those often fell apart.

So they landed on a deal. It's called {sharecropping}.

Here's how it worked, with one family. A planter divides his land into small farms. A family moves onto one, maybe 20 or 30 acres, with a cabin. The planter provides the land, and maybe a mule and tools. The family provides the work. At harvest, they split the crop. Often half and half.

There was a step up from that, called {tenant farming}. A tenant rented the land, paid in cash or a fixed amount of crop, and usually owned their own tools and mule. More freedom. More risk.

At first, sharecropping looked like progress. No overseer. No whip. A family working together, on its own schedule, growing a crop that was partly theirs.

But that word, partly, is where the trouble started.
