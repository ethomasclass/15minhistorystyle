April 1865. The main Confederate army surrenders. The war is all but over.

Two days later, a crowd gathers outside the White House, and Lincoln speaks to them from a window. Near the end, he says something no president had said in public before. Some Black men, he says, should be able to vote. Men who were "very intelligent," and men who had served as Union soldiers.

It was a small step. But it was the first one.

Standing in that crowd, according to later accounts, was an actor named John Wilkes Booth. A Confederate supporter. And he was furious.

Booth and a few friends had been plotting for months. Now the plan became murder. The President, the vice president and the Secretary of State, all on the same night.

April 14. Lincoln and his wife go to see a comedy at Ford's Theatre in Washington. A little after 10, Booth slips into the President's box and fires one shot into the back of his head.

Then he jumps down to the stage, breaking his leg, shouts "Sic semper tyrannis," Latin for "thus always to tyrants," and escapes out the back.

Lincoln dies early the next morning.

Across town, the Secretary of State, William Seward, is stabbed in his bed. He survives. The man sent for the vice president, Andrew Johnson, never goes through with it.

So by morning, the man who was supposed to die that night was the President of the United States.

And in Washington, people mostly knew him for one thing. Six weeks earlier, at his swearing-in as vice president, Johnson had been drunk. He'd been sick, taking whiskey for it, and he gave a rambling speech in front of the whole Senate. Lincoln reportedly told worried friends, "Andy ain't a drunkard."

Now Andy was in charge. And he wasn't even a Republican. He was a Democrat from Tennessee, put on the ticket to show the country could come together. Same name as today's Democrats, but a very different party.
