By the summer of 1866, the fight had a deadline. That fall, the country would vote for a new Congress.

And in the South, the violence was getting worse. In May, white mobs, with police joining in, attacked Black neighborhoods in Memphis, Tennessee. In three days, 46 Black people were killed. In July, in New Orleans, a mob attacked a meeting of Black and white Republicans. Dozens more were killed.

Northern newspapers covered both. Northern voters read them.

Johnson's answer was to hit the road. Presidents back then almost never campaigned in person. Johnson went on a speaking tour across the North, nicknamed the {Swing Around the Circle}.

It did not go well. Crowds heckled him. He heckled them back. In Cleveland, someone shouted that they should hang Jefferson Davis, the Confederacy's president. Johnson shot back: why not hang Thad Stevens?

Thaddeus Stevens. The Radical with the land plan. The President of the United States was asking a crowd why nobody had hanged a congressman.

In November, voters answered. Republicans won more than two-thirds of both the House and the Senate. Enough to override anything Johnson vetoed.

So, back to the question. Who decides? From now on, Congress does.

And Congress's plan included sending the army back into the South.
