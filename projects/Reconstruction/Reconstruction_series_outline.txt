RECONSTRUCTION: A THREE-PART SERIES (15 Minute History)
Outline of essential content and curriculum ideas
=====================================================================

Standard 8.1: Identify legal, political, and social dimensions of Reconstruction
(Presidential and Congressional plans, Freedmen's Bureau, tenant farming/sharecropping,
13th-15th Amendments, Black Codes, KKK, military districts).

Class notes it draws on: Lesson 1 "Political Reconstruction", 10.2 "Realities of
Reconstruction", 10.3 "The End of Reconstruction".

SERIES QUESTION: What does it take to make freedom real?
SERIES ANSWER:   Three things: the LAW, the LAND, and someone willing to PROTECT both.
                 Reconstruction delivered the law, never the land, and protection
                 only while the North was willing.

RUNNING THREAD:  The freedmen of Edisto Island, S.C. described themselves in 1865 in
                 three words: "Landless, Homeless, Voteless."
                 Ep 1 shows how they got there; Ep 2 crosses out VOTELESS;
                 Ep 3 writes it back in.


=====================================================================
EPISODE 1: LANDLESS, HOMELESS, VOTELESS  (1865-1866)  about 16 min
=====================================================================
Driving question: The war was over. Slavery was over. Who got to decide what
freedom would look like? And why was the land going back?
Big idea: the LAW. The President decided first; Congress took over by the end of 1866.

 1. A Few Acres (cold open)
    - Edisto Island freedmen write to President Johnson, Oct 1865:
      "We wish to have A home if It be but A few acres." Johnson had already said no.
    - Their three words: Landless, Homeless, Voteless.

 2. Forty Acres
    Terms: Union, Confederacy, Special Field Order No. 15, "forty acres and a mule"
    - Sherman and 20 Black ministers in Savannah, Jan 1865 (Garrison Frazier: "have land").
    - Field Order 15 sets aside a coastal strip for freed families; up to 40 acres each.
    - Curriculum (10.2): "40 Acres and a Mule" as the symbol of freedpeople's hopes.

 3. Ten Percent
    Terms: Reconstruction, veto, pardon, Ten Percent Plan, Republican,
           Radical Republicans, freedpeople, Wade-Davis Bill
    - Lincoln's plan (1863): 10% loyalty oath, accept the end of slavery, pardons.
    - Radical Republicans think it's too weak; Wade-Davis Bill (a majority must swear loyalty); Lincoln
      blocks it with a pocket veto.
    - Curriculum (Lesson 1): Lincoln's Plan; Radical complaints; Wade-Davis.

 4. Ford's Theatre
    - Lincoln's last speech hints at votes for some Black men; Booth is in the crowd.
    - Assassination, April 14, 1865; Andrew Johnson becomes president.

 5. Treason Must Be Made Odious
    Terms: planters, Presidential Reconstruction, homestead
    - Johnson: poor, anti-planter, but against equal rights for Black Americans.
    - Presidential Reconstruction: even more lenient; thousands of personal pardons;
      land returned to former owners (Edisto loses its land).
    - Curriculum (Lesson 1): Johnson's Plan, "far more lenient than Lincoln."

 6. Except as a Punishment for Crime
    Terms: 13th Amendment, Black Codes, vagrant
    - 13th Amendment (Dec 1865) ends slavery, "except as a punishment for crime."
    - Southern states elect ex-Confederates and pass Black Codes (Mississippi first):
      labor contracts, vagrancy arrests, convicts hired out.
    - Curriculum (Lesson 1, 10.2): Failures of Johnson's plan; Black Codes; vagrancy laws.

 7. The Bureau
    Terms: Freedmen's Bureau, Civil Rights Act of 1866, override
    - Freedmen's Bureau: food, medicine, schools, legal marriages, reuniting families
      (Hannah Barnett's 1866 newspaper ad looking for her daughter).
    - Johnson vetoes the Bureau bill and the Civil Rights Act; Congress overrides.
    - Curriculum (Lesson 1, 10.2): Freedmen's Bureau; "Experiencing Freedom"
      (marriages, family ads).

 8. Equal Protection
    Terms: 14th Amendment, Dred Scott decision, due process, equal protection
    - 14th Amendment (1866): birthright citizenship (overturns Dred Scott), due process,
      equal protection. Aimed squarely at the Black Codes.
    - Curriculum (Lesson 1): 14th Amendment as a rebuttal of Dred Scott.

 9. Swing Around the Circle
    Terms: Swing Around the Circle
    - Memphis and New Orleans riots (1866) shock Northern voters.
    - Johnson's speaking tour goes badly ("why not hang Thad Stevens?").
    - 1866 elections give Republicans a veto-proof Congress.
    - Curriculum (10.2): Swing Around the Circle.

10. Landless, Homeless, Voteless (ending)
    - Answer: the President decided first, then Congress took control.
    - Freedpeople now have the LAW on their side, but not the LAND.
      Most Edisto families lost their land.

EPISODE 2: BOTTOM RAIL ON TOP  (1867-1873)
=====================================================================
Driving question: Five years after slavery ended, a Black minister from Mississippi
was in the U.S. Senate. How did that change so fast? And what was it resting on?
Big idea: the VOTE (and the LAND that never came).

 1. The First Black Senator (cold open)
    - Jefferson Davis leaves the Senate in 1861; Hiram Revels is sworn in for
      Mississippi in 1870 (vote 48-8), despite a Dred Scott-based objection.
    - Introduces Charles Caldwell, a blacksmith from Clinton, Mississippi.

 2. The Army Goes Back In
    Terms: Reconstruction Act, military districts, Congressional Reconstruction
    - Reconstruction Act of 1867, passed over Johnson's veto: 10 states, 5 military
      districts (not 7, as the class notes say), new constitutions,
      Black men vote, 14th Amendment required.
    - Curriculum (10.2): Congressional Reconstruction vs. Lincoln's and Johnson's plans.

 3. The Blacksmith
    - Hundreds of thousands of Black men register; churches, rallies, night clubs.
    - Caldwell, born enslaved, helps write Mississippi's 1868 constitution.
    - New state constitutions: Black voting and office-holding; first public schools.
    - Curriculum (10.2): school and church as pillars of Black life.

 4. One Vote Short
    Terms: Tenure of Office Act, impeach
    - Johnson fires Stanton in violation of the Tenure of Office Act; the House
      impeaches him; the Senate falls one vote short of removal (35-19).
    - Curriculum (10.2): Johnson impeached.

 5. The 15th Amendment
    Terms: 15th Amendment
    - Grant elected in 1868 with Black votes in the South.
    - 15th Amendment (1870): the vote can't be denied by race, color or previous
      servitude. VOTELESS is crossed out.
    - Its gaps: says nothing about poll taxes, reading tests, grandfather rules
      (they pay off in Ep 3). Women left out.
    - Curriculum (10.2, 10.3): 15th Amendment and its loopholes.

 6. Bottom Rail on Top
    Terms: bottom rail
    - Revels sworn in; about 2,000 Black officeholders; 16 in Congress, 2 senators
      (corrects the class notes' "7").
    - What the new governments did: public schools, hospitals, roads, railroads;
      the Black Codes repealed; higher taxes, some corruption (as everywhere then).
    - Curriculum (10.2): African-Americans in government.

 7. Carpetbaggers and Scalawags
    Terms: carpetbaggers, scalawags
    - Who they really were vs. the insults; Adelbert Ames (from Maine) wins the
      Senate seat Jefferson Davis once held.
    - Curriculum (10.2): white Republicans in the South.

 8. No Land
    Terms: sharecropping, tenant farming
    - The land never came (Frazier, Edisto, Stevens's plan dies with him).
    - The Bureau pushes labor contracts; sharecropping explained with one family.
    - Curriculum (10.2): new working conditions; sharecropping.

 9. The Debt  (heavy chapter)
    Terms: crop lien, convict leasing
    - Crop liens, store credit and interest; "settling up" ends in debt; leaving
      in debt can mean arrest.
    - Circular diagram: the debt cycle keeps families poorer every year.
    - Convict leasing completes the loop from the 13th Amendment's "except" clause.
    - Curriculum (10.2): sharecropping debt; vagrancy laws; prisoners leased as labor.

10. The Ground They Stood On (ending)
    - Answer: Congress forced it, with the army and the vote.
    - But it rested on votes and soldiers, not land. VOTELESS is crossed out,
      LANDLESS is still there.

EPISODE 3: REDEMPTION  (1866-1877)
=====================================================================
Driving question: Five years after he was elected, a Black state senator was shot in
the street and nobody stopped it. How does a country go from that to this?
And why did nobody stop it?
Big idea: PROTECTION, and what happened when it was withdrawn.

 1. A Christmas Drink (cold open, heavy)
    - Charles Caldwell is killed in Clinton, Mississippi, Dec 30, 1875; told through
      his widow Margaret Ann Caldwell's sworn testimony to the U.S. Senate.
    - Correction to the class notes (10.3): he was talked into a Christmas drink in a
      store's cellar, not at dinner in a friend's home on Christmas Day.

 2. The Night
    Terms: Ku Klux Klan, terrorism
    - Recap of Eps 1-2: it all rested on votes and the soldiers who protected them.
    - KKK founded 1866 in Pulaski, Tennessee; night raids against Black voters,
      officeholders, teachers, churches and white Republicans.
    - "Reclaim the night" (from slave patrols to Black church and political meetings).
    - Curriculum (10.3): the KKK; "reclaim the night"; domestic terror.

 3. Protection
    Terms: Enforcement Acts, habeas corpus
    - Enforcement Acts / Ku Klux Klan Act (1870-71); Grant suspends habeas corpus in
      nine South Carolina counties (1871); hundreds arrested; the Klan largely broken.
    - Key idea: when the federal government used real force, it worked.

 4. Colfax  (heavy chapter)
    Terms: Colfax Massacre
    - Easter 1873, Louisiana: at least 60, possibly 150+ Black men killed.
    - U.S. v. Cruikshank (1876): the 14th Amendment limits states, not private mobs,
      so punishing them was left to Louisiana. Nobody was ever punished.

 5. Tired
    Terms: Panic of 1873, Whiskey Ring, Civil Rights Act of 1875
    - Depression after the Panic of 1873; scandals (the Whiskey Ring; Grant's
      private secretary charged).
    - Reformers turn from Black rights to "clean government"; Democrats win the
      House in 1874.
    - Civil Rights Act of 1875: barely enforced, struck down in 1883.
    - Curriculum (10.3): Grant's presidency; Northern disillusionment.

 6. The Bank
    Terms: Freedman's Savings Bank
    - Created 1865; tens of thousands of depositors; risky loans; Frederick Douglass
      made president in 1874; the bank fails.
    - 60,000+ depositors owed almost $3 million; only about half ever repaid.
    - Curriculum (10.2): the Freedman's Bank and the 1873 depression.

 7. Mississippi, 1875  (heavy chapter)
    Terms: Mississippi Plan, Redeemers, Redemption
    - Armed Democratic clubs; the Clinton rally violence (30-50 killed).
    - Governor Ames asks for troops; the reply quotes Grant: the public is
      "tired out with these annual autumnal outbreaks."
    - Caldwell leads militia, Ames disbands it, Democrats sweep, Caldwell is killed
      "for obeying Governor Ames."
    - The Redeemers and "Redemption."
    - Curriculum (10.3): "Redemption"; Grant refuses troops. (The notes' "60,000 kept
      from voting" figure isn't used: no source found.)

 8. Two Sets of Results
    Terms: Compromise of 1877
    - Hayes vs. Tilden, 1876: Tilden 184 electoral votes, Hayes 165, 20 disputed.
    - A 15-man commission votes 8-7 and gives them all to Hayes.
    - Compromise of 1877: no proof of a written deal (the notes say "secret deal").
    - Troops leave the South Carolina and Louisiana statehouses (April 1877);
      Reconstruction ends.
    - Curriculum (10.3): Election of 1876.

 9. Voteless
    Terms: poll tax, literacy test, grandfather clause, Jim Crow
    - Over 25 years, the 15th Amendment's gaps are used to take the vote back.
    - Louisiana: about 130,000 Black voters registered (1896), about 1,300 (1904).
    - Jim Crow segregation laws. VOTELESS is written back in.
    - Curriculum (10.3): disenfranchisement through intimidation, literacy tests,
      taxes.

10. A Few Acres (series ending)
    - The answer: the LAW (13th, 14th, 15th) was delivered; the LAND never was;
      PROTECTION lasted only while the North was willing.
    - Was Reconstruction a failure? Two answers: landless and voteless again by 1900,
      but schools, churches, reunited families, Black leaders and three amendments
      lasted (a "second founding").
    - The amendments wait on paper until Brown v. Board (1954) and the
      Voting Rights Act (1965).
    - Ends on the Edisto petition: "A home if It be but A few acres."
