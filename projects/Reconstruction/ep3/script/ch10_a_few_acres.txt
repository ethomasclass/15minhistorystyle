So step all the way back and look at the whole wall. Three episodes. And the question from the very start: what does it take to make freedom real?

Here's what Reconstruction suggests. Three things. The law. The land. And someone willing to protect both.

Reconstruction delivered the law. The 13th Amendment ended slavery. The 14th made freedpeople citizens, with equal protection of the laws. The 15th protected the vote.

It never delivered the land. Most of Edisto Island went back to its old owners. Stevens's plan died with him. Most freedpeople ended up sharecropping on someone else's ground.

And it delivered protection only while the North was willing. When the army enforced the law, the Klan broke. When the army left, the law stayed on paper.

Was Reconstruction a failure? People have argued about that ever since.

One answer: yes. By 1900, most Black Southerners were landless again, and voteless again.

Another answer: it built things that lasted. Schools. Churches. Families that found each other. A generation of Black leaders. And three amendments that never went away. Some historians call them a second founding.

In 1954, the Supreme Court used the 14th Amendment's equal protection to end segregated schools. In 1965, Congress finally put real force behind the 15th. The words written down in the 1860s were still there, waiting.

Remember those three men on Edisto Island? They asked their President for "A home if It be but A few acres." They didn't get it.

But their country did write something down that decade, about citizens, about equal protection, about the vote. It would take almost a hundred years for the country to start living up to it.
