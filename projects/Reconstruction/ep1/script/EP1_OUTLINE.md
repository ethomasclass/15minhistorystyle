## The hook: the Edisto Island petition

**Cold-open artifact:** the handwritten petition from the freedmen's committee of Edisto Island, South Carolina, to President
Andrew Johnson, **October 28, 1865**. It was written by Henry Bram and signed by Bram, Ishmael Moultrie and Yates Sampson, each in his
own hand. A week earlier, their letter to the head of the Freedmen's Bureau summed up their situation in three words: *"Landless,
Homeless. Voteless."*

**Why this works:**
- **It's the lesson in one document.** The land, the President's plan, the Freedmen's Bureau and the question of citizenship are all
  in it. The hook is the content, not decoration.
- **It has a protagonist with a goal and an obstacle.** The freedpeople want to keep the land they were given in January. The
  President is taking it back. Everything else in the episode is the fight over who decides.
- **It's a mystery with an answer.** Viewers know enough to care (the war is over, slavery is ending) but not how it ends. The
  episode closes the loop in chapter 10: most of them lost the land.
- **It starts the series.** The three words become the series' thread. Episode 2 crosses out *Voteless*. Episode 3 writes it back in.

**First 30 seconds: three versions to choose from** (the first sentence is the hook, never a date):

> **A.** In the fall of 1865, a group of freed people on an island in South Carolina wrote a letter to the President of the United
> States. They weren't asking for money. They were asking him not to take back their land.

> **B.** Three men on an island in South Carolina wrote to the President. Every line came down to one request: *"we wish to have A
> home if It be but A few acres."* The President had already said no.

> **C.** *"Landless, Homeless. Voteless."* That's how a committee of freedmen in South Carolina described themselves in the fall of
> 1865. Months after the end of slavery. Months after the government gave them land.

*Recommendation:* **A** for the first line (clearest for a general audience), then show the petition and read the "few acres"
line, then end the cold open on C's three words.

**Driving question (said out loud at the end of the cold open):**
> The Union had won. Slavery was ending. So who got to decide what freedom would actually look like? And why was the land going back?

**Promise:** "We'll come back to Edisto. And to those three words."

**Answer (chapter 10 says it):** At first the President decided. Lincoln wanted a quick, generous peace. Johnson made it even more
generous to the men who had led the Confederacy: he pardoned them and gave back their land. The South used that freedom to pass the
Black Codes. That pushed Congress to fight back with the Civil Rights Act and the 14th Amendment, and by the end of 1866, voters had
handed Congress the power. Freedom now had the **law** behind it. It still didn't have the **land**.

## The story spine

**Who wants what, and what's in the way:** The freedpeople of Edisto Island want to keep their land and be treated as citizens.
The President in Washington is giving the South back to the men who owned them.

**Shape: man in a hole** (down, then up, with the ending still short of where it started).

```
  ch2 land given ▲                                          ch9 Congress wins ▲
                  \                                        /
     ch3 stalemate \                         ch7 override /
                    \  ch4 Lincoln shot     ch8 14th Am. /
                     \ ch5 pardons, land back           /
                      \___ ch6 Black Codes (bottom) ___/        ch10: law ✓, land ✗
```

**The chain, said with but/therefore:**
Sherman gives freedpeople land, **but** nobody in Washington has decided what Reconstruction means. Lincoln wants a generous plan,
**but** the Radicals want a harsh one, **therefore** they deadlock. Lincoln finally says some Black men should vote, **but** three days
later he's shot. **Therefore** Andrew Johnson decides, **but** he pardons the planters and gives their land back. **Therefore** the
freedpeople on Edisto lose their farms, and the old leaders take over the South again. The 13th Amendment ends slavery, **but** it
leaves one exception, **therefore** the Black Codes can arrest freedpeople for having no job. The Freedmen's Bureau is the one thing
helping them, **but** Johnson vetoes it, **therefore** Congress overrides him and writes the 14th Amendment. Johnson tells the South to
reject it, **but** his tour backfires, **therefore** voters hand Congress control.

## Chapter by chapter

### 1 · A Few Acres (cold open, ~170 words)

- **Frame 0:** the petition on the desk, camera already moving. Hook line (version A).
- Point at the page: "This is their letter. Look at this line." Read *"we wish to have A home if It be but A few acres."* Translation
  is barely needed. Then *"Shall not we who Are freedman and have been always true to this Union have the same rights as are enjoyed by
  Others?"*
- Who they are, in two sentences: formerly enslaved, farming land the U.S. Army gave them in January. By October, the government was
  taking it back.
- Their three words: *"Landless, Homeless. Voteless."* The words go on the motif card.
- **Driving question** and **promise** (above).
- **Loop opened:** what happens to the land? (closed in ch10)

→ Channel intro, title card: **A Few Acres** · *Reconstruction, Part 1* · 1865 – 1866.

### 2 · Forty Acres (~200 words) ▲

- Start in the action, not with backstory: January 1865, Sherman's army reaches the Atlantic coast with thousands of freedpeople
  following it.
- **Special Field Order No. 15:** a strip of coastal land from South Carolina to Florida set aside for freed families, up to 40 acres
  each. Some got Army mules too. That's where **"forty acres and a mule"** comes from. By summer, about 40,000 people were farming it.
- The catch: the order gave them the right to *use* the land, not a clear title. Nobody in Washington had decided whether it was
  permanent.
- **Define Reconstruction** here: rebuilding the South, deciding how the Confederate states come back into the Union, and deciding what
  freedom would mean for about 4 million people.
- **But:** the big question is who decides. The President? Or Congress? The Constitution doesn't say.
- **Loop:** who decides? (ch3 opens the fight)

### 3 · Ten Percent (~260 words) ▼

- **Lincoln's plan** (1863): when **10%** of a state's 1860 voters swear loyalty, the state can form a new government. It must
  accept the end of slavery. Most Confederates get a pardon; top leaders don't. *Why so generous?* Lincoln wanted the war over fast and
  white Southerners won back to the Union.
- **Radical Republicans** (defined: the wing of Lincoln's own party that wanted to remake the South and protect freedpeople's rights)
  thought that was far too soft. Their answer, the **Wade-Davis Bill**: a *majority* has to swear, and swear they never supported the
  Confederacy. Lincoln lets it die without signing it (a pocket veto).
- **Character introduction: Thaddeus Stevens**, Radical leader in the House. He wants to break up the big plantations and give the
  land to freedpeople. *"Hold onto that idea. It's the closest anyone in Washington came to the Edisto petition."*
- Both views, side by side: to Lincoln, generosity would end the war faster; to the Radicals, it let the men who started the war off
  the hook.
- **Therefore:** deadlock. Neither side had won when the war ended.
- One dry aside allowed (e.g., on the pocket veto: "Which is the presidential version of leaving someone on read." Only if it lands;
  cut if it doesn't).

### 4 · The Last Speech (~170 words) ▼

- April 11, 1865. Two days after Lee surrenders, Lincoln speaks from a White House window about Louisiana's new government. For the
  first time in public, he says some Black men, the "very intelligent" and those who served as soldiers, should be able to vote.
- In the crowd, **reportedly**, was John Wilkes Booth, who was furious about it. (Hedge it: the words he's said to have used come from
  later accounts.)
- Three days later, Lincoln is shot.
- **Therefore:** whatever Lincoln's plan would have become, it died with him. The new president is a Southern Democrat from
  Tennessee. "Same name as today's Democrats, but a very different party."
- Tone: plain, no jokes, a short beat of silence.

### 5 · Treason Must Be Made Odious (~280 words) ▼

- **Character:** Andrew Johnson. He grew up poor in Tennessee, was a tailor, and hated the rich planter class. He was the only senator from a
  seceded state who stayed loyal to the Union. In April 1865 he says treason "must be made odious" and traitors punished. The
  Radicals think he's their guy.
- **But:** **Johnson's plan** (summer 1865): pardons for most white Southerners who take a loyalty oath. The richest and the
  Confederate leaders have to ask him personally. *And they do, by the thousands, and he signs.* (Dry aside: the planters he hated
  were now lining up outside his office to say please.)
- A pardon meant you got your property back. That included land like Edisto's. **Callback:** in October, Johnson sends the head of the
  Freedmen's Bureau, General Oliver Howard, to Edisto Island to tell the freedpeople the land is going back. They form a committee and
  write the letter from the cold open. Howard writes back that they're "right in wanting homesteads", but "Congress must meet" first.
- On freedpeople's rights, Johnson's line was simple: slavery is over, and that's as far as it goes. No vote.
- **Loop opened:** the South now gets to rebuild itself. What does it do with that? (ch6)
- Vocab: **Presidential Reconstruction**, **pardon**.

### 6 · Except as a Punishment for Crime (~240 words) ▼ bottom (heavy, voiced slower, no jokes)

- The good news first: in December 1865, the **13th Amendment** is ratified. Slavery is abolished everywhere in the United States.
  ✔ on the motif card.
- Put its words on screen and swipe one phrase: *"except as a punishment for crime."* "Hold onto those words."
- **But** the new Southern governments under Johnson's plan elect the men who ran the Confederacy. Georgia sends the Confederacy's own
  vice president, Alexander Stephens, to the U.S. Senate.
- And they pass the **Black Codes** (defined: laws that only applied to Black Southerners, written to keep them working the same
  fields on their former owners' terms). Mississippi goes first, in November 1865:
  - Every January, a freedman needs written proof of a job for the year.
  - Without it, he's a "vagrant." He can be arrested, fined, and if he can't pay, hired out to whoever pays the fine.
  - **The land link:** freedpeople can't rent or lease farmland outside towns.
- **Close the planted loop:** "That's the exception. A crime no white person could commit, punished with work." (Episode 2 picks this
  up again.)
- "Translation:" line on the vagrancy law, then space.
- Vocab: **13th Amendment**, **Black Codes**, **vagrancy law**.

### 7 · The Bureau (~250 words) ▲

- **Therefore,** who stands between freedpeople and the Black Codes? One federal agency: the **Freedmen's Bureau**, set up in March
  1865 under Lincoln, run by Howard.
- What it did, in four quick images: food and medicine; **schools** for children *and* adults; making marriages from slavery legal;
  helping families find each other.
- Human beat: the "Information Wanted" ads. Read one real ad from a Black newspaper (pick from the *Last Seen* collection) and let it
  sit.
- **Both sides, honestly:** the Bureau also pushed freedpeople to sign year-long labor contracts with planters, sometimes their former
  owners. It wanted them working; it didn't give them land. (Sets up sharecropping in Episode 2.)
- **But:** the Bureau was temporary. In early 1866 Johnson vetoes a bill to extend it, and then the **Civil Rights Act of 1866**,
  which said Black Americans were citizens with equal rights under the law.
- **Therefore:** Congress overrides him. The Civil Rights Act becomes the first major law in U.S. history passed over a president's
  veto, and the Bureau's extension follows that summer.
- Vocab: **Freedmen's Bureau**, **veto / override**.

### 8 · Equal Protection (~260 words) ▲

- Back up a step: in December 1865 Congress refused to seat the South's newly elected members, Stephens included. (Callback to ch6.)
- **Therefore,** the Republicans in Congress (Radicals and moderates together now, because Johnson keeps pushing the moderates away)
  write the **14th Amendment** (June 1866):
  - Anyone born in the United States is a citizen. That overturns the *Dred Scott* decision, which had said Black Americans could
    never be citizens.
  - No state can deny anyone "the equal protection of the laws."
  - **Say the cause out loud:** "The Black Codes are *why* this amendment exists."
- **But:** Johnson tells the Southern states to reject it, and they do. All except one: Tennessee, Johnson's own home state. (Dry
  aside: "Which must have been awkward at Thanksgiving.")
- **Mid-video subscribe plug** (about two-thirds in, after the build, not in a heavy chapter): two lines, wry, tied to the topic.
  Draft: "Quick thing. If this is helping, hit subscribe. It's free, and unlike the Freedmen's Bureau, it doesn't need an act of
  Congress to renew."
- Vocab: **14th Amendment**, **citizenship**, **equal protection**.

### 9 · Swing Around the Circle (~250 words) ▲

- Summer 1866: white mobs, with police joining in, kill dozens of Black people in Memphis in May and in New Orleans in July. Northern
  newspapers cover it. (Plain, numbers, no jokes; brief.)
- Johnson goes on a speaking tour to win voters back before the fall election: the **"Swing Around the Circle."** He argues with
  hecklers. In Cleveland he asks the crowd why not hang Thaddeus Stevens. *(The wry beat. One aside, e.g. "This is not a normal campaign
  strategy.")*
- **Therefore:** in the 1866 election, Republicans win more than two-thirds of both houses, enough to override any veto.
- **Close the ch2 loop:** who decides? From now on, Congress does.
- **Tease, not explain:** Congress's plan is coming, and it includes sending the army back into the South (**military districts**,
  Episode 2).

### 10 · Landless, Homeless, Voteless (~220 words), the ending

- "So let's go back to the question." Who decided what freedom would look like? Lincoln planned to. Johnson actually did, and
  his peace was generous to the men who led the Confederacy. The South used it to write the Black Codes. That drove Congress to answer
  with the Civil Rights Act and the 14th Amendment, and voters gave Congress the final say.
- **Both readings**, no verdict: to Johnson's supporters, he was following the Constitution: let the states run their own affairs and
  heal the country fast. To the Radicals, and to the freedpeople, he had handed the South back to the men who'd led it out of the
  Union.
- **Close the Edisto loop:** most of the freedpeople on Edisto lost the land. Some stayed and worked it for the returning owners.
  (Exact outcome to confirm; see flags.)
- **Motif card:** LAW ✔ (13th; 14th on its way). LAND ✘. Their three words again: *Landless. Homeless. Voteless.* "By the end of 1866,
  one of those words was about to change."
- **Tease Episode 2:** "Next time, Congress sends the army back into the South. And a Black minister from Mississippi takes a seat in
  the Senate that Jefferson Davis once held."
- **End screen:** thank-you and subscribe line, after the closing line has landed.

## Fact-check flags (verify before voicing)

1. **Petition wording and dates.** Committee letter to Howard, Oct 20 or 21, 1865 ("Landless, Homeless. Voteless."; "we were promised
   Homestead's"); petition to Johnson, Oct 28, 1865 ("A few acres"; "always true to this Union"). Quote from the scan, in the original
   spelling. Source: Freedmen and Southern Society Project, University of Maryland.
2. **What happened to Edisto's land.** Most was restored to former owners (e.g., a Bureau order restoring Bleak Hall plantation, Feb
   1866). Some freedpeople elsewhere on the Sea Islands kept or bought land. Confirm a specific outcome for Edisto before ch10.
3. **Field Order 15 numbers:** "about 40,000 people" and "about 400,000 acres" are the usual figures; mules were loaned by the Army,
   not in the order itself.
4. **Booth's reaction to the April 11 speech** comes from later accounts (Townsend, via Frederick Stone). Say "reportedly"; don't
   quote the words.
5. **Johnson's pardons:** say "thousands"; find a sourced number if we want one on screen.
6. **Civil Rights Act of 1866** as the first major law passed over a veto: confirm wording ("first major").
7. **Mississippi Black Code land clause** (no renting or leasing land outside towns): confirm against the statute text.
8. **Memphis and New Orleans death tolls:** use sourced numbers (Memphis: 46 Black people killed is the common figure).
9. **Stevens and Cleveland:** confirm Johnson's "why not hang Thad Stevens" exchange and the city.
10. **Lesson 1 notes correction:** military districts were not Lincoln's; they came in 1867 (Episode 2).
