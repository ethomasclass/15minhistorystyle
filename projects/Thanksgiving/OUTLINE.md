# Thanksgiving food video: beat outline (draft 1)

**Audience:** general. **Length:** 10–11 min, about 1,950 words of narration, 8 chapters. **Tone:** light and wry.
**Status:** facts marked [check] still need a primary source before the script is locked.

## Header
- **Core concept:** Every Thanksgiving dish was invented to prove something: that you're rich, a good home cook, or modern. And the cheap fake of the fancy dish keeps winning.
- **Driving question:** How did we get from eels to *this*?
- **Answer:** Showing off, then homemade pride, then companies selling shortcuts dressed up as homemade. Every era wrote its own "tradition."
- **Spine (the tug-of-war):** showing off vs. saving money, and homemade vs. store-bought. Joined by the running joke **"mock"**: mock turtle, mock aspic (Jell-O), mock homemade (test kitchens). Said out loud three times, with a MOCK tag on the board each time.
- **Long loops:** (1) the can from the cold open, which pays off in ch7; (2) "we'll come back to the turkey," which pays off in ch8.
- **Midpoint twist:** Jell-O was the rich family's dish.
- **Climax twist:** the companies redesigned the turkey itself.
- **Ending device:** back to the can; the gentle let-off.
- **Collage board:** one pin per chapter, from the turtle to the casserole, linked by teal *but* / *therefore* yarn.

## Chapters

**1. Cold open: the can (~0:45, 170 words)**
- Frame 0: a cranberry log slides out of the can, ridges and all.
- Number ladder: turkeys eaten each Thanksgiving [check, about 46M], then cans of cranberry sauce [check], then green bean casseroles [check].
- "It's a great meal. Honestly, it should be. But almost none of it was there at the first one."
- Name the strangeness: "They ate deer. Birds. And that fall, the colonists' big survival food was eels."
- Question: "So how did we get from eels to *this*?" Promise: "And why is so much of it shaped like a can?"
- A 1-second beat.

**2. 1621: the meal that proved nothing (~1:00, 190 words)**
- The bridge: "To understand dinner, go back to when it wasn't turkey in any sense of the word."
- The artifact: Edward Winslow's letter. Its whole account of the meal is a few short lines. Read the key line ("fowl"), then translate it.
- Massasoit arrives with about 90 men and five deer. He came for diplomacy, not as a dinner guest (one line).
- Eels: Squanto treads them out of the mud, "fat and sweet." No pie: no flour, no butter, no oven.
- Bradford's "great store of wild turkeys" [check the quote]: maybe turkey, maybe not.
- "Nobody called it Thanksgiving. Nobody wrote it down again for 200 years."
- **But:** "When Americans did start feasting in November, dinner had a new job: showing off."

**3. Showing off: turtle soup and meat pie (~1:30, 280 words)**
- 1800s holiday tables: a menu card opening with oyster soup [check the 1870 menu].
- **Turtle soup**: green turtles shipped alive from the Caribbean. Read Amelia Simmons' recipe straight: start by cutting off the head the night before [check the wording]. Deadpan beat.
- **Mince pie**: "the pie had meat in it." Beef, suet, fruit, brandy. Count it out: one meat for the soup, one for the main, one for the pie. The 1887 green-tomato version as a quick aside [check].
- "The point wasn't flavor. The point was: look what we can afford."
- **But:** "Not everyone could afford a turtle."

**4. Mock turtle: the first fake (~0:50, 160 words)**
- Mock turtle soup: a boiled calf's head, brains included, cooked to imitate turtle [check the period recipe].
- Running joke #1, said out loud: "Mock turtle. Remember that word. It's going to keep coming back."
- The fake was cheaper, and it caught on. Real turtle disappeared from tables; mock turtle stuck around [check the decline].
- **But:** "One woman thought the whole thing had lost the plot."

**5. Sarah Josepha Hale writes the menu (~1:30, 280 words)**
- *Northwood*, 1827: the turkey "at the head of the table" and pumpkin pie "indispensable" [check exact quotes]. Mince pie is on her table too [check].
- The surprise: she also wrote "Mary Had a Little Lamb."
- As editor of *Godey's Lady's Book*: 17 years of editorials and letters to five presidents. Show the 1863 letter to Lincoln.
- Lincoln proclaims a national day of thanksgiving. And it comes with her menu.
- Her ideal: everything from scratch, made all day, made with love.
- **But:** "Homemade takes all day. And by 1900, someone was selling a shortcut."

**6. Midpoint: Jell-O, the fanciest dish in the house (~1:30, 280 words)**
- Twist: in the 1800s, a shimmering gelatin mold (aspic) was a rich family's showpiece, made by boiling calves' feet for hours [check].
- Then powdered gelatin: Knox in the 1890s, Jell-O in 1897 [check]. The rich family's dish in ten minutes.
- Running joke #2: "Mock aspic."
- 1905, Perfection Salad: vegetables suspended in gelatin [check the Knox contest].
- Pattern interrupt: a hard cut to a bright 1950s savory gelatin ad (tuna or vegetables in lime gelatin) [find a dated ad]. Three seconds of quiet. "Yes. People really ate this. On purpose."
- **But:** "And companies noticed something. They didn't just sell the ingredients. They could write the recipe."

**7. The test kitchens (~2:00, 380 words)**
- 1917: the Angelus marshmallow booklet. Read it straight: sweet potatoes topped with marshmallows [check]. Motive first: they had marshmallows to sell.
- 1912, then 1941: cranberry sauce canned, then sold nationally as Ocean Spray. Long loop #1 pays off: "That's why it's shaped like a can. It *is* the can."
- Second-person scene: "It's 1955. You're hosting. Your in-laws are coming. And the magazine has a recipe…"
- A fast date montage: Butterball (1954). Green bean casserole (1955): Dorcy Reilly at Campbell's, with every piece from a can. [Plus one more 1950s item to check.]
- Running joke #3, said out loud: "Mock homemade. Store-bought, dressed up as Grandma's."
- **But / climax setup:** "And then they redesigned the turkey itself."

**8. The turkey, and the ending (~1:20, 230 words + plug)**
- The Broad-Breasted White: so heavy in the breast it can't mate on its own [check the wording]. It depends on artificial insemination. A deadpan line.
- Long loop #2 pays off: the bird on the table is an invention too.
- **Subscribe plug, about 80% in:** "Quick favor: subscribe. Unlike the marshmallow people, I'm not selling you anything."
- Back to the question: from eels to this. A recap stack: showing off, then homemade, then the shortcut dressed up as homemade. "That means every 'traditional' dish was somebody's new idea."
- Pull back to the whole collage board.
- Let-off, over the can: "So yes, your grandmother's recipe card was probably an ad. It's still delicious."
- End screen: "Thanks for watching. Subscribe, and I'll see you next time."
