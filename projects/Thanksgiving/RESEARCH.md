# Thanksgiving Menu: Fact Check and Research

Written from established scholarship and primary texts. No live web check was run this session, so open every URL and match every quote against a scan before the script is locked.

## 1. "1621 had no turkey, no potatoes, no pie; they ate eels." Verdict: PARTLY FALSE / OVERSTATED
- **Primary source, Edward Winslow, letter of 11 Dec 1621, printed in *Mourt's Relation* (1622), modernized spelling:**
  > "Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling, that so we might after a special manner rejoice together after we had gathered the fruit of our labors. They four in one day killed as much fowl as, with a little help beside, served the company almost a week. At which time, amongst other recreations, we exercised our arms, many of the Indians coming amongst us, and among the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some ninety men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted, and they went out and killed five deer, which they brought to the plantation and bestowed on our governor, and upon the captain and others. And although it be not always so plentiful as it was at this time with us, yet by the goodness of God, we are so far from want that we often wish you partakers of our plenty."
  - Source: Pilgrim Hall Museum, https://www.pilgrimhallmuseum.org/pdf/TG_What_Happened_in_1621.pdf ; full text at https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/ (search "Mourt's Relation")
- **William Bradford, *Of Plymouth Plantation*, fall 1621:** "...and besides waterfowl there was great store of wild turkeys, of which they took many, besides venison, etc. Besides they had about a peck a meal a week to a person, or now since harvest, Indian corn to that proportion." Bradford is describing the season, not the feast. Turkey is **plausible** ("fowl" could mean ducks, geese or turkey), so "no turkey" can't be proven. Manuscript scans: State Library of Massachusetts, https://www.mass.gov/info-details/of-plimoth-plantation
- **No potatoes and no pie: TRUE.** White potatoes weren't yet a New England food, and the colonists had no butter or wheat flour for crusts and no working ovens.
- **Eels: real, but not documented at the feast.** *Mourt's Relation* (spring 1621) says Squanto (Tisquantum) "went at noon to fish for eels; at night he came home with as many as he could well lift in one hand... they were fat and sweet. He trod them out with his feet." Winslow's 1621 letter also lists eels among their autumn foods ("we have... eels"). So eels were in the diet that fall, but neither feast account mentions them.
- **Accurate wording:** "The only foods recorded at the 1621 harvest celebration are wild fowl and five deer. Turkey may have been on the table. Potatoes and pie were not. Eels were a staple that year, and the Wampanoag had taught the colonists how to catch them."
- **Wampanoag view:** Massasoit (Ousamequin) came with about 90 men, roughly twice the ~50 surviving colonists. One theory (offered, never proven) is that they arrived in response to the gunfire. The five deer were a diplomatic gift tied to the March 1621 mutual-defense treaty. See Plimoth Patuxet Museums, https://www.plimoth.org ; Smithsonian NMAI, *Do All Indians Live in Tipis?* and the NMAI Thanksgiving teaching guide, https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/
- **Hook:** Nobody at the time called it "Thanksgiving." The Pilgrims would have reserved that word for a day of prayer and fasting. The two feast sources together run under 200 words.

## 2. "Victorian hosts served turtle brains to show wealth." Verdict: FALSE as worded
- What actually signaled wealth was **green turtle soup**, an 18th-19th c. elite dish in London and in US cities such as Philadelphia and New York. Live turtles were shipped from the Caribbean. Mock turtle soup was invented to imitate it and was made from **calf's head**, brains included.
- **Accurate wording:** "Turtle soup was the caviar of its day. Cooks who couldn't afford a turtle boiled a calf's head instead and called it 'mock turtle.'" Turtle soup on Thanksgiving tables isn't well documented; drop the Thanksgiving link unless a specific menu turns up.
- Sources: Amelia Simmons (1796, 2nd ed.) "To dress a Turtle"; Hannah Glasse, *Art of Cookery* (1747) mock turtle. Smithsonian: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/rise-and-fall-turtle-soup-180955393/ (verify slug)
- **Hook:** Simmons' turtle recipe opens by telling you to hang the turtle up and cut off its head the night before.

## 3. Lincoln 1863. Verdict: TRUE, but credit belongs to Hale
- Proclamation dated 3 Oct 1863. It was drafted by Secretary of State William Seward and set "the last Thursday of November next." Text: https://www.abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/thanks.htm ; Hale's letter to Lincoln, 28 Sept 1863, is in the LOC Lincoln Papers: https://www.loc.gov/item/mal2629300/
- **Sarah Josepha Hale** pushed for a national holiday for about 17 years, through editorials in *Godey's Lady's Book* (editor 1837-77) and letters to five presidents. *Northwood* (1827) describes a full feast: roast turkey "at the head of the table," sirloin of beef, leg of pork, goose, ducks, chicken pie, plum pudding, and pumpkin pie as "an indispensable part." **She effectively wrote the menu before the holiday existed.**
- **Hook:** Hale also wrote "Mary Had a Little Lamb."

## 4. Early cookbooks
- **Amelia Simmons, *American Cookery* (1796)**, the first American cookbook. It has "Pompkin" pudding baked in a crust (essentially pumpkin pie) and roast turkey served with "cramberry-sauce." LOC scan: https://www.loc.gov/item/16001941/ (verify)
- **Lydia Maria Child, *The Frugal Housewife* (1829)** has pumpkin pie and cranberry recipes. She also wrote "Over the River and Through the Wood" (1844), originally "Thanksgiving Day."

## 5. "Post-WWII corporations hijacked recipes." Verdict: OVERSTATED. Corporate recipes started before the war.
| Item | Facts | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Marshmallow sweet potatoes | 1917 Angelus Marshmallows booklet, recipes by Janet McKenzie Hill (Boston Cooking School). Angelus was made by Cracker Jack Co. | Pre-war (WWI era) |
| Canned cranberry | Marcus Urann began canning cranberry sauce in 1912. Jellied sauce went national in 1941 under the Ocean Spray name (co-op formed 1930). | 1941 is just before US entry into WWII |
| Green bean casserole | Dorcy Reilly, Campbell's test kitchen, 1955 | True, post-war |
| Perfection Salad | Mrs. John E. Cooke, 3rd prize in a 1905 Knox gelatine contest judged by Fannie Farmer | 1905 |
| Savory/tuna lime gelatin | A 1950s trend; Jell-O even sold celery, tomato and seasoned-salad flavors (c. 1960s). "Tuna in lime Jell-O" is real 1950s ad/cookbook fare, but find a specific dated ad before using it. | Plausible; verify |
| Butterball | Swift & Co. launched the brand in 1954 | True |
| Broad-Breasted White turkey | Bred in the 1950s; became dominant in the 1960s. It is too breast-heavy to mate naturally and depends on artificial insemination. | True; great detail |

**Accurate wording:** "Companies have been writing the Thanksgiving menu since 1912. The postwar boom just put it on every table."
Laura Shapiro's *Something from the Oven* covers 1950s food marketing.

## 6. Other beats
- **Franksgiving:** FDR moved Thanksgiving to the 4th Thursday in 1939 to lengthen the holiday shopping season. Twenty-three states refused to follow, and Texas observed both dates. Congress fixed the 4th Thursday in law on 26 Dec 1941. National Archives: https://www.archives.gov/legislative/features/thanksgiving
- **Pilgrim myth revival:** The 1621 letter was rediscovered and footnoted as "the first Thanksgiving" by Alexander Young in 1841. The legend grew 1880s-1910s through Jane G. Austin's novel *Standish of Standish* (1889), school pageants, and Jennie Brownscombe's painting *The First Thanksgiving at Plymouth* (1914). Those pageants were used to "Americanize" immigrant children.
- **1970 National Day of Mourning:** Wamsutta (Frank B.) James, an Aquinnah Wampanoag, was invited to speak at Plymouth's 350th anniversary. When organizers saw his speech, they told him he couldn't give it. He gave it on Cole's Hill instead, and it has been held every year since, organized by the United American Indians of New England. Speech: http://www.uaine.org/suppressed_speech.htm

## 8. Main characters
1. **Sarah Josepha Hale.** The holiday's inventor and the author of its menu.
2. **Ousamequin (Massasoit).** The Wampanoag sachem who came with 90 men and five deer, for diplomacy, not as a guest.
3. **Edward Winslow.** The colonist who wrote the only eyewitness account.
4. **Wamsutta Frank James.** The 1970 speech Plymouth wouldn't let him give.
5. **Dorcy Reilly** or **Marcus Urann.** The corporate kitchen side (or FDR for the "Franksgiving" fight).
