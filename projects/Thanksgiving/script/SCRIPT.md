# From Eels to This: How the Thanksgiving Menu Was Invented (script draft 1)

**Core concept:** Every Thanksgiving dish was invented to prove something (that you're rich, a good home cook, or modern), and the cheap fake of the fancy dish keeps winning.
**Driving question:** How did we get from eels to this? **Answer:** showing off, then faking it, then a homemade menu made national, then companies selling the shortcut dressed up as homemade.
**Long loops:** the can (paid off in ch7); the turkey (ch8). **Midpoint twist:** Jell-O was the rich family's dish. **Climax:** they redesigned the turkey. **Ending device:** the eel next to the can; the gentle let-off.
**Running joke:** "mock," said three times (ch4, ch6, ch7).
Voice cues in [brackets] are for the voice model, used sparingly. Facts are sourced in `../RESEARCH.md` (verified section).

## Ch1 · Cold open: the eel

The people at the first Thanksgiving knew how to catch these with their feet.

Eels. A Wampanoag man named Squanto showed the English colonists the trick. You wade out onto the mud flats, and you tread them out. "Fat and sweet," one colonist wrote. By that fall, they could fill a whole barrel with eels in a single night.

Now look at what we eat. A turkey. Green beans under fried onions. Marshmallows on sweet potatoes. And this.

By the industry's own count, Americans eat about forty-six million turkeys every Thanksgiving. And we open about seventy million cans of cranberry sauce a year, most of them in about two months.

[warm] It's a great meal. Honestly, it should be. But almost none of it was on the table in 1621.

So how did we get from eels... to *this*? And why is the cranberry sauce shaped like a can?

## Ch2 · 1621: the meal that proved nothing

To understand Thanksgiving dinner, you have to go back to when it wasn't turkey in any sense of the word.

Plymouth, autumn 1621. The colonists have made it through their first year. Barely. About half of the people who came over on the Mayflower are dead. Now the harvest is in.

So how do we know what they ate? Mostly from one letter. Edward Winslow wrote it home that December, and his whole account of the meal is a few short lines. Four men went out "fowling." In one day they brought back enough birds to feed the colony for almost a week. Then Massasoit, the Wampanoag leader, arrived with about ninety men. They went out and came back with five deer.

Translation: birds. Maybe ducks, maybe geese, maybe turkey. And venison. A lot of venison.

Massasoit wasn't really a dinner guest, by the way. The two sides had signed an alliance that spring, and this was diplomacy, with food.

What wasn't there? Pie. No real flour, no butter, no oven. No potatoes. No cranberry sauce.

And here's the strangest part. Nobody called it Thanksgiving. And for about two hundred years, almost nobody remembered it.

But when Americans did start feasting every November, dinner had picked up a new job. Showing off.

## Ch3 · Showing off: turtle soup and meat pie

By the 1800s, a fancy holiday menu could run course after course. It often opened with oysters. And at the grandest dinners of the age, the dish that said "we've arrived" was turtle.

Green sea turtles, shipped up alive from the Caribbean. Here's how one of the first American cookbooks, from 1796, tells you to begin.

"About nine o'clock, hang up your Turtle by the hind fins, cut off the head and save the blood."

[deadpan] That's step one.

Then it's cleaned, seasoned, and baked with Madeira wine.

Then came dessert. And the pie had meat in it.

Mince pie. The same cookbook's version calls for cattle feet, beef suet and wine, along with the apples and raisins. So count it out. Meat in the soup. Meat on the platter. Meat in the pie.

Was it delicious? Maybe. But that wasn't really the point. A turtle meant ships. A mince pie meant hours. A table like that said one thing: look what we can afford.

But not everyone could afford a turtle.

## Ch4 · Mock turtle

So people faked it.

Flip a few pages in that same 1796 cookbook and you'll find a recipe called "To dress a Calve's Head, Turtle fashion." You take a calf's head. You open it up. You take out the brains. And you cook it until it tastes, more or less, like turtle.

Mock turtle. [wry] Remember that word. It's going to keep coming back.

And the fake had a big advantage. It was cheap, and there was always more of it. The real turtles weren't so lucky. Caribbean green turtles were hunted so hard that, over time, their numbers fell by about ninety-seven percent. Real turtle slowly disappeared from American tables. Mock turtle hung around for generations.

The fake outlasted the real thing.

But one woman thought this whole holiday had lost the plot. And she had a very specific menu in mind.

## Ch5 · Sarah Josepha Hale writes the menu

Her name was Sarah Josepha Hale. And you already know one thing she wrote. "Mary had a little lamb." That was her.

In 1827, she published a novel called *Northwood*. And in it, there's a Thanksgiving dinner. Look at this table. The turkey sits "at the head of the table." There are pies "of every name and description." And among them, the pumpkin pie holds, in her words, "the most distinguished niche."

Sound familiar? That's basically your menu. Turkey in the middle, pumpkin pie at the end. Written down by a novelist almost two hundred years ago.

Hale didn't stop at fiction. She became the editor of *Godey's Lady's Book*, one of the most popular magazines in the country. And she used it. Year after year, she ran editorials calling for one national day of Thanksgiving. She wrote to governors. She wrote to presidents.

In September 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, she wrote to Abraham Lincoln. "For the last fifteen years," she told him, she had been pushing for this. Days later, Lincoln proclaimed a national day of Thanksgiving, on the last Thursday of November.

And Hale's menu came along with it. In her pages, Thanksgiving dinner was homemade. From scratch. All day long.

But homemade takes all day. And by 1900, somebody was ready to sell a shortcut.

## Ch6 · Midpoint: Jell-O, the fanciest dish in the house

Here's a dish that seems like it came from nowhere. The gelatin mold.

But in the 1800s, a shimmering jelly on the table was a showpiece. To make one, you boiled calves' feet for hours. Then you skimmed it, strained it, cleared it, and set it in a copper mold. Some were sweet. The savory ones, with meat or vegetables set inside, had a fancy French name: aspic. Only a house with a cook, and a lot of time, could pull it off. A wobbling aspic said: we have servants.

Then came the shortcut. Around 1890, Charles Knox started selling gelatine already ground up and ready to dissolve. In 1897, a company in upstate New York added sugar and fruit flavor and called it Jell-O. Suddenly, anyone could make the rich family's dessert. In about ten minutes.

Mock aspic.

And then home cooks got ambitious. Around 1905, a prize in a gelatine contest went to something called "Perfection Salad": shredded cabbage, celery and peppers, suspended in gelatin. By the 1950s, people were putting just about anything in there.

Like this. "Monterey Soufflé Salad," from 1955. That's tuna. In lemon gelatin. The rich family's aspic, now from a box.

[pause 3 s]

[deadpan] Yes. People ate this. On purpose.

And the companies noticed something. They didn't have to just sell the ingredients. They could write the recipe.

## Ch7 · The test kitchens

Take the sweet potatoes. In 1917, a marshmallow brand called Angelus put out a recipe booklet. The company behind it also made Cracker Jack. They hired a well-known cooking teacher, Janet McKenzie Hill, to write the recipes. And one of them was sweet potatoes, baked with marshmallows on top.

Was it a good idea? Maybe! But notice the motive. The point was to sell marshmallows. And a hundred years later, that booklet is on your table.

Then there's the cranberry sauce. Cranberries really are an American fruit, and people have been making sauce from them for centuries. But in 1912, a cranberry grower named Marcus Urann had a problem. Fresh berries only sell for a few weeks a year. His fix: cook them down and put them in a can. In 1941, his growers' cooperative took its brand national. Ocean Spray.

And that answers one of our questions. Why is the cranberry sauce shaped like a can? [wry] Because it *is* the can. Those ridges are the can's ridges.

Now jump ahead. It's 1955. You're hosting. The in-laws are coming at four. You've got a new refrigerator, a pantry full of cans, and a magazine that says you can do it all in half an hour.

In 1954, Swift and Company puts the Butterball turkey on the market. In 1955, in Campbell's test kitchen in New Jersey, a home economist named Dorcas Reilly is asked for a recipe made from things people already have in the pantry. Green beans. A can of cream of mushroom soup. Fried onions on top. Mix, bake, done. Green bean casserole. Campbell's says about twenty million of them get made every Thanksgiving.

Mock homemade. Store-bought, dressed up as Grandma's.

And then they went one step further. They redesigned the turkey.

## Ch8 · The turkey, and the ending

The turkey on most American tables is a breed called the Broad-Breasted White. It was bred for one thing: as much white breast meat as possible. And it worked. It worked so well that the birds got too top-heavy to mate on their own. Every one of them now depends on artificial insemination.

[deadpan] Happy Thanksgiving.

So even the bird is an invention.

[wry] Quick favor: hit subscribe. It's free. And unlike the marshmallow people, I'm not selling you anything.

So let's go back to the question. How did we get from eels... to this?

Step by step. First, dinner had to show off: turtles, oysters, meat in the pie. Then the people who couldn't afford that faked it. Then Sarah Hale wrote down a homemade menu and got a president to make the holiday national. And then companies sold us the shortcut, and dressed it up as homemade.

That means almost every "traditional" dish was somebody's brand-new idea. A novelist's. A magazine's. A marshmallow company's.

The people of 1621 ate what they could catch. We eat what somebody wrote down, and sold back to us.

[warm] So yes. Your grandmother's recipe card was probably an ad.

It's still delicious.

## Fact-check flags (before voicing)
1. "Fill a whole barrel with eels in a single night": Winslow says "a Hogshead of Eeles in a night" (a hogshead is a large barrel). OK.
2. "About half of the Mayflower passengers dead" by autumn 1621: standard figure; confirm the count.
3. "Signed an alliance that spring" (March 1621 treaty with Massasoit): confirm the wording.
4. "For about two hundred years, almost nobody remembered it": the account resurfaced and was labelled "the first Thanksgiving" in 1841.
5. Turtles at the grandest dinners: framed as dinner parties of the age, not Thanksgiving specifically, because the evidence for turtle at Thanksgiving itself is thin.
6. "One of the first American cookbooks": Amelia Simmons, *American Cookery* (1796), which is often called the first. Keep "one of the first."
7. Green turtle decline of about 97%: a historical-ecology estimate; confirm the source.
8. "Days later, Lincoln proclaimed": the letter is dated Sept 28, the proclamation Oct 3, 1863. OK.
9. Knox around 1890 and Jell-O 1897 (LeRoy, NY). Perfection Salad around 1905 (Knox contest; it won third prize, so "a prize" is accurate).
10. The 1955 Star-Kist "Monterey Soufflé Salad" ad: rights must be checked before it goes on screen; otherwise show a recipe page of the same era.
11. The Angelus 1917 booklet is paraphrased (no scan yet). "The company behind it also made Cracker Jack" (Rueckheim Bros. & Eckstein). OK.
12. "Fresh berries only sell for a few weeks a year": a simplification of Urann's motive; confirm or soften to "a short season."
13. Campbell's test kitchen location (Camden, NJ) and the 20 million figure (Campbell's, 2018).
14. "Most American tables": the Broad-Breasted White dominates commercial production. OK.
