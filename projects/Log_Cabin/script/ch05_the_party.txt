Then the campaign starts. And it isn't really a campaign. It's a party.

Whig rallies draw crowds in the tens of thousands. Newspapers claim even more. Parades, flags, fireworks, and songs. The big hit has a slogan built in: "Tippecanoe and Tyler Too." Tyler is John Tyler, his running mate. It rhymes, which helps.

There are songs about Van Buren too. The chorus goes: "Van, Van, Van is a used-up man."

Whig clubs build log cabins in the middle of cities, just to hold meetings in. Sometimes the raccoon is real. A Philadelphia distiller sells whiskey in cabin-shaped bottles, and some people say that's where the word "booze" comes from. It isn't. The word is much older.

A weekly newspaper called The Log Cabin, edited by a young Horace Greeley, prints the songs. Supporters roll a huge ball covered in slogans from town to town, shouting "keep the ball rolling!" That's where the phrase is said to come from.

Women can't vote, but plenty come to the rallies, and the Whigs love having them there.

And the candidate himself? He goes on the road and gives speeches. Candidates mostly stayed home back then and let others talk for them. Harrison has a lot to prove. He's 67.

The Democrats fight back with their own gimmick: O.K. clubs. O.K. for "Old Kinderhook," Van Buren's hometown. It was already a silly abbreviation, and the Democrats just made it theirs. It lost the election. But it outlived both parties.
