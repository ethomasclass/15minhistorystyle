December 1839. A newspaper in Baltimore.

An editor has just heard who the other party picked for president, and he is not impressed. He thinks the man is too old and too tired. So he offers some advice.

"Give him a barrel of hard cider, and settle a pension of two thousand a year on him, and, my word for it, he will sit the remainder of his days in his log cabin."

That's an insult. A president doesn't sit on a porch drinking cider. A farmer does.

The other party reads it, and thinks: that's perfect.

So here's the question I want to answer today: How did an insult about a log cabin get a man elected president?

And here's the first thing to know about the man in the log cabin. He was born on a plantation, to a father who signed the Declaration of Independence. Not exactly a cabin kid.

To see why this worked, we have to start with a crash.
