## Answering "Why were kids doing this on Columbus Day in the 1940s?"
They weren't doing it *because* of Columbus Day. The 1942 photos show an ordinary school flag ceremony. The Pledge and its salute were **born** on Columbus Day 1892, and schools used the same straight-arm salute every time they said the Pledge **from 1892 until December 1942**, when Congress changed it. The famous photos are from Southington, Connecticut, May 1942, taken by Fenno Jacobs for the U.S. Office of War Information (Library of Congress), the last months before the change.

## Fact-check flags
1. **The 1942 photo.** Fenno Jacobs, Office of War Information, Southington, Connecticut, May 1942: "Children stage a patriotic demonstration" (Library of Congress, LC-USW36-803 and related). It's a U.S. government work, so public domain.
2. **"Fifty years."** 1892 to December 1942.
3. **Columbus Day 1892.** The Pledge was published in *The Youth's Companion* on Sept. 8, 1892, for the National Public School Celebration of Columbus Day. President Harrison's proclamation centered the celebration on schools. The official program was for **October 21, 1892**. The script says "That October."
4. **"A popular kids' magazine… Francis Bellamy wrote… and a salute."** Bellamy wrote the Pledge. Who devised the salute is disputed (often credited to James B. Upham, the magazine's editor, or to Bellamy), so the script doesn't name its designer.
5. **"Millions of schoolchildren."** Estimates run from about 10 to 12 million.
6. **1920s–30s.** Italian Fascists adopted a similar raised-arm salute in the 1920s, and the Nazis after that. The U.S. was at war with both by December 1941.
7. **December 1942.** Congress amended the Flag Code on Dec. 22, 1942, replacing the Bellamy salute with the hand over the heart.
8. **Left out:** "under God" (added 1954) and "my Flag" → "the Flag of the United States of America" (1923–24). They're true but off the story, and the first would invite politics.
