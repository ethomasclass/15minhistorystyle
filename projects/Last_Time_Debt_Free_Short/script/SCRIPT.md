**15 Minute History · Short** · standalone vertical · 162 words (148 story + 14 subscribe) · about **0:56** at the normal pace.
**The idea:** the first episode of a "The Last Time…" series, and the channel's recession story told through one surprising date. It ties to the *King Andrew* long video, so set that as the Short's "Related video."
**Built on the analytics lessons:** a bold cover frame, a question in the first line, no text to read in the first 15 seconds, the answer said out loud at the end, and the picture changing every 3–5 seconds.

## Script

> When was the last time America didn't owe anybody a single dollar?
>
> January 1835. The national debt hit *zero*. The only time in U.S. history.
>
> President Andrew Jackson hated debt. Land sales were booming, money poured in, and he paid off every cent. Washington threw a party.
>
> It lasted about two years.
>
> That land boom was a bubble. People borrowed to buy land they'd never seen, and banks printed paper money to keep up. Then the government said: pay for land in gold or silver.
>
> In 1837, the banks ran short. People lined up to pull out their savings. Hundreds of banks failed. It was called the Panic of 1837, and the depression after it lasted about six years. Historians still argue over exactly what caused it.
>
> And the government? It had to start borrowing again.
>
> So, the last time America was debt-free? 1835. For about two years.
>

## Fact-check flags
1. **Debt hit zero in January 1835, the only time.** History.com and NPR's Planet Money: the debt was paid off in 1835 (Jan. 1 or Jan. 8 depending on the source; a celebration was held in Washington around Jan. 8). The script says "January 1835" and "threw a party" (the Jackson Day banquet).
2. **Land sales and the payoff.** Federal land sales and tariff revenue produced the surplus that paid the debt off.
3. **The bubble, the bank notes, "gold or silver."** Speculative land buying on credit and state banks issuing paper notes. The Specie Circular (July 1836) required gold or silver for most federal land purchases.
4. **1837.** The panic began in spring 1837 (New York banks suspended specie payments in May 1837). Bank runs. "Hundreds of banks failed" over the following years (a commonly cited figure is about 343 of 850 banks closed entirely or partly by the early 1840s; the script doesn't give a number).
5. **About six years.** The downturn lasted into the early 1840s (about 1837–1843).
6. **Causes are debated.** British credit tightening, falling cotton prices, the Specie Circular and the end of the Second Bank are all cited. Hence "Historians still argue."
7. **Borrowing again.** The federal government issued Treasury notes in 1837 and was back in debt soon after.
