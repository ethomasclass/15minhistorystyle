# The Last Time America Was Debt-Free (Short) · series: "The Last Time…"

**15 Minute History · Short** · standalone vertical · 168 words (154 story + 14 subscribe) · about **0:58** at the normal pace.
**The idea:** the first episode of a "The Last Time…" series, and the channel's recession story told through one surprising date. It ties to the *King Andrew* long video, so set that as the Short's "Related video."
**Built on the analytics lessons:** a bold cover frame, a vivid scene with people in the first line (the 1837 Flour Riot, like the execution Short's 20,000 at sunrise), then the question, no text to read in the first 15 seconds, the answer said out loud at the end, and the picture changing every 3–5 seconds.
**Neutral:** the current figure is stated once, as a plain number with no comment on who or why; the rest is pure history. No argument about today's debt, no modern politicians, and the cause of the panic is hedged ("Historians still argue…"). The ending says what happened, not what should happen.

## Script

> February 1837. New York City. Thousands of hungry people storm a flour warehouse and roll the barrels into the street.
>
> Just two years earlier, the national debt was *zero*. The only time in U.S. history. Today, it's more than *$40 trillion*.
>
> So what went wrong?
>
> President Andrew Jackson hated debt. Land sales were booming, and he paid off every cent. Washington threw a party.
>
> But that boom was a bubble. People borrowed to buy land they'd never seen, and banks printed paper money to keep up. Then the government said: pay for land in gold or silver. Money got tight, and food got expensive.
>
> By spring, banks ran short. People lined up to pull out their savings, and hundreds of banks failed. The Panic of 1837 turned into a depression that lasted about six years. Historians still argue over exactly why.
>
> So, the last time America was debt-free? 1835. It lasted about two years.
>

## Fact-check flags
00. **The Flour Riot, Feb. 13, 1837, New York.** A crowd of several thousand (accounts say about 4,000–6,000) gathered at City Hall Park over the price of flour and other necessities. Part of it stormed Eli Hart & Co.'s flour warehouse, threw barrels out of the windows and broke them open in the street, then hit a second warehouse before the militia arrived (J.T. Headley, *The Great Riots of New York*, 1873; Untapped Cities). Flour prices had risen sharply after poor wheat harvests and speculation, not only from tight money. The script says "Money got tight, and food got expensive" side by side, not as a single cause. The riot came about three months before the May 1837 bank suspensions, hence "By spring, banks ran short."
0. **More than $40 trillion.** Treasury's "Debt to the Penny" (total public debt outstanding) was about $40.31 trillion on October 8, 2026, and first passed $40 trillion earlier in 2026. "More than $40 trillion" stays true as long as the debt keeps rising. **Re-check on the day you post.** This is *total* debt, including about $7.85 trillion the government owes its own trust funds; debt held by the public is about $32.45 trillion. The 1835 figure is also total debt, so the comparison is like for like.
1. **Debt hit zero in January 1835, the only time.** History.com and NPR's Planet Money: the debt was paid off in 1835 (Jan. 1 or Jan. 8 depending on the source; a celebration was held in Washington around Jan. 8). The script says "January 1835" and "threw a party" (the Jackson Day banquet).
2. **Land sales and the payoff.** Federal land sales and tariff revenue produced the surplus that paid the debt off.
3. **The bubble, the bank notes, "gold or silver."** Speculative land buying on credit and state banks issuing paper notes. The Specie Circular (July 1836) required gold or silver for most federal land purchases.
4. **1837.** The panic began in spring 1837 (New York banks suspended specie payments in May 1837). Bank runs. "Hundreds of banks failed" over the following years (a commonly cited figure is about 343 of 850 banks closed entirely or partly by the early 1840s; the script doesn't give a number).
5. **About six years.** The downturn lasted into the early 1840s (about 1837–1843).
6. **Causes are debated.** British credit tightening, falling cotton prices, the Specie Circular and the end of the Second Bank are all cited. Hence "Historians still argue."
7. **Borrowing again.** The federal government issued Treasury notes in 1837 and was back in debt soon after.
