## The hook: "This painting was a message to a boss."

**Cold-open artifact:** Charles M. Russell's watercolor *Waiting for a Chinook* (also called *The Last of the 5000*), Montana, winter
1886–87.

A starving steer in the snow, hip bones showing. Wolves waiting at the edge. It's about the size of a postcard. **Look again:** it
wasn't made as art. The ranch's owner had written asking how the herd had come through the winter. A young cowhand named Charlie
Russell painted this instead of writing back.

**Why this works:**
- **The artifact is a story** (a reply to a letter), so the cold open has a beat every sentence.
- **It starts at the end** and makes the viewer ask how a boom became this in about 20 years.
- **It closes the episode:** chapter 9 is the same winter, and the closing line lands over the same painting.

**Driving question (by about 0:40):**
> The open range promised that anyone with a horse and some nerve could get rich. How did it go from a gold mine to this in about
> twenty years, and who actually got rich?

**Bigger promise:** "The answer is a story about who owns the grass." Seeds: "We'll come back to this steer." and, in chapter 6, "Hold
onto that treaty."

**Answer (the last chapter says it):** The opportunity was real, but short. Cheap Texas cattle plus a railhead made fortunes for the
men with herds and capital; cowboys (many of them Black or Mexican) got wages, danger and, for some, more freedom than they'd had
before. Gold in the Black Hills pulled thousands onto land promised to the Lakota by treaty, and the richest ground ended up with a
corporation. Barbed wire fenced the range; too many cattle and one winter finished it. Ranching survived, but as a business, not a
free-for-all.

**Possible titles:**
1. **Cheaper Than Dirt** · *Cattle, Gold, and the End of the Open Range* (recommended; from the barbed-wire slogan)
2. **The Last of the 5000** · *How the Open Range Died*
3. **Four-Dollar Cows** · *The Cattle Boom and the Black Hills*

Dates on the title card: **1866 – 1887**.

## 1 · The Last of the 5000 (cold open) · 0:00 · ~170 words

- First line is the hook: "This little painting was a message to a boss. It told him his cattle were dead."
- The story of the letter: Montana, winter 1886–87. The owner asks how the herd wintered. Russell sends this. (Hedge: the details of
  the letter vary in the retellings.)
- Look again: point with words. "Look at the hip bones. Look at what's waiting behind him."
- Twenty years earlier, cattle were the surest money in America. Driving question + bigger promise.
- Hook out: "It starts in Texas, with too many cows and no money at all."

*On screen:* the watercolor at frame 0, camera already drifting in; teal loops on the wolves; title "WAITING FOR A CHINOOK" on
orange tape; Russell's own caption in his hand if the scan shows it.

## 2 · Four-Dollar Cows · 1:20 · ~280 words

- Start in the action: Texas, 1865. Confederate soldiers come home to a state with almost no cash and millions of longhorns that ran
  wild during the war (hedge: "about five million").
- The price gap as a pair: a steer is worth about **$4** in Texas. In Chicago or New York, about **$40**. (Hedge, fact-check.)
- Just-in-time: "So why not just take them north? That's where the railroad comes in." (Callback to Ep 1.)
- One example carries it: **Joseph McCoy**, a cattle dealer from Illinois, picks a tiny town on the Kansas Pacific line, **Abilene**, and
  builds pens, a hotel and a shipping yard there in 1867. Texans can drive cattle to the track; the train does the rest.
- **Stack, then "That means":** cheap cows, a hungry market, a railroad in between. "That means a cow became a ticket."
- Define the setting: {open range}, public land with no fences, where anyone's cattle could graze for free.
- Hook out: "All you had to do was walk them there. About a thousand miles."

*On screen:* the price pair as two stamped tags; series map, rail layer from Ep 1, with Abilene pinned; McCoy portrait.
*Vocab:* open range, railhead, cow town.

## 3 · The Long Drive · 2:53 · ~300 words

- **The cattle drives map** (test item): our tilted map, trails drawn one at a time, each on its own word: the **Chisholm Trail** to
  Abilene; the Western Trail to Dodge City; the Shawnee Trail to Sedalia; the **Goodnight-Loving Trail** west and north to New Mexico,
  Colorado and Wyoming.
- One drive as the example: a herd of about 2,500, a crew of about 10 or 12, 10 to 15 miles a day, two or three months. The trail
  boss, the cook, the wrangler with the spare horses, the young riders eating dust at the back. (Hedge numbers.)
- Count it out loud: "Three things could end a drive. One: stampedes. Two: river crossings. Three: the people whose land you were
  crossing, who sometimes charged a toll in cattle." (Comanche and other nations in Indian Territory; hedge.)
- The scale: between the late 1860s and the mid-1880s, millions of cattle went north (hedge: "five million or more").
- Cow towns: wild for a season, then the railhead moves west and the town moves on. One dry aside.
- Hook out: "But one pair of ranchers went the other way, and their customer was the U.S. Army."

*On screen:* the map as the chapter's spine; a chuckwagon photo; a dust-and-cattle photo; "1/3" counter.
*Vocab:* long drive.

## 4 · Beef for Bosque Redondo · 4:34 · ~260 words

- **Charles Goodnight and Oliver Loving**, 1866. Instead of north to Kansas, they push west along the Pecos River to **Fort Sumner**,
  New Mexico.
- Why there (from your Navajo paper): the Army was holding thousands of Diné (Navajo) and Mescalero Apache people at a camp called
  **Bosque Redondo**, after forcing them to march there. The camp couldn't feed them. The Army needed beef. (Hedge numbers: "about
  8,000 or more.")
- Say it plainly, no joke: "The first big customer of one of the most famous cattle trails in America was a prison camp."
- Goodnight's color: he's usually credited with inventing the **chuck wagon**, out of an old Army wagon, for this kind of drive.
- 1867: Loving is wounded by Comanche fighters and dies at Fort Sumner. Goodnight later carries his body home to Texas.
- Seed: "Hold onto Bosque Redondo. We'll be back there in the last episode."
- Hook out: "So who were the men actually riding these trails?"

*On screen:* Goodnight portrait (coral); the Goodnight-Loving trail lights up on the map; a Bosque Redondo photo (Fort Sumner, 1860s,
quiet, teal only); a chuck wagon diagram.
*Vocab:* Bosque Redondo, Diné.

## 5 · Who the Cowboys Were · 6:01 · ~300 words

- **Myth beat:** "You probably picture a cowboy from the movies. He was usually older, whiter and better paid than the real thing."
- The real thing: mostly teenagers and young men, paid about a dollar a day (hedge: "$25 to $40 a month").
- **Black cowboys:** estimates run from about one in six to one in four on Texas drives (hedge). Many had been enslaved on Texas ranches
  before the war.
- **Flashback** (from your borderlands paper): before the war, this same Texas cattle country was where enslaved people ran *from*,
  south, across the Rio Grande to Mexico, where slavery was illegal. "Twenty years later, some of the same country was where free Black
  men rode for wages."
- For the unit question, both readings: on the trail, a Black cowboy ate, slept and was paid much like everyone else (hedge: "often");
  in town at the end of it, he hit the same color line he'd left. Nat Love (*"Deadwood Dick"*) wrote a lively memoir; say plainly that
  some of his stories are tall tales.
- **Vaqueros:** Mexican cowboys taught the trade. Count the words out loud: lariat (*la reata*), chaps (*chaparreras*), rodeo, ranch
  (*rancho*), bronco.
- Hook out: "In 1874, two things happened that would end all of this. One was in a gully in the Black Hills."

*On screen:* period photos of Black and Mexican cowboys (coral one face); Nat Love portrait; the Spanish words as torn-tape tags pairing
English to Spanish; a small Rio Grande inset map for the flashback.
*Vocab:* vaquero.

## 6 · 1874 · 7:41 · ~300 words

- Hinge year Stamp: **1874**.
- Just-in-time backstory: the **1868 Fort Laramie Treaty** promised the Black Hills to the Lakota (one sentence; Ep 4 opens on it).
- One example carries it: summer 1874, **George Armstrong Custer** leads an Army expedition of about 1,000 men into the Black Hills,
  with miners along. They report gold, reportedly "from the grass roots down" (verify).
- Newspapers do the rest. Prospectors pour in. The Army tries to keep them out at first, then stops trying.
- The government offers to buy or lease the Hills (1875). The Lakota refuse.
- **Deadwood**, 1876: from nothing to thousands of people in months (hedge). One color line: Wild Bill Hickok is shot there while playing
  cards. (Cut for time if needed.)
- Seed: "Hold onto that treaty. It's where the last episode begins."
- Hook out: "So who got the gold?"

*On screen:* the 1874 Stamp; the expedition photo (the long wagon train in the hills); the treaty boundary on the series map with the
miners' arrow crossing it; a Deadwood street photo.
*Vocab:* prospector, gold rush.

## 7 · Who Got the Gold · 9:22 · ~240 words · ends with the plug

- The arc of every mining rush, as three steps (count them): **one**, a man with a pan in a creek (placer mining); **two**, the easy gold
  runs out and the rest is locked in rock; **three**, it takes money, machines and a company to get it out.
- One example: the **Homestake** claim, bought in 1877 by a group of California investors (George Hearst's syndicate). It becomes one of
  the richest gold mines in the world and runs until 2002 (verify).
- **Stack, then "That means":** a free-for-all, then hard rock, then a company. "That means most miners ended up where they'd started:
  working for wages. Just underground."
- Deadwood also had a Chinatown (callback to Ep 1): Chinese miners and merchants, a few hundred people (hedge).
- **Mid-video plug** (~10:40, two-thirds in). Draft: "Quick thing before the fences go up. If you're enjoying this series, subscribe.
  It's free. Cheaper than dirt, even. Hold onto that phrase."

*On screen:* a three-step drawn diagram (pan → shaft → stamp mill); Homestake mill photo; Deadwood Chinatown photo if available.
*Vocab:* placer mining.

## 8 · Cheaper Than Dirt · 10:43 · ~300 words

- One example carries it: **Joseph Glidden**, a farmer in DeKalb, Illinois, patents a twisted wire with barbs in **1874** (patent granted
  November 1874). Point with words at the patent drawing.
- Why it mattered: the Plains had almost no trees for wooden fences. Wire is cheap, light and fast.
- The salesman's pitch (attributed to the barbed-wire salesmen of the 1870s; hedge): "Lighter than air, stronger than whiskey, cheaper
  than dirt." (Pays off the plug.)
- Who fenced, and who it hurt, both readings: farmers fenced to keep cattle out of crops; big ranchers fenced public land they didn't
  own, around water; small ranchers and cowboys lost the open range.
- **Range wars** (test item): the 1883 fence-cutting war in Texas; Texas makes fence-cutting a felony (1884). One forward-looking line:
  the fights go on into the 1890s (Wyoming's Johnson County War, 1892). Hedge as needed.
- Hook out: "The fences changed the range slowly. The weather did it in one winter."

*On screen:* the patent drawing with a coral swipe on "barbs"; a fence-cutter engraving; the series map grows a fence-line texture;
the slogan on three orange tapes.
*Vocab:* barbed wire, range war.

## 9 · The Big Die-Up · 12:23 · ~300 words · slower

- **Stack** the causes, counted: **one**, too many cattle on the northern ranges (investors, some from Britain and Scotland, had poured
  money in); **two**, a hot, dry summer in 1886 left the grass thin; **three**, a winter of blizzards and deep cold, January 1887.
- One example carries it: one ranch's count in the spring (verify a source; or Theodore Roosevelt's Dakota ranches, where he lost much
  of his herd).
- Losses: on some northern ranges, many ranchers lost more than half their cattle (hedge heavily; estimates vary widely).
- Quiet: carcasses in the creeks in spring. Hold a picture 2 s, no narration.
- Back to the painting (callback): "That's the winter Charlie Russell painted."
- What came after: smaller herds, fenced pastures, hay for winter. Ranching became a business. The open range was over.
- Hook out: "So let's go back to the question."

*On screen:* quiet mode; a period photo of a frozen herd or spring carcasses; the three causes as a teal stacked diagram; Russell's
painting returns.
*Vocab:* Big Die-Up.

## 10 · The Last of the 5000 · 14:03 · ~220 words · the answer

- **"So let's go back to the question."** Who got rich? Pull the threads: McCoy and the herd owners (ch 2), the trail crews' wages and
  freedoms (ch 5), the Black Hills gold and Homestake (ch 6–7), the fences (ch 8), the winter (ch 9).
- **Promise Ledger, row 2:** *Anyone with nerve can get rich* · *Who got it:* herd owners, investors, the Homestake syndicate, some
  cowboys for a while · *Who didn't:* most cowboys and miners (wages), small ranchers fenced out, the Lakota whose treaty land was
  mined.
- **Reversal over the opening image** (back on the steer). Closing line options:
  - A: "The open range was free. That turned out to be the problem."
  - B: "It took about twenty years to turn four-dollar cows into a fortune. It took one winter to turn it into a painting."
- **Handoff:** "The cowboys moved on. The farmers stayed. And they'd been promised something even bigger than grass: the land itself."
- **End-screen line.**

*On screen:* the Promise Ledger (row 1 from Ep 1 visible, row 2 writes in); the Russell watercolor for the closing line.
