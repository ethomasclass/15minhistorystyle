## The hook: "Absolute and undisturbed."

**Cold-open artifact:** the **Treaty of Fort Laramie, 1868**, Article 2, setting apart the Great Sioux Reservation, Black Hills included,
"for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Lakota (National Archives; verify wording).

Neat clerk's handwriting. Signatures and marks down the page. **Look again** at two words. Then the date the Black Hills were taken:
nine years later.

**Why this works:**
- **It's a written promise**, so the unit question doesn't need translating: here is what the United States promised, in ink.
- **It pays off Ep 2** ("Hold onto that treaty").
- **It sets the frame for the whole episode:** the Plains nations weren't only on the receiving end of promises. They made agreements,
  enforced them, fought for them, and won some of them.
- **It returns twice:** chapter 6 (the Black Hills taken) and the closing line.

**Driving question (by about 0:40):**
> The United States made promises to the Plains nations, in writing, signed and ratified. What happened to them?

**Bigger promise:** "And once you see what happened, the question this whole series has been asking gets harder." The series question
itself (Harkness) is held for the end.

**Answer (the last chapter says it, then asks the series question):** The treaties were real agreements between nations, and the
Plains nations held the United States to some of them, by negotiation and by war (Red Cloud won). But each time settlers, miners, a
railroad or a gold strike wanted the land, the United States broke the promise and then wrote a new one. War, the destruction of the
bison, reservations, boarding schools and the Dawes Act took most of the land that remained. The last chapter doesn't give a verdict;
it hands the series question to the viewer.

**Possible titles:**
1. **Absolute and Undisturbed** · *The Plains Nations and the Promises on Paper* (recommended)
2. **Two Pictures of One Battle** · *The Plains Wars and Who Told the Story*
3. **For Whom?** · *Manifest Destiny's Promise and the Plains Nations*

Dates on the title card: **1851 – 1890**.

## 1 · Absolute and Undisturbed (cold open) · 0:00 · ~180 words

- First line is the hook: "In 1868, the United States promised the Black Hills to the Lakota, 'for their absolute and undisturbed use.'
  Nine years later, it took them."
- Look again: point with words at the two words; then at the marks of the Lakota signers.
- Callback: "Remember Custer's gold in Episode 2? This is the treaty he rode past."
- Driving question + bigger promise.
- Hook out: "To understand that treaty, go back to the biggest gathering the Plains had ever seen."

*On screen:* treaty page at frame 0 with a DocLift on "absolute and undisturbed"; a coral swipe; the series map with the Great Sioux
Reservation outlined.

## 2 · Horse Creek · 1:23 · ~260 words

- Start in the action: 1851, near Fort Laramie. Thousands of Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow and others gather (hedge: "maybe ten
  thousand people"). The largest treaty council on the Plains.
- {treaty}: an agreement between nations. Say plainly: the Plains nations were nations, with their own governments, alliances and wars.
- The deal: safe passage for travelers on the trails; boundaries for each nation; goods paid each year. The Senate cut the payments from
  fifty years to ten (verify).
- The way of life at stake, briefly and concretely: the bison, the horse, the seasonal round.
- {reservation}: land set aside for a nation, usually much smaller than its homeland.
- Hook out: "Ten years later, the United States split in two. Out here, the war looked very different."

*On screen:* a period engraving or drawing of the 1851 council; the series map with the 1851 boundaries; bison herd photo.
*Vocab:* treaty, reservation, annuity.

## 3 · The Other War · 2:50 · ~340 words

- The frame (from your Navajo paper): the Civil War's western front was short (one Confederate push into New Mexico, stopped at
  Glorieta Pass in 1862). After that, most of the Army's fighting in the West was against Native nations. **Three of the six cards in
  this episode happen during the Civil War.**
- **Card 1 · Dakota War, 1862** (Minnesota): treaty payments late; traders refuse credit (the trader Andrew Myrick reportedly said, "let
  them eat grass"; hedge); the Dakota go to war; hundreds of settlers and soldiers killed. Little Crow. In the end, **38 Dakota men are
  hanged at Mankato**, December 26, 1862, the largest mass execution in U.S. history. Lincoln had reduced the list from 303 (verify).
  Plain, no aside.
- **The Long Walk, 1864** (Diné): the Army burns crops and forces about 8,000 or more Diné to walk hundreds of miles to **Bosque
  Redondo**. Callback to Ep 2: "That's where Goodnight's beef was going."
- What the 1867 **Doolittle Commission** heard (from your paper): starvation, disease and abuse at the camp. One plain line.
- 1868: the Diné sign a treaty and go **home**, one of very few nations to get their homeland back. ("Remember that year.")
- Hook out: "And in Colorado, in 1864, there was a camp flying an American flag."

*On screen:* Dakota card; Little Crow portrait (coral); Mankato is told without the execution image (use the card and a dated Stamp);
the Long Walk on the map as a dashed route; Bosque Redondo photo, quiet; the 1868 Diné treaty.
*Vocab:* Long Walk.

## 4 · Sand Creek · 4:43 · ~300 words · HEAVY (no jokes, slower, quiet mode)

- **Card 2 · Sand Creek, 1864.** Black Kettle's Cheyenne and Arapaho camp, told by Army officers it would be safe. An American flag over
  his lodge, and a white flag (as reported).
- November 29, 1864: Colonel John Chivington's Colorado volunteers attack at dawn. About 150 to 200 people are killed, most of them women,
  children and old people (hedge; estimates vary).
- One example carries it (from your Navajo paper): **Captain Silas Soule** and **Lieutenant Joseph Cramer** refuse to order their men to
  fire. Soule writes a letter describing what he saw. (Quote a short, non-graphic line; verify.)
- Both readings, said plainly: Denver celebrated the soldiers as heroes. Soule testified against Chivington before an Army inquiry.
  Congress's investigators called it a massacre.
- **April 1865:** Soule is shot dead on a Denver street (verify). Hold 2 s on his portrait, no narration.
- Hook out: "The Cheyenne and the Lakota answered with war. And one leader won."

*On screen:* quiet mode, teal only. Sand Creek card; Black Kettle portrait; Soule portrait; the letter as a DocLift; the investigation
report cover.
*Vocab:* massacre (defined by its use in the 1865 investigations).

## 5 · The War Red Cloud Won · 6:24 · ~240 words

- **Card 3 · Red Cloud's War, 1866–68.** The Army builds forts along the **Bozeman Trail** to the Montana gold fields, straight through
  the Lakota's best hunting grounds.
- One example: **Red Cloud**, an Oglala Lakota leader, leads a war against the forts. December 1866: the Fetterman fight; 81 soldiers
  killed (verify).
- How it ended: the United States **abandons the forts** and signs the 1868 treaty: the one from the cold open. "This is one of the very
  few wars the United States lost outright to a Native nation." (Hedge: "one of the few.")
- **Turning the camera around** (the "Three Lives" technique): 1870, Red Cloud travels to Washington and New York. He sees the cities and
  the crowds and the Capitol. At Cooper Union in New York, he tells the audience his side (verify a short quote).
- Hook out: "Six years later, gold in the Black Hills tested that treaty."

*On screen:* Red Cloud card; the Bozeman Trail forts on the map; Red Cloud's 1870s portrait (coral); a Cooper Union engraving.

## 6 · Two Pictures · 7:45 · ~320 words

- Back to the treaty (callback): 1874, Custer's gold (Ep 2). 1875: the Lakota refuse to sell. The government orders all Lakota onto the
  reservation by January 31, 1876, in the middle of winter. Those who don't come are declared hostile.
- **Card 4 · Little Bighorn, 1876.** **Sitting Bull**, a Hunkpapa Lakota holy man, and **Crazy Horse**. A great camp of Lakota, Cheyenne
  and Arapaho on the Little Bighorn River (Montana). June 25, 1876: Custer's 7th Cavalry attacks. Custer and about 260 of his men are
  killed (verify).
- **Two pictures of one battle** (both readings, and the myth beat):
  - Picture one: the lithograph *Custer's Last Fight* (Otto Becker after Cassilly Adams), which a beer company printed and hung in
    saloons across the country (hedge the number). Custer heroic, surrounded. This is the picture most Americans saw.
  - Picture two: the drawings of **Red Horse**, a Lakota who fought there, made in 1881: the battle from inside the camp.
  - "Look at who's at the center of each one."
- What came after: the Army pours in. Sitting Bull goes to Canada. In 1877 Congress takes the Black Hills, without the signatures of
  three-quarters of the adult men that the 1868 treaty required. Back to the treaty page.
- One forward line, dated: in 1980 the Supreme Court ruled the Hills had been taken illegally and awarded money; the Lakota have refused
  it, because they want the land (verify; "when this video was made").
- Hook out: "On the southern Plains, the same thing was happening, and some of the soldiers had their own story."

*On screen:* Little Bighorn card; Sitting Bull portrait (coral); Custer portrait; the two pictures side by side with a teal divider and a
Loupe on the center of each; the treaty page with "three-fourths" swiped.
*Vocab:* Black Hills.

## 7 · South and North · 9:31 · ~320 words · ends with the plug

- **Card 5 · Red River War, 1874–75** (southern Plains; 1874 again, Stamp). Comanche, Kiowa, Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho fight hide
  hunters who are wiping out the herds. September 1874, Palo Duro Canyon: the Army captures the camps and kills about 1,000 or more of
  their horses (hedge). Quanah Parker's Comanche surrender in 1875.
- **The scouts** (from your borderlands paper): some of the Army's best scouts in this war were **Black Seminoles**. Flashback: their
  families had fled south to Mexico with Wild Cat in 1850, the same border from Ep 2. After the Civil War they came back and enlisted.
  Four earned the Medal of Honor. The Army promised them land. In 1914, they were ordered off Fort Clark with none. (Plain; verify the
  four names and the 1914 eviction.)
- **Card 6 · Nez Perce, 1877** (north). Told to move onto a much smaller reservation, about 800 Nez Perce flee, about 1,170 miles over
  nearly four months, trying to reach Canada (hedge). They're stopped about 40 miles short.
- **Myth beat:** Chief Joseph's surrender speech: "From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever." "Those are the words an
  Army officer wrote down. Historians still argue about how much of it was his." (Keep both: the words are famous; the record is
  secondhand.)
- **Mid-video plug** (two-thirds in after cuts). Plain, no joke: "Quick thing before the last part. If this series is helping you, please
  subscribe. It helps other people find it."

*On screen:* Red River card (Quanah Parker portrait); the scouts' group photo (Fort Clark) in coral; a small border inset map (callback
to Ep 2); Nez Perce card with the route drawn on the series map; Joseph's portrait.
*Vocab:* Black Seminole scouts.

## 8 · The Bison · 11:18 · ~260 words

- Numbers as a pair: before the Civil War, maybe 30 million bison or more on the Plains (hedge: estimates vary a lot). By the late
  1880s, a few hundred wild ones (hedge).
- One example carries it: the photograph of a mountain of bison skulls, men standing on top (c. 1892, Michigan; the bones were ground
  for fertilizer and refining). Look again: the men are tiny.
- **Stack, then "That means":** a railroad to ship hides east (Ep 1 callback), a new tanning process that made the hides valuable,
  professional hide hunters. "That means the herds could be killed faster than any hunt in history, and shipped to a factory."
- Some Army officers openly welcomed it: no bison, no independence. (Don't quote the famous Sheridan line; its wording is disputed.)
- Hold 2 s on the skull pile, no narration.
- Hook out: "Without the bison, nations that had fed themselves for generations were dependent on the reservation's rations. Then the
  government came for their children."

*On screen:* skull-pile photo (Burton Historical Collection, Detroit Public Library); a StepBars from 30 million to hundreds (log
rescale shock); the railroad on the series map.

## 9 · Kill the Indian, Save the Man · 12:46 · ~300 words

- Reservations, and Grant's **Peace Policy** (callback to Ep 1): churches named agents; the goal was to turn Native people into
  farmers. {assimilation}: making a people give up their culture to fit into another.
- **Carlisle Indian Industrial School**, 1879, Pennsylvania. Its founder, Richard Henry Pratt: "Kill the Indian in him, and save the
  man" (1892 speech; verify). Haircuts, new names, English only, uniforms.
- The before-and-after photographs (e.g. Tom Torlino, Diné). Look again, carefully and respectfully. Say what they were made for: to
  prove the school worked.
- Both readings: the reformers called themselves "Friends of the Indian" and believed they were saving people; the families whose
  children were taken, and many of the students, saw a war by other means.
- **The Dawes Act, 1887**: reservations broken into family plots. Callback: "**160 acres.** Remember that number?" The land left over is
  called "surplus" and sold.
- **Stack, then "That means":** 160 acres per family, the rest for sale. "That means in less than fifty years, Native-held land fell from
  about 138 million acres to about 48 million" (1887 → 1934; verify).
- Hook out: "In 1889, a new religion spread across the reservations. It promised everything back."

*On screen:* Carlisle photo; Pratt quote on orange tape with a DocLift; the before/after pair with a Wipe (quiet treatment, no coral on
the children); the Dawes Act text; the series map: the reservation outline breaking into a grid (the 160-acre grid from Ep 3); a StepBars,
138 → 48.
*Vocab:* assimilation, boarding school, allotment, Dawes Act.

## 10 · Wounded Knee · 14:26 · ~320 words · HEAVY (no jokes, slower, quiet mode)

- **The Ghost Dance:** a Paiute prophet, **Wovoka**, has a vision (1889): dance, live peacefully, and the dead and the bison will return.
  It spreads to the Lakota. Agents and newspapers call it a war dance. It was not.
- December 15, 1890: reservation police sent to arrest **Sitting Bull** kill him in a struggle outside his cabin.
- His people flee to join **Big Foot's** (Spotted Elk's) band. The Army catches them at **Wounded Knee Creek**.
- December 29, 1890: during the disarming, a shot; the soldiers open fire, including with rapid-firing Hotchkiss guns. About 250 to 300
  Lakota are killed, many of them women and children (hedge). It was the 7th Cavalry: Custer's old regiment.
- One plain fact, no comment: about twenty soldiers later received the Medal of Honor (verify).
- Callback (1890, from Ep 3): "The same year the census said there was no frontier line." Hold 2 s on the photograph of the frozen
  field, or on a winter landscape if the burial photos are too graphic for the classroom.
- Hook out: "So let's go back to the question."

*On screen:* quiet mode throughout; Wovoka portrait; Sitting Bull's cabin; the map pin at Wounded Knee; a dated Stamp: 1890. (Pick the
Wounded Knee images with the teacher's audience in mind: see questions.)
*Vocab:* Ghost Dance.

## 11 · For Whom? · 16:13 · ~260 words · the answer and the series question

- **"So let's go back to the question."** What happened to the promises? Pull the threads, card by card: the 1851 treaty cut down
  (ch 2); 1862 and Bosque Redondo (ch 3); Sand Creek (ch 4); Red Cloud's treaty kept, then broken (ch 5–6); the scouts' land (ch 7);
  the bison (ch 8); 160 acres again (ch 9); 1890 (ch 10).
- The Plains nations' side, said plainly: they negotiated, fought, won and lost, and they're still here. (One line on the present,
  dated "when this video was made," with no politics.)
- **Promise Ledger, row 4:** *The promises made to the Plains nations* · *Who got it:* settlers, miners, railroads, the people the land
  went to · *Who didn't:* the nations the promises were made to.
- Then **all four rows together** (the series' big reveal). The **Greater Reconstruction** frame from Ep 1, one sentence: the question of
  what freedom meant, asked in the West with everyone in the room.
- **The Harkness question, on screen and out loud:** "**Did Manifest Destiny fulfill its promise? And for whom?**" Then stop. No verdict.
- **Reversal over the opening image** (the treaty). Closing line options (said *before* the question, so the question is last):
  - A: "Absolute. Undisturbed. Nine years."
  - B: "The United States kept a lot of promises in the West. The question is who they were made to."
- **End-screen line.**

*On screen:* the Promise Ledger, all four rows, then stamped; the 1868 treaty page for the closing line; the series question on orange
tape, held.
