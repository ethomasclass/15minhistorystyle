## The hook: "A new life in Kansas, for five dollars."

**Cold-open artifact:** the 1877 handbill *"All Colored People that want to Go to Kansas, on September 5th, 1877, Can do so for $5.00"*,
advertising the new town of **Nicodemus**, Kansas (Kansas Historical Society; verify wording).

A plain printed poster. A date, a price, a destination. **Look again:** who it's addressed to. Almost everyone reading it had been
enslaved about twelve years earlier.

**Why this works:**
- **It puts the unit question in the first ten seconds:** freedom and opportunity, on paper, for five dollars.
- **It reframes "free land."** The usual homesteader picture is a white family in a sod house. This opens on the people the promise
  was hardest to keep for, and the sod houses come later.
- **It pays off in chapter 7** (the Exodus) and **again at the end**, over the same handbill.

**Driving question (by about 0:40):**
> Free land was the biggest promise the government ever made to ordinary people. What did it actually cost, and who got to keep it?

**Bigger promise:** "The answer depends on who you were, and on something nobody in Washington checked: the rain." Seed: "We'll come
back to Nicodemus."

**Answer (the last chapter says it):** About 1.6 million families eventually proved up homesteads, a real and huge transfer of land to
ordinary people (hedge figures). But the land came from Native nations, speculators and railroads got much of the best of it, the
machines that made it pay also made farmers borrow, and west of the 100th meridian, 160 acres was too small without water, exactly as
one explorer warned Congress. Black Southerners who went west found more freedom than they'd left, and a hard life. By 1890 the census
said the frontier line was gone. It didn't ask who had been there first.

**Possible titles:**
1. **Free Land** · *Homesteaders, Exodusters, and the Edge of the Rain* (recommended)
2. **Rain Follows the Plow** · *The Promise of the Great Plains*
3. **Five Dollars to Kansas** · *The Homestead Act and the Exodusters*

Dates on the title card: **1862 – 1890**.

## 1 · Five Dollars to Kansas (cold open) · 0:00 · ~170 words

- First line is the hook: "This poster offered a new life in Kansas for five dollars. The people it was printed for had been enslaved
  twelve years earlier."
- Look again, pointing with words: the date, the price, the word "Colored," the town's name.
- Widen: in the same years, millions of people (Union veterans, immigrants from Germany, Sweden and Norway, single women, freedmen)
  answered the same kind of promise.
- Driving question + bigger promise.
- Seed: "We'll come back to Nicodemus."
- Hook out: "It starts at a land office, with a line out the door."

*On screen:* the handbill at frame 0, camera already moving; a coral swipe on "$5.00"; teal loop on "Kansas."

## 2 · 160 Acres · 1:20 · ~320 words

- Start in the action: a land office on the Plains, a line of people with papers. (Period engraving or photo.)
- Just-in-time: "That's where the **Homestead Act** comes in." 1862, the year from Ep 1 (callback, Stamp).
- The deal, counted out: **one**, be 21 or the head of a family; **two**, file a claim and pay a small fee (about $18; hedge); **three**,
  live on it and farm it for **five years**. Then 160 acres is yours.
- Who could: citizens and immigrants who intended to become citizens; women who were single, widowed or heads of household; after the
  14th Amendment, Black Americans. Who couldn't: anyone who had fought against the United States (until that changed). (Hedge the
  Confederate clause.)
- The 1866 Southern Homestead Act for freedmen, in one plain line: the land offered was mostly poor, and few claims succeeded (hedge).
- The **Morrill Act**, also 1862: each state got federal land to sell to pay for colleges teaching farming and engineering, the
  land-grant colleges. Say plainly where the land came from: much of it from land taken from or ceded by Native nations.
- Both readings: the biggest giveaway of land to ordinary people in American history; and land that was not empty.
- Hook out: "So who was going to fill it? The newspapers had an idea."

*On screen:* the three requirements as a numbered card; a homestead certificate (National Archives) with a swipe on "one hundred and
sixty acres"; the series map gets a homestead-claim grid; the Morrill Act as a second stamped card.
*Vocab:* Homestead Act, homestead, land-grant college.

## 3 · Go West · 3:06 · ~240 words

- **The Greeley excerpt** (test item) on screen, read and translated. *(Which excerpt? See questions.)*
- **Myth beat:** "'Go West, young man.' You've probably heard that Horace Greeley said it. He used ideas like it, and his name stuck to

- Who else was selling the West: railroads with land to sell (callback to the Ep 1 land grants) printed posters in English, German,

- Fraud, one example: a claim that the law needed a "12 by 14" house, and the story of a builder who put up a house 12 by 14 *inches*

- Speculators and railroads got much of the best land. (Hedge numbers.)
- Hook out: "For the people who actually showed up, the first problem was simple. There were no trees."

*Vocab:* speculator.

## 4 · Nebraska Marble · 4:27 · ~320 words

- One example carries it: a **Solomon Butcher** photograph from Custer County, Nebraska, 1880s. A family in front of a house made of
  dirt. Look again at one detail (pick a photo with a clear oddity, e.g. the cow on the roof or the family's prized organ outside).
- How it was built: strips of prairie sod cut about a foot wide and stacked like bricks. People joked it was "Nebraska marble." Cool in
  summer, warm in winter. Also: dirt falling from the ceiling, snakes, rain that leaked for days after a storm.
- Fuel: no trees, so dried buffalo or cow chips. Women's work, said plainly: cooking, water, children, chips, the garden, the books.
- **1874 again** (hinge Stamp): the grasshopper summer. Swarms of Rocky Mountain locusts (hedge: "so many they blocked out the sun") ate
  crops and, reportedly, harness and tool handles.
- {sodbuster}: a farmer who broke the prairie sod.
- Hook out: "To make this land pay, you needed machines."

*On screen:* two or three Butcher photos (Nebraska State Historical Society); a drawn sod-brick diagram; the 1874 Stamp; a locust-plague
engraving.
*Vocab:* sod house, sodbuster.

## 5 · The Machine Card · 6:14 · ~260 words

- One card, built step by step (test items), each item ruling out an old way:
  - **Steel plow:** cast iron stuck in prairie sod; John Deere's steel blade (first made in 1837) cut through and stayed clean.
  - **Windmill:** no streams, so pump water up from deep underground.
  - **Reaper:** cut grain by horse instead of by hand.
  - **Grain binder:** cut it *and* tie it in bundles with twine (twine binder, 1878).
- Barbed wire returns (callback to Ep 2): fence the cattle out.
- **Stack, then "That means":** more land worked, more grain, falling prices, and machines bought on credit. "That means a good year
  paid the bank. A bad year took the farm."
- Seed (one line): "By the 1890s, farmers would organize to fight back. That's another story."
- Hook out: "And there was one thing no machine could make."

*On screen:* the machine card as a 4-row teal diagram (old way → new machine), each row landing on its word; the series map's farm
layer.
*Vocab:* grain binder.

## 6 · The Edge of the Rain · 7:41 · ~280 words

- The line on the map: the **100th meridian**. West of it, most years bring less than 20 inches of rain. Farming without irrigation gets
  risky. Point with words at the line.
- **Myth beat:** the land promoters' slogan, **"rain follows the plow"**: break the sod and the rain will come. "It didn't. A run of wet
  years in the 1880s just made it look true for a while."
- One example carries it (from your Powell paper): **John Wesley Powell**, a one-armed Union veteran (he lost his arm at Shiloh), who
  took boats down the Colorado River through the Grand Canyon in 1869. His log has him clinging to a cliff he wasn't sure he could
  get down.
- 1878: his *Report on the Lands of the Arid Region*. His point, translated: 160 acres was too small out here. A farm with irrigation
  might need 80; a ranch without it, about 2,560. Organize settlement around water. Congress ignored him.
- **Dry farming:** deep plowing, leaving fields fallow to save moisture, and hardy wheat (Turkey Red, brought by Mennonite immigrants in
  1874; verify).
- Hook out: "For one group of settlers, though, the risk was worth it. Because of what they were leaving."

*On screen:* the series map with the 100th meridian and the 20-inch line in teal; Powell portrait (coral) and a canyon photo from his
survey; the report's title page with a DocLift on the acreage line.
*Vocab:* 100th meridian, dry farming.

## 7 · Exodus · 9:15 · ~320 words · ends with the plug

- Back to the handbill (callback). **Nicodemus**, founded 1877.
- Just-in-time: why leave? After the Panic of 1873 (Ep 1 callback), the North pulled back; by 1877, Reconstruction was over.
  Sharecropping debt, violence, and no vote that counted. (Plain.)
- One example: **Benjamin "Pap" Singleton**, who had escaped slavery, organized Black settlement in Kansas and printed circulars.
- **1879, the Exodus:** thousands of Black Southerners head for Kansas, many on rumors of free land and free transport. They called
  themselves **Exodusters**. (Hedge numbers: "about six thousand in a few months; more over the next years.")
- Some got stranded on the levee at St. Louis; Black churches and aid societies fed them.
- Both readings: some Black leaders urged people to go; others, including Frederick Douglass, urged them to stay and fight in the
  South. Southern planters tried to stop them leaving.
- The result for the unit question: in Kansas, more freedom than they'd left: a vote, land, a town of their own. Also: dry land,
  poverty, and a railroad that skipped Nicodemus. The town is still there.
- **Mid-video plug** (~10:55, two-thirds in). No joke here; keep it plain: "Quick thing before the hardest part of this story. If this
  series is helping you, subscribe. It's free and it helps other people find it."

*On screen:* the handbill again with a new swipe; Singleton's circular; the Exoduster photo on the levee or in Kansas; Nicodemus photo
(1885); the two-views card for Douglass vs. Singleton.
*Vocab:* Exodusters, sharecropping.

## 8 · The Children's Blizzard · 11:02 · ~300 words · HEAVY (no jokes, slower voice, quiet mode)

- January 12, 1888. The morning is mild, so children walk to school without heavy coats.
- Around midday, a cold front hits Nebraska, Dakota Territory and Minnesota. The temperature falls by dozens of degrees in hours; snow
  so thick you can't see your hand. (Hedge figures.)
- One example carries it: one schoolhouse and one teacher's choice: keep the children in or send them home. (Pick a documented case;
  the Minnie Freeman rope story is famous but embellished; hedge or choose another.)
- About 235 people die, many of them children walking home (hedge: estimates run higher). Hold a picture 2 s, no narration.
- Say it plainly, with no lesson-drawing joke: the promise said rain would follow the plow. The Plains had their own weather, and
  people had settled them anyway.
- Hook out: "Two years later, the government made an announcement."

*On screen:* quiet mode, teal only; a period schoolhouse photo; a newspaper headline from January 1888.

## 9 · No Frontier Line · 12:42 · ~320 words · the answer

- The 1890 census, and a bulletin the next year: there "can hardly be said to be a frontier line" anymore. Swipe the line.
- One line on what came next: in 1893, a young professor in Chicago argued that the frontier had shaped American democracy. *(Name
  Turner or not? See questions.)*
- Seed for Ep 4: "That same year, 1890, something happened at a creek in South Dakota that the census didn't count."
- **"So let's go back to the question."** What did free land cost, and who kept it? Pull the threads: the act (ch 2), the promoters
  (ch 3), the sod house (ch 4), the machines and the debt (ch 5), Powell's warning (ch 6), the Exodus (ch 7), the blizzard (ch 8).
- Numbers as a pair: about 1.6 million homestead claims proved up; about 270 million acres, about a tenth of all U.S. land (hedge).
- **Promise Ledger, row 3:** *Free land for anyone who'd work it* · *Who got it:* about 1.6 million families, many immigrants, some
  Exodusters · *Who didn't:* most freedmen in the South, families who went broke in the dry years, the nations the land was taken from.
- **Reversal over the opening image** (the handbill). Closing line options:
  - A: "The land was free. Keeping it was the expensive part."
  - B: "Five dollars got you to Kansas. Everything after that, you paid for yourself."
- **Handoff:** "All that free land came from somewhere. Next time: the people who were already there, and the promises the United States
  made to them, in writing."
- **End-screen line.**

*On screen:* the census bulletin with a swipe; the series map, frontier line dissolving; the Promise Ledger (rows 1–2 visible, row 3
writes in); the handbill for the closing line.
*Vocab:* frontier line, census.
