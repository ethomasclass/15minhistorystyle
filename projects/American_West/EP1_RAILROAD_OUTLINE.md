## The hook: "Look for the people who built it."

**Cold-open artifact:** Andrew J. Russell's photograph *East and West Shaking Hands at Laying Last Rail*, Promontory Summit, Utah
Territory, May 10, 1869.

Two locomotives nose to nose. Men on the cowcatchers holding up bottles. Engineers shaking hands in the middle. It's the most famous
picture of the railroad that tied the country together. **Look again:** the Central Pacific's crews were mostly Chinese, and in this
picture you can't find them.

**Why this works:**
- **It's a look-again artifact** (explainer rule 1). Everyone sees a celebration; one detail (who's missing) turns it.
- **It's the unit question in one picture.** A national triumph, and a promise of opportunity that left the builders out of the frame.
- **It pays off twice.** Chapter 5 returns to the photo with the crews who laid the last rail; chapter 9 ends on it, after the
  Exclusion Act.

**Driving question (by about 0:40):**
> The railroad was supposed to tie a reunited country together and open the West to anyone. So who did it actually open it for?

**Bigger promise:** "The answer isn't really about trains. It's about who counts as part of 'the country.'" Plus one seed: "We'll come
back to the men who aren't in this picture."

**Answer (the last chapter says it):** It did tie the country together: coast to coast in about a week instead of months, and it built
the West of the next three episodes. But it was built on government land and loans that rewarded speed and invited fraud; Grant's
government, which promised peace and protected freedmen in the South, was also shaken by scandals in the people around him. The Panic
of 1873, set off by a railroad, helped end the North's will to keep Reconstruction going. And the Chinese workers who did the hardest
part were the first people the United States banned by nationality.

**Possible titles:**
1. **Not in the Picture** · *Grant, the Railroad, and Who Built It* (recommended)
2. **Done.** · *The Transcontinental Railroad and What It Cost* (the one-word telegram from Promontory)
3. **Let Us Have Peace** · *Grant, Railroads and Scandal*

Dates on the title card: **1862 – 1882**.

## 1 · Not in the Picture (cold open) · 0:00 · ~160 words

- First line is the hook: "This is the most famous photograph of the railroad that tied America together. And the people who built
  most of it aren't in it."
- Look again: two engines, champagne bottles, a handshake. Point with words: "Look at the faces."
- One number: the Central Pacific's crews were mostly Chinese (hedge: "most estimates say around four out of five").
- Driving question + bigger promise.
- Seed: "We'll come back to the men who aren't in this picture."
- Hook out: "To see how they got there, start with a room full of empty chairs."

*On screen:* the Russell photo, camera already moving at frame 0; teal loops around the crowd; a coral swipe across "May 10, 1869."

## 2 · 1862 · 1:16 · ~300 words

- Start in the action: Washington, 1862. The Southern members of Congress are gone; their states have left the Union.
- Just-in-time backstory: for a decade, North and South had blocked each other on where a Pacific railroad would run and who would
  get the West. With the South gone, three laws pass in one year: the Homestead Act, the Morrill Act and the **Pacific Railway Act**.
  ("Remember that year. All three come back in this series.")
- **The series frame (said once, plainly):** "When people say Reconstruction, they picture the South. But the same question, what
  does freedom mean now and for whom, was being asked out West too, with more people in the room: Native nations, Chinese immigrants,
  Mexican Americans, Black homesteaders. Historians call this the Greater Reconstruction." {Greater Reconstruction}
- The deal, as one example: two companies, the **Union Pacific** building west from Omaha and the **Central Pacific** building east from
  Sacramento. For every mile, the government gives land on both sides of the track, and loans in government bonds, more per mile in
  the mountains.
- **Stack, then "That means":** land per mile. Money per mile. More for hard miles. "That means the companies weren't paid to build a
  good railroad. They were paid to build a long one, fast."
- Hook out: "So it became a race."

*On screen:* the empty-chairs idea as a drawn House chamber with Southern seats greyed; the three laws as three stamped cards
(1862); the series map's first layer: the two lines drawn toward each other. A checkerboard diagram of the land grant.
*Vocab:* Pacific Railway Act, land grant, Greater Reconstruction.

## 3 · The Race · 2:57 · ~320 words

- **Central Pacific:** a 690-mile line with the Sierra Nevada right at the start. No workers: men kept leaving for the mines.
- One example carries it: 1865, Charles Crocker tries **about 50 Chinese workers** (hedge). Doubters say they're too small. Crocker
  reportedly answered that they built the Great Wall. Within a couple of years, Chinese crews are most of the workforce.
- The pay gap, as a pair: Chinese workers paid less than white workers, and they paid for their own food; white workers' board was
  included (hedge the exact figures).
- **Union Pacific:** across the flat Plains, fast. Irish immigrants, Civil War veterans from both armies, formerly enslaved men.
  Rolling tent towns behind the line, nicknamed "Hell on Wheels." One dry aside.
- The UP line runs through Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho country. "Hold that thought. It's the last episode."
- Hook out: "The Union Pacific had the Plains. The Central Pacific had a mountain."

*On screen:* map with the two lines advancing; period photos of CP crews (Sierra snowsheds) and UP crews; "1/2" counter on the two
companies.
*Vocab:* Union Pacific, Central Pacific.

## 4 · The Mountain · 4:43 · ~320 words · the strike section is QUIET (slower, no jokes)

- Summit Tunnel: hand drills, black powder, then nitroglycerin. Progress measured in inches a day (hedge: "about a foot a day" range).
- The winters of 1866–67: snow deeper than the tunnels; crews living in tunnels under the snow; avalanches. How many died is not
  known; the companies kept poor records of Chinese deaths. "Say how we know": we mostly know from newspapers and letters.
- Quiet turn: **June 1867.** About 3,000 Chinese workers put down their tools (hedge the number). Demands: equal pay with white workers,
  shorter shifts in the tunnels, and an end to whipping by overseers (hedge).
- Crocker cuts off their food and supplies. After about a week, the strike ends. The pay doesn't change.
- Hold a picture with no narration for 2 s. Then: "It was one of the largest strikes in the country up to that time. Almost nobody
  outside the camps heard about it."
- Hook out: "Two years later, the two lines met."

*On screen:* the tunnel as a teal cross-section diagram; Sierra snowshed photo; quiet mode for the strike: a single period photo of a
CP camp, teal only.
*Vocab:* strike.

## 5 · Done · 6:30 · ~280 words

- April 28, 1869: Central Pacific crews lay **ten miles of track in one day**, a record. One number, then the object: "That's about
  25,000 ties and 3,500 rails, by hand" (hedge, fact-check).
- **May 10, 1869, Promontory Summit.** The telegraph is wired to the spike so the whole country can "hear" the blows. The message goes
  out: **"DONE."**
- **Myth beat:** "You may have heard they drove a golden spike to finish the railroad. They did. Sort of. It went into a pre-drilled
  hole, got a ceremonial tap, and came right back out. An iron spike went in its place. And the man with the hammer, Leland Stanford,
  reportedly missed."
- Back to the cold-open photo. **Look again:** the Chinese crew that laid the last rails was there that day (hedge: accounts say they
  were cheered at the ceremony). They're not in the famous frame.
- What it changed, as a pair: New York to San Francisco took months by wagon or ship. Now, about a week.
- Hook out: "The man who'd won the war was now president. And he'd promised the country something too."

*On screen:* the "DONE" telegram as a stamped card; a Loupe on the photo's edges; a time comparison bar (months vs. a week).

## 6 · Let Us Have Peace · 8:04 · ~300 words

- Grant's 1868 campaign slogan, from his acceptance letter: **"Let us have peace."** Translation: the war's over; let's finish the job.
- Supporters' evidence: he signed the Enforcement Acts and used federal troops against the Ku Klux Klan (1871); the 15th Amendment was
  ratified on his watch (1870); his "Peace Policy" promised to deal with Native nations through churches and reservations instead of
  war. ("Remember that policy. It comes back in Episode 4.")
- **The Grant cartoon** (test item) as the chapter's artifact. Look again at one detail; translate it. *(Which cartoon? See questions.)*
- Critics' evidence, as a setup: Grant trusted his friends. Some of them didn't deserve it.
- Hook out: "And the biggest scandal of all had started before he even took office."

*On screen:* Grant portrait (coral); the slogan on orange tape; "two views" layout: supporters left, critics right (left side filled
now, right side filled in ch 7).
*Vocab:* Enforcement Acts, Peace Policy.

## 7 · Where It Will Do the Most Good · 9:44 · ~300 words · ends with the plug

- One example carries it: **Crédit Mobilier.** The Union Pacific's insiders set up their own construction company, hired it to build
  the railroad, and paid it far more than the work cost, with government money behind it.
- **Stack, then "That means":** the same men owned the railroad, owned the builder, and set the price. "That means they were billing
  themselves, and the public was paying."
- Congressman Oakes Ames handed out shares to other members of Congress, cheaply, and wrote that he'd put them "where they will do most
  good" (verify wording).
- 1872: a newspaper breaks the story in the middle of Grant's re-election campaign. Vice President Schuyler Colfax is caught up in it.
  Say plainly: the deal began before Grant's term, and Grant himself wasn't accused of taking shares.
- **Whiskey Ring** (1875): distillers and Treasury officials skimming the whiskey tax. Grant's own private secretary, Orville Babcock,
  is charged. Grant says "Let no guilty man escape," then testifies in Babcock's favor. Babcock is acquitted. Dry aside.
- Both readings, said out loud: to supporters, an honest man with bad friends; to critics, a president who let it happen.
- **Mid-video plug** (~11:20, two-thirds in). Draft: "Quick thing before the money runs out. If you're enjoying this, subscribe. Unlike
  Oakes Ames, I'm not handing out shares, but it's free and it really helps."

*On screen:* a drawn money-loop diagram (UP → Crédit Mobilier → same men); the newspaper headline; the two-views card's right side
fills in.
*Vocab:* Crédit Mobilier, Whiskey Ring.

## 8 · Panic · 11:25 · ~260 words

- Start in the action: **September 18, 1873.** Jay Cooke's bank, the most famous bank in the country, closes its doors. It had bet
  everything on another railroad, the Northern Pacific.
- The stock exchange closes for ten days. Then a depression that lasts about five or six years (hedge). Railroads go bankrupt;
  workers lose jobs.
- Why it matters for the unit question: with the economy collapsing, Northern voters turn to their own problems. In 1874, the
  Democrats win the House. The North's will to keep enforcing Reconstruction drains away.
- **Stack, then "That means":** a railroad boom, a railroad bust, a tired North. "That means the railroad didn't just change the West.
  It helped end Reconstruction in the South." (Hedge: "one of the reasons.")
- Hook out: "And when times got hard out West, people went looking for someone to blame."

*On screen:* the bank's closed door (period engraving of the run); a StepBars chart of railroad bankruptcies (verify a source); a dated
Stamp: 1873.
*Vocab:* panic, depression.

## 9 · The Picture · 12:52 · ~340 words · the answer

- After 1869, Chinese workers move into farms, mines, laundries, factories. In the depression, they're blamed for low wages. "The
  Chinese must go!" becomes a California slogan (Denis Kearney's Workingmen's Party, 1877).
- **Race science** (from your Powell paper): doctors and editorials claimed Chinese workers were a threat to "white labor," and some
  dressed it up as science, measuring skulls. Say how we know: it's in the newspapers and medical writing of the time. (Flag: check
  wording before quoting.)
- **1882: the Chinese Exclusion Act.** No Chinese laborers allowed in for ten years. Renewed, made permanent, and not repealed until
  1943. "It was the first major federal law to ban immigrants by nationality."
- **"So let's go back to the question."** Did the railroad open the West to anyone? Pull the threads: the land grants (ch 2), the race
  (ch 3), the strike (ch 4), Grant's promise and his friends (ch 6–7), the panic (ch 8).
- **Promise Ledger, row 1:** *Progress for a reunited nation* · *Who got it:* travelers, shippers, settlers, the railroad's owners ·
  *Who didn't:* the Chinese workers, freedmen in the South after 1873, the nations whose land the track crossed.
- **Reversal over the opening image** (back on the photo). Closing line options:
  - A: "The railroad tied the country together. Then the country decided who was allowed to be in the picture."
  - B: "The men who built the railroad weren't in the picture. In 1882, the country made it official."
- **Handoff:** "Meanwhile, at the end of one of those new rail lines in Kansas, a man had an idea involving about five million cows."
- **End-screen line:** "Thanks for watching. If you want more stories like this one, subscribe, and I'll see you next time."

*On screen:* "The Chinese Must Go" cartoon or broadside; the Exclusion Act text with a swipe; the Promise Ledger card (first appearance);
the Russell photo for the closing line.
*Vocab:* Chinese Exclusion Act.
