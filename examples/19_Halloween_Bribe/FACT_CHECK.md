## 1. Earliest print use of "trick or treat"

**Verified fact.**
- The demand-at-the-door custom and its wording first appear in **Ontario newspapers in 1917**, as variants:
  *Sault Daily Star* (Sault Ste. Marie), 1 Nov 1917, p. 2: "'Tricks or treats' you could hear the gangs call out, and if the
  householder passed out the 'coin' for the 'treats' his establishment would be immune from attack". *Owen Sound Sun*,
  2 Nov 1917: "treat or trick".
- **Exact "trick or treat" before 1927:** *Red Deer (Alberta) News*, 4 Nov 1925, p. 7: juveniles made a house-to-house canvass
  "in response to their time-honored threat of 'trick or treat'". *Sault Daily Star*, 3 Nov 1926, p. 8: "'trick or treat,' being
  the password."
- **The well-known 1927 Blackie item.** It was printed in the **Lethbridge Herald, 4 Nov 1927, p. 5** ("'TRICK OR TREAT' IS DEMAND",
  datelined Blackie, Nov. 3). A *Calgary Daily Herald* item of 3 Nov 1927 also used the phrase, and CBC cites a *Blackie Times*
  of 3 Nov 1927. There is no paper called the "Alberta Herald". The Blackie item mentions **wagons**: "No real damage was done except
  to the temper of some who had to hunt for wagon wheels, gates, wagons, barrels, etc., much of which decorated the front street.
  The youthful tormentors were at back door and front demanding edible plunder by the word 'trick or treat'".
- **First US uses:** the earliest US exact-phrase item is the *Oregonian* (Portland), 31 Oct 1932. Then come Oregon, Montana and
  Washington papers in 1934 and 1935. It reached national US print in the late 1930s.

**Sources.**
- Barry Popik, "Trick or Treat", barrypopik.com (newspaper transcriptions with page and column), https://barrypopik.com/blog/trick_or_treat
- David Wilton, "trick or treat", Wordorigins.org, 7 Nov 2021, https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/trick-or-treat
  (treats Calgary Herald, 3 Nov 1927 as the first use "as the greeting at the door"; first US use Oregonian, 31 Oct 1932)
- CBC, "Albertans may have been 1st to say 'trick or treat'", https://amp.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.3829669
- Dictionary of Canadianisms on Historical Principles (DCHP-2), "trick or treat", https://dchp.arts.ubc.ca/entries/trick%20or%20treat

**Confidence:** High that the phrase is Canadian and from the 1920s. High that 1917 variants exist. Medium on "first ever", because
newspaper digitization keeps turning up earlier hits.

**Safe narration:** "The phrase 'trick or treat' turns up in Canadian newspapers in the 1920s, and versions of it go back to 1917.
In 1927, a paper in Alberta reported kids 'demanding edible plunder by the word "trick or treat"'. The same report has the town
hunting for its wagons the next morning." (The wagon detail is a free callback to the cold open.) Do **not** say "first used in 1927".

## 2. A well-documented 1930s city, and ban talk

**Verified fact.**
- **"Black Halloween" 1933:** popular histories call Halloween 1933 "Black Halloween": cars overturned, telephone poles sawed down,
  and vandalism nationwide. The source is HISTORY, which quotes Halloween historian Lisa Morton: "by 1933, the holiday had become so
  destructive that cities were considering banning it ... they might be able to buy these kids off". **No source I found names a
  city that formally proposed a ban in the 1930s.** No council vote or ordinance was found. The origin of the name "Black Halloween"
  is also undocumented in these sources.
- **Best-documented local 1933 examples (contemporary newspapers cited):**
  - **Fargo, ND:** six boys "all from prominent Fargo families" were charged with malicious mischief for false fire alarms. Porch
    lights and streetlamps were shot out. The *Fargo Forum*, 1 Nov 1933, is cited in the Briggs article.
  - **Bismarck, ND:** police used tear gas on a mob of about 300 boys piling junk cars and garbage at the high school (same article).
  - **Minneapolis:** *Minneapolis Tribune*, 2 Nov 1933: "Halloween Has Police Sequel: More Than 100 Juveniles" questioned (Popik).
  - **Chicago, 1933:** "the entire uniformed Chicago Police Department was placed on the street for Halloween, but still trolley poles
    were ripped out, windows broken, streetcar tracks greased and fences set on fire." This exact claim comes from only one
    secondary blog, and I could not trace it to the Tribune. Treat it as Low.
  - **Oakland:** greased streetcar rails forced the traction company to send out a special crew (Oaklandside, 2022, quoting the
    *Oakland Tribune*; that specific item is from the 1930s era of prank coverage, so check its year before using it).
- **The only real, dated "ban" found:** **Chicago, October 1942.** The New York Times reported on 15 Oct 1942 that the City Council
  voted to abolish Halloween for the duration of the war ("If plans work out, there will not be any Halloween in Chicago"). The
  Chicago Tribune reported that the council asked Mayor Edward J. Kelly to proclaim it "Conservation Day". I found nothing on whether
  it was enforced. Separately, some Pennsylvania towns banned adults from wearing masks in 1942 (Heinz History Center).

**Sources.**
- Christopher Klein, "Halloween Was Once So Dangerous That Some Cities Considered Banning It", HISTORY, 25 Oct 2017 (upd. 28 May 2025),
  https://www.history.com/articles/halloween-was-once-so-dangerous-that-some-cities-considered-banning-it
- Becky Little, "How the Great Depression Gave Rise to the Haunted House", HISTORY, 31 Oct 2018,
  https://www.history.com/articles/halloween-haunted-house-great-depression ("Black Halloween")
- Tracy Briggs, "'Black Halloween': The year Halloween went off the rails", Grand Forks Herald / Forum Communications, 29 Oct 2025,
  https://www.grandforksherald.com/news/the-vault/black-halloween-the-year-halloween-went-off-the-rails (cites *The Forum*, 1 Nov 1933)
- Adam Harrington, "In Chicago and beyond, Halloween was once associated with pranks, hooliganism and destruction", CBS Chicago,
  upd. 31 Oct 2025, https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/chicago-halloween-pranks-hooliganism-destruction/ (1942 "Conservation Day")
- "Home Front: Rationing Halloween" (quotes NYT, 15 Oct 1942),
  https://www.mydigitalpublication.com/article/Home+Front%3A+Rationing+Halloween/1780642/219594/article.html.
  For broadcast, pull the NYT 15 Oct 1942 item from TimesMachine to confirm.
- Oaklandside, "Halloween was once a chaotic night of pranks and violence in the East Bay", 28 Oct 2022,
  https://oaklandside.org/2022/10/28/halloween-east-bay-oakland-berkeley-alameda-history-pranks-violence-police/

**Confidence:** High for widespread 1933 vandalism. Medium for "Black Halloween" as a label (popular histories). **Low for any named
1930s city ban.** High that Chicago's council moved to abolish Halloween in 1942 (NYT plus Tribune via CBS); Low on enforcement.

**Safe narration (ch 3):** "By 1933, the damage was so bad that some people called it 'Black Halloween'. In North Dakota, police used
tear gas on a mob of 300 boys. Officials in some cities talked about banning Halloween altogether. Most decided a ban wouldn't work."
Avoid "cities banned it". If you want a concrete ban beat, use Chicago 1942 in ch 5: "In 1942, Chicago's city council even voted to
abolish Halloween for the rest of the war."

## 3. WWII sugar rationing and Halloween campaigns

**Verified fact.**
- Sugar was the **first food rationed** in the US and the **last to come off**. Sales were halted in **late April 1942**. War Ration
  Book One (the "Sugar Book") was issued **4 and 5 May 1942** (sources give both days). The allowance was about half a pound per
  person per week (about 26 lb a year), cut to 15 lb a year in May 1945. Rationing ended in **June 1947**.
- Candy: armed services had priority (1943 Life Savers ad). Newspapers ran low-sugar Halloween recipes.
- **Campaigns replacing pranks (real):** youth groups organized anti-vandalism campaigns and **scrap drives** (*Oshkosh Northwestern*,
  1942, via National WWII Museum). The *Des Moines Register* likened pranksters to saboteurs "helping the Axis". Pittsburgh's
  **Junior Commandos** pledged no destructive pranks and suggested collecting scrap metal while trick-or-treating. Pittsburgh judges
  warned that soap, metal and rubber were needed for the war. Children in Pittsfield, Mass. vowed to "back our fighting men by
  observing Halloween as they would want me to" (HISTORY). The NYT reported in 1942 that Rochester's school superintendent called
  soaping windows out of place, since soap and grease were needed for the war. Chicago's council voted to abolish Halloween for the
  duration (flag 2).
- After the war: by fall 1947, *Jack and Jill* and *Children's Activities* magazines featured trick-or-treating. UNICEF's campaign
  began in 1950. "By 1952 ... firmly established" (HISTORY).

**Sources.**
- U.S. National Park Service, "Sugar: The First and Last Food Rationed on the World War II Home Front" (upd. 16 Nov 2023),
  https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/sugar-the-first-and-last-food-rationed-on-the-world-war-ii-home-front.htm
- Brynn Holland, "How Donald Duck and Peanuts Saved Trick-or-Treating After WWII", HISTORY, 10 Oct 2017 (upd. 15 Oct 2025),
  https://www.history.com/articles/how-donald-duck-and-peanuts-saved-trick-or-treating
- Abbie Edens, "Halloween on the Home Front", National WWII Museum, 23 Oct 2020,
  https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/halloween-in-1940s-home-front
- Leslie Przybylek, "Have Fun but Remember the War: Halloween during World War II", Heinz History Center, 28 Oct 2021,
  https://www.heinzhistorycenter.org/blog/general/have-fun-but-remember-the-war-halloween-during-world-war-ii

**Confidence:** High for the dates. High that anti-prank and scrap campaigns existed. Medium on specifics of individual campaigns.

**Safe narration (ch 5):** "In May 1942, sugar became the first food America rationed, and it stayed rationed until 1947. Halloween
got pulled way back. Kids were told that pranks helped the enemy. Some towns ran scrap drives instead, and Chicago's city council
even voted to call the whole thing off for the duration." The plug joke ("unlike 1943, I'm not asking for your sugar") is fine.

## 4. Irish famine emigration numbers

**Verified fact.** In the Great Famine (1845 to 1852) and the years right after, roughly **2 to 2.1 million** people left Ireland
(1845 to 1855), about a quarter of the population. Kerby A. Miller estimates **about 1.5 million went overseas, mainly to the
United States**, plus 200,000 to 300,000 to Britain. About a million died. Estimates vary by date range and destination.

**Outline issue (causality).** The "Famine immigrants bring it over. **Therefore** the jack-o'-lantern switches vegetables" step is a
simplification. Scots and Irish Halloween customs reached North America before the 1840s. "Jack-o'-lantern" was in American print by
the 1830s (Hawthorne, *Twice-Told Tales*, 1837; single source, unverified). Pumpkin lanterns were known in America independently. The
famine wave did spread Irish Halloween customs widely, so hedge the switch.

**Sources.**
- IrishCentral, "Facts about Great Famine emigration from Ireland" (quotes Ciarán Ó Murchadha, *The Great Famine*, 2011: about 2.1 million,
  1845 to 1855), https://www.irishcentral.com/roots/history/facts-great-famine-emigration-ireland
- Kerby A. Miller, *Emigrants and Exiles* (Oxford UP, 1985), via UMass Lowell, "Irish Immigration: Interpreting the Irish Famine",
  https://faculty.uml.edu/sgallagher/irish.htm
- Cambridge, *Irish Historical Studies*, "Which Irish men and women immigrated to the United States during the Great Famine migration of 1846–54?",
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/irish-historical-studies/article/abs/which-irish-men-and-women-immigrated-to-the-united-states-during-the-great-famine-migration-of-184654/1D1E9244601ABDCF2C7E28EED3CF321C

**Confidence:** High for the order of magnitude. Medium for the exact figure.

**Safe narration:** "In the 1840s, famine drove more than a million Irish people to America, and they brought Halloween with them. But
in America the big, cheap, easy-to-carve vegetable was the pumpkin." (Say "brought their Halloween customs", not "brought Halloween to
America" as if it were absent.)

## 5. The Samhain link

**Verified fact.** The popular "Halloween is Samhain" line comes largely from 19th-century scholars: John Rhys proposed Samhain as a
"Celtic New Year", and James Frazer popularized the idea while admitting the evidence was inconclusive. Ronald Hutton (*The Stations of
the Sun*, 1996) finds little evidence that Samhain was a festival of the dead. He argues the dead-related customs came from the
Christian All Hallows / All Souls observances. Steve Roud rejects the Samhain-origin theory for lack of evidence. Others, such as
Nicholas Rogers (*Halloween: From Pagan Ritual to Party Night*, 2002), accept some Samhain inheritance alongside the Christian feast.
The name itself is Christian: "All Hallows' Eve".

**Sources.**
- Ronald Hutton, *The Stations of the Sun: A History of the Ritual Year in Britain* (Oxford UP, 1996), ch. 37 to 38
- Nicholas Rogers, *Halloween: From Pagan Ritual to Party Night* (Oxford UP, 2002)
- Steve Roud, *The English Year* (Penguin, 2006)

**Confidence:** High that the link is contested among historians.

**Safe narration:** "You'll hear that Halloween is a 2,000-year-old Celtic festival. Historians actually argue about that. The name
comes from a Christian holiday, All Hallows' Eve, and the line back to anything ancient is a lot messier than the internet says."

## 6. Texas, 1974 (reused from the Candy Scare Short)

**Verified fact.** On **31 Oct 1974**, **Timothy O'Bryan, 8**, died less than an hour after eating a Pixy Stix laced with **potassium
cyanide**. The family lived in **Deer Park, Texas**, and trick-or-treated in **Pasadena, Texas**, both suburbs of Houston. His father,
**Ronald Clark O'Bryan**, claimed a stranger at a dark house had handed out the sticks. He had poisoned them himself, for life insurance
on his children (about $100,000 in policies, while he was heavily in debt). He also gave poisoned sticks to his daughter and three other
children, none of whom ate them. He was convicted of capital murder on **3 June 1975** and executed by lethal injection on **31 Mar 1984**.
Joel Best lists the case as Pasadena, TX, 1974.

**Sources:** the same as `../../Candy_Scare/script/SCRIPT.md`:
- Wikipedia, "Ronald Clark O'Bryan", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Clark_O%27Bryan
- Snopes, "Poisoned Halloween candy", https://snopes.com/horrors/poison/halloween.asp (URL as used in the Candy Scare script)
- Joel Best, "Halloween Sadism: The Evidence" (rev. Nov 2025, deaths list), https://udspace.udel.edu/bitstreams/9443db0d-cf0e-4476-b12e-953645b876d7/download

**Confidence:** High.

**Safe narration:** keep the outline: "1974. Outside Houston. An eight-year-old boy dies from candy in his own Halloween bag. His father
blamed a stranger. The poison came from his father." "He did it for the insurance money" is safe to add.

## 7. The sociologist's tally (Joel Best), updated

**Verified fact.**
- Best and Horiuchi (1985) searched the NYT, LA Times, Chicago Tribune and Fresno Bee for 1958 to 1984, and found 76 reported incidents.
- Best's running document, **"Halloween Sadism: The Evidence"** (University of Delaware, revised each fall; current PDF created
  **5 Nov 2025**), tallies reported incidents in the US and Canada **by year from 1958 through 2025**. My sum of his table: **134 reported
  incidents in 68 years**. 1982 (the Tylenol year) peaks at 12, and 2025 has 8.
- He finds most reports are of a contaminated treat discovered with **no injury** (sharp objects like pins, needles and razor blades are
  typical), and many are hoaxes. His conclusion: "I have been unable to find a substantiated report of a child being killed or seriously
  injured by a contaminated treat picked up in the course of trick-or-treating."
- Five deaths were initially blamed on "Halloween sadism", and every one had another explanation: Kevin Totson (Detroit, 1970; heroin
  found at a relative's home), Timothy O'Bryan (1974, his father), plus 1978, 1990 and 2001 deaths from natural causes or illness.
- He repeated it on NPR, 31 Oct 2023: "I have data going back to 1958, and I have yet to find a report of a child that's been killed or
  seriously hurt..."
- His tally excludes internet-only and small local reports. That makes it a newspaper-based floor, not a census.

**Sources.**
- Joel Best, "Halloween Sadism: The Evidence", University of Delaware (PDF, rev. fall 2025),
  https://udspace.udel.edu/bitstreams/9443db0d-cf0e-4476-b12e-953645b876d7/download
- Best and Horiuchi, "The Razor Blade in the Apple", *Social Problems* 32:5 (1985), https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/1985-best.pdf
- NPR / WNYC, "Why the urban legend of contaminated Halloween candy won't disappear", 31 Oct 2023,
  https://www.wnyc.org/story/why-the-urban-legend-of-contaminated-halloween-candy-wont-disappear/

**Confidence:** High.

**Safe narration:** "One sociologist has tracked these reports every year since 1958. A hundred and thirty-some reports in almost seventy
years. Most of them are hoaxes, or a pin with no one hurt. And he has never found a child killed or seriously hurt by a stranger's
Halloween candy." Keep "stranger's" in. The one death was the father.

## 8. The wagon-on-the-roof photo

**Finding.** I could **not find** a dated, captioned, public-domain photograph of a Halloween wagon on a barn roof. I searched Wikimedia
Commons (no match), NPR/WAMC (its lead image is an unrelated 1911 Library of Congress horse-and-wagon photo), and web searches of
historical-society collections. The Library of Congress website blocked automated search, so check loc.gov by hand.

What does exist:
- **"Halloween Prank, 1955"**, Cleveland State University, Michael Schwartz Library Special Collections, via Cleveland Historical:
  https://clevelandhistorical.org/files/show/6098. The caption says wagons "regularly found their way to the roofs of buildings on
  Halloween night". It does not give a place, photographer or rights statement, and I could not see the image to confirm it shows a
  wagon on a roof. CSU's Cleveland Press Collection images usually need permission and a credit line. **Not confirmed public domain.**
- **Text records of the prank (strong):** Beloit Academy, Wisconsin, 1888 (a loaded wagon hauled onto a college building; *Weekly
  Wisconsin*, 10 Nov 1888). Newton, Kansas, 1892 (a spring wagon on the roof of Fox Winne's lumberyard; *Daily Republican*, 1 Nov 1892).
  A farmer's wheat-loaded wagon was found on top of his barn (*Youth's Companion*, 1922). Source: Linton Weeks, "Halloweens Past: Wacky
  Pranks With Wagons", NPR History Dept., 30 Oct 2015,
  https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-history-dept/2015/10/30/453135085/halloweens-past-wacky-pranks-with-wagons
- Lethbridge Herald, 4 Nov 1927 (flag 1): Blackie residents hunting for "wagon wheels, gates, wagons" that "decorated the front street".
- Dreamstime "vintage wagon on the roof of a barn" photos are modern stock images, are not Halloween, and are licensed. Do not use them.

**Confidence:** Medium that no PD photo is easily available. High that the prank itself is well documented.

**Options (pick one before scripting):**
1. Ask CSU Special Collections for the 1955 image and its rights. If cleared, caption it on screen "Halloween prank, 1955"

2. Use a channel-style painted reconstruction (Gemini painting), and change the hook from "Look at this ... photo" to something true

3. Keep searching by hand: the LOC Prints & Photographs catalog and the Minnesota, Wisconsin and Iowa historical societies, with terms
   like "Halloween prank" and "wagon".

**Safe narration (with option 2):** "On Halloween night in 1892, in Newton, Kansas, somebody hauled a wagon onto the roof of the
lumberyard. That wasn't unusual. For decades, wagons kept turning up on roofs every November first."
Do not describe the image as "a photo" unless it is a real, dated photograph. Do not say how it got up there ("took it apart and
rebuilt it") unless the source for that image says so. The Champaign, Illinois, 1890s story (a wagon taken apart, carried up and
rebuilt in a third-floor room) supports that detail in general.

## 9. 1940s/50s critics calling trick-or-treating begging or extortion

**Verified fact (dated letters; quoted from transcriptions, originals not seen):**
- *The Fresno Bee*, 7 Nov 1941, p. 20, a letter signed "A. Mother": "I wish to register indignation at the 'trick or treat' racket ...
  This is pure and simple blackmail and it is a sad state of affairs when parents encourage their youngsters".
- *Washington Post*, 11 Nov 1948, p. 12, a letter from Mrs. B. G. McElwee: officials "should enact a law to prohibit 'beggars night' at
  Hallowe'en. It is making gangsters of children." She proposed fining parents at least $25 "for putting their children out to beg".
- *Washington Post*, 21 Nov 1948, "Ask Anne" column: "I never saw nor heard of the begging practice until about 1936."
- Also: *American Notes & Queries*, March 1942 (Decatur, Ill.): "a mild kind of blackmail" (Wordorigins). Before the war there was
  the *Spokane Daily Chronicle*, 1935 ("petty 'blackmail'") and the *Bay City Daily Times*, 1928 ("the gentle art of blackmail").
  Historian Samira Kawash notes that 1930s press used gangster language ("shakedown", "goon squads").

**Sources.**
- Transcriptions: Lincoln Discussion Symposium forum thread (secondary; cites paper, date and page),
  https://rogerjnorton.com/LincolnDiscussionSymposium/printthread.php?tid=2005. **Before broadcast, pull the originals from
  Newspapers.com (Fresno Bee) and ProQuest Historical (Washington Post).**
- Wordorigins.org (1942 *American Notes & Queries*), link under flag 1
- Barry Popik (1928, 1935), link under flag 1
- Mental Floss, "How Gangsters and the Media Helped Make Trick or Treating a Halloween Tradition" (Kawash),
  https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/630095/gangsters-media-trick-or-treating-halloween

**Confidence:** Medium-High. The quotes are detailed with page numbers and fit the 1930s pattern, but the originals are unseen.

**Safe narration:** keep the ch 7 line. "Not everyone liked the deal. In 1948, a letter to the Washington Post said trick-or-treating
was 'making gangsters of children', and asked the city to fine parents who sent their kids out to beg."

## 10. Current participation figure (cold open)

**Verified fact.** NRF's 2026 Halloween survey, released **22 Sep 2026**: **74% of consumers plan to celebrate** (73% in 2025).
**61% plan to hand out candy**. Record total spending is expected at **$13.5 billion** ($115.14 per person). Candy is the top purchase:
96% of celebrants plan to buy it, worth $4.1 billion. The survey (Prosper Insights & Analytics) asked 7,889 adults from 1 to 9 Sep 2026,
with a margin of ±1.1 points. **NRF reports no count of trick-or-treaters**, and no figure for "taking children trick-or-treating"
appears in the 2026 release.

**Sources.**
- NRF press release, "NRF Halloween survey shows consumer spending expected to reach $13.5 billion", 22 Sep 2026,
  https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/nrf-halloween-survey-shows-consumer-spending-expected-to-reach-13-5-billion
- NRF Halloween data hub, https://nrf.com/research-insights/holiday-data-and-trends/halloween

**Confidence:** High for the NRF figures.

**Safe narration (ch 1):** "This Saturday, about six in ten American adults will stand at the door handing out candy. That's around
four billion dollars of it." Halloween 2026 is a Saturday, so "this Saturday" is correct when published this week. Change it if the
video slips to an evergreen release. "Millions of kids" is safe but unsourced; don't attach a specific number to it.
