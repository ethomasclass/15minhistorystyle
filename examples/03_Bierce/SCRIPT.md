## 0:00 | An Unknown Destination

December 26th, 1913. Chihuahua, Mexico.

A 71-year-old American sits down to write a letter. He has asthma. He has a scar on his skull from a bullet he took fifty years ago. And for the last month, he's been riding with a revolutionary army.

He finishes with one line: "As to me, I leave here tomorrow for an unknown destination."

And then... nothing. No more letters. No body. No grave anyone can prove. He just walks off the map.

His name was Ambrose Bierce. And vanishing into a war zone at 71? That's not even the strangest thing about him. Before that, this man was a soldier. A mapmaker. A humor writer in London. A gold-mine manager in the Wild West. The most feared newspaper columnist in America. A horror writer people still read in English class. And the guy who told one of the richest men in America exactly what his bribe was worth.

Most people get one life. Maybe two. Bierce got about seven.

So here's the question I want to answer today: How does one man live that many lives?

And the answer starts with a kid covered in ink.

## 1:12 | Thirteen A's

Ambrose Bierce was born in 1842, in a log cabin in Meigs County, Ohio. He was number ten of thirteen kids. And his parents gave every single one of them a name that started with A. Abigail. Amelia. Ann. Addison. Aurelius. You get the idea.

The family was poor, but they read constantly. And at 15, Ambrose left home to work as a "printer's devil" at an anti-slavery newspaper in Indiana.

Don't worry, it's not as spooky as it sounds. A printer's devil was the lowest kid on the newspaper totem pole. Think unpaid intern, except your job is hauling heavy metal type and getting ink all over yourself.

But notice this. Before he's old enough to shave, this kid is already working at a newspaper. Hold that thought.

## 1:54 | Broke Like a Walnut

Then, 1861. The Civil War starts, and Bierce, just 18, joins the Union army's 9th Indiana Infantry. That first summer, he makes the newspapers for running out under fire to drag a badly wounded soldier to safety.

The next spring, he's at Shiloh. Two days of fighting in Tennessee, and more than 23,000 men killed, wounded or missing. That's more than the Revolution, the War of 1812 and the Mexican War put together.

Most old soldiers told stories about flags and bravery. When Bierce wrote about Shiloh, he told you about the dead.

He was good at soldiering, though, and he got promoted to lieutenant. His new job: topographical engineer for General William Hazen.

That sounds fancy. It means mapmaker. Picture being the human version of Google Maps, except you're sketching the hills and roads where a battle is about to happen. And sometimes while it's happening.

Then, June 23rd, 1864, in the fighting around Kennesaw Mountain in Georgia, a Confederate sharpshooter shoots him in the head.

The bullet cracks his skull and lodges behind his left ear. He's shipped to a hospital on an open railroad flatcar. Two days. No roof. Bierce later said his head "broke like a walnut."

And he survives. He's even back on duty that fall. But the headaches and fainting spells don't stop, and in January 1865, he resigns.

So that's life number one, done. He is 22 years old.

## 3:10 | Go West, Young Grump

But the army isn't quite done with him. In 1866, General Hazen invites him along to inspect army forts across the Great Plains. Horseback and wagon, Omaha to San Francisco. When they get there, Bierce quits the army for good, and just... stays.

San Francisco is a Gold Rush boomtown, packed with miners, gamblers, con men, and a whole lot of newspapers. And Bierce figures out his real talent: roasting people. Politicians. Preachers. Poets. Anybody.

In 1872, he moves to England for about three years and writes for a London humor magazine called Fun. His first books come out under a fake name: Dod Grile.

Pen names were basically the 1800s version of a burner account. You could say savage things and keep a little distance.

Back in America, he tries something totally different. Around 1880, he heads out to the Dakota Territory, to Deadwood. Yes, that Wild West Deadwood. His job: managing a gold-mining company.

The company fails. That life lasts about a year. Back to the newspapers.

## 4:05 | Bitter Bierce

This is where Bierce becomes BIERCE. In 1881, he takes over a San Francisco magazine called The Wasp. Which is the perfect name for anything he's involved in. A few years later, a young newspaper owner named William Randolph Hearst hires him to write a column for the San Francisco Examiner.

Back then, there's no radio, no TV, no internet. Newspapers were the internet. And Bierce was Hearst's star attack dog. People called him "Bitter Bierce." Think of the most brutal critic online today, then hand him the biggest megaphone in the West. Everybody read him. A lot of people were scared of him.

His columns became his most famous book: a fake dictionary called The Devil's Dictionary. And since this is a history channel, I have to share this one:

"History: an account mostly false, of events mostly unimportant, which are brought about by rulers mostly knaves, and soldiers mostly fools."

Okay. Rude. But he was a soldier, so he'd know.

And in his spare time? He writes war stories and horror stories. The most famous one is "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge." Spoiler warning. During the Civil War, a man is about to be hanged from a railroad bridge. The rope snaps. He falls into the creek, dodges the bullets, and runs all day and all night to get home to his wife. He reaches out to hug her...

And he's dead. The rope never snapped. The whole escape happened in his head, in the second before he died.

It later aired as an episode of The Twilight Zone, and Kurt Vonnegut called it the greatest American short story. Notice what it's about: that blurry line between being here and being gone. Bierce kept coming back to it his whole life.

## 5:41 | Name Your Price

But my favorite Bierce moment is 1896.

Here's the setup. The government had loaned the railroads a fortune to build the transcontinental railroad. Now railroad boss Collis Huntington, one of the richest men in America, was pushing a bill through Congress to basically let them off the hook for paying it back.

So Hearst sends Bierce to Washington to kill it.

And one day, on the steps of the Capitol, Huntington walks up to Bierce and tells him to name his price. Everybody has a price, right?

Bierce did. His price was 130 million dollars. The exact amount the railroads owed. And, he said, "If, when you are ready to pay, I happen to be out of town, you may hand it over to my friend, the Treasurer of the United States."

That line ran in newspapers all over the country. People were furious, and the bill died.

So: soldier, mapmaker, failed mine manager, and now the reporter who beat a railroad tycoon.

## 6:33 | Not a Highlight Reel

But this isn't just a highlight reel. Bierce's personal life was hard.

He married Mollie Day in 1871, and they had three kids. In 1888 they separated, and years later they divorced.

Both of his sons died young. His oldest, Day, died in a violent tragedy in 1889, when he was still a teenager. His younger son, Leigh, died in 1901 of pneumonia, made worse by his drinking.

And Bierce himself had asthma his whole life, and he never fully escaped that head wound.

By 1913, he's living in Washington, D.C. He's famous. He's just finished putting together a twelve-volume set of everything he ever wrote. He's 71 years old. By any normal standard, he's done. He's earned the right to sit on a porch.

Ambrose Bierce does not want to sit on a porch.

## 7:24 | One Last War

In October 1913, he leaves Washington on a farewell tour of the Civil War battlefields where he fought. Then he just keeps going. New Orleans. Texas. El Paso. Right up to the Mexican border.

And across that border, there's a war.

Quick background. For more than thirty years, Mexico had been ruled by a dictator, Porfirio Díaz. In 1910, Mexicans rose up and threw him out. That's the Mexican Revolution. But in 1913, a general named Victoriano Huerta seized power, and the elected president was murdered. So now the revolution is fighting Huerta.

One of the most famous rebel generals is Pancho Villa. A former outlaw turned general, moving a huge army across northern Mexico by train. American newspapers were obsessed with him.

So Bierce, age 71, crosses the bridge into Ciudad Juárez and gets papers to travel with Villa's army as an observer.

In late November, he's there for the Battle of Tierra Blanca, a big win for Villa. One account even says Bierce grabbed a rifle, shot an enemy soldier, and was given a sombrero for his aim. That story is hard to confirm. But honestly? It's not out of character.

He rides with the army to Chihuahua City. And he writes home like a man who knows exactly what he's doing:

"If you hear of my being stood up against a Mexican stone wall and shot to rags please know that I think that a pretty good way to depart this life. It beats old age, disease, or falling down the cellar stairs."

That's Bierce. Even his goodbye is a punchline.

## 8:50 | Walking Off the Map

Which brings us back to December 26th, 1913. The letter. The "unknown destination." And then, silence.

U.S. officials asked around, even among Villa's own men, and the stories didn't match. So for over a hundred years, people have been guessing.

Maybe he died in the fighting at Ojinaga, in January 1914.

Maybe he was shot by a firing squad in a desert town called Sierra Mojada. A priest there collected stories from the town's old-timers, and in 2004 he put up a gravestone. It says "very trustworthy witnesses suppose" that Bierce is buried there. Suppose. Not exactly proof.

Or maybe he never went deep into Mexico at all. The investigator Joe Nickell points out that the original of that last letter has never been found. We only have a copy in his secretary's notebook. Nickell thinks Bierce may have gone to the Grand Canyon instead, to quietly end his own life.

And yes, some people online say aliens took him. That comes from a 1955 sci-fi story. Fun story. Not evidence.

The truth is, nobody knows.

So, back to our question. How does one man live that many lives?

Look at them again. The printer's devil. The mapmaker. The columnist. The war correspondent. Remember that kid covered in ink? It's the same job every time. Go where the trouble is. Look at it hard. Write down what you see.

The jobs kept changing because Bierce kept leaving. Every time one life ended, he didn't settle down. He went looking for the next place trouble was. Even at 71. He told us himself: a stone wall beats old age.

He spent his whole life writing about that blurry line between being here and being gone. And then his own last chapter just... never got written.

He said he was leaving for an unknown destination.

And as far as anyone can tell, he got there.

## Fact-check flags

- **Stone-wall letter:** wording varies a little between sources; this follows TIME's version. The recipient is uncertain, so the script doesn't name them.
- **Sombrero story:** single source (attributed to Friedrich Katz in The Paris Review), so the narration hedges it.
- **Huntington bill:** the refunding bill would have stretched repayment out over decades rather than cancel it outright; "basically let them off the hook" is a fair plain-language version, but tighten it if you want to be exact.
- **Divorce:** most sources say 1904 (a few say 1905), so the script says "years later."
