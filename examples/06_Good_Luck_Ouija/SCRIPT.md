# Good Luck: How the Ouija Board Went from Romantic to Scary

**15 Minute History** · narration script · 1,600 words · about 9:05 of narration (about 9:45 finished, with the intro, breaks and a 15-second end screen)

**Driving question (asked in the cold open, answered in the last chapter):** How did a game for sweethearts become the object people are afraid to touch?
**Answer:** the board never changed; the story around it did. Every generation put its biggest feeling into the pointer (love, then grief, then fun, then fear), and the ideomotor effect means the pointer goes where the players' own hands take it. *The Exorcist* didn't invent the fear, it gave everyone the same story at once, and the toy company eventually sold that story too.

## 0:00 | Two Pictures

May 1st, 1920. The cover of The Saturday Evening Post, one of the most-read magazines in America.

Norman Rockwell paints a young couple sitting face to face. Knees touching. A board across both their laps, their fingertips sharing one little wooden pointer. It's sweet. A little flirty. Basically, it's a date.

Now jump ahead fifty-three years. December 1973. In movie theaters across America, a 12-year-old girl finds the same kind of board in her basement. She makes a new friend on it. She calls him Captain Howdy. And by the end of the movie, two priests are fighting for her soul.

Same board. Same alphabet. Same YES and NO in the corners. In 1920, it's what you bring on a date. Today, plenty of people won't keep one in the house.

So here's the question I want to answer today: How did a game for sweethearts become the object people are afraid to touch?

One warning. This wasn't a light switch. For most of its life, people used the Ouija board for love, fun, grief and fear, all at the same time. But the story around it changed. And we're going to find out who changed it.

It starts with some knocking.

## 1:15 | Knock Once for Yes

If you watched our Reform Era video, you've met the Fox sisters. 1848, upstate New York. Two girls say the knocks in their house answer questions. Once for yes. Silence for no.

That sparks Spiritualism, the belief that the living can talk to the dead. And after the Civil War, millions of grieving families want very badly to believe it.

But knocking is slow. Someone calls out the alphabet, A, B, C, and waits for a knock on the right letter. It's like texting, if you had to scroll through the whole alphabet for every letter.

Then, in 1886, newspapers across the country report a shortcut. Spiritualists in Ohio have a "talking board," with the alphabet printed right on it. Nobody has to call out letters anymore. The spirits can just point.

And a few businessmen in Baltimore think: we could sell this.

## 2:01 | Good Luck

1890. Baltimore. A businessman named Charles Kennard and a lawyer named Elijah Bond start the Kennard Novelty Company. Novelty. As in, a toy.

They need a name. The story goes that Bond's sister-in-law, Helen Peters, said to be a strong medium, asked the board itself. The pointer spelled O-U-I-J-A. And it said the word meant "good luck."

Helen, it turns out, was wearing a locket that night, with a picture of a woman and the name "Ouija" next to it. Probably the popular novelist Ouida. So the spirits might have just... read her necklace.

Then came the company's favorite legend. The patent office won't approve the board unless it works. If it can spell the examiner's name, which they don't know, they get their patent. The pointer spells his name. Patent granted, February 10th, 1891.

Did that really happen? We only have the inventors' word. But here's what we know for sure. The patent doesn't say one word about spirits. It's filed as a "Toy or Game."

And the first ads promise both. The Ouija answers questions "about the past, present and future with marvelous accuracy." It's also "a never-failing amusement." Price: a dollar forty-nine.

Hold that thought. Toy, or spirit? Even the people selling it wouldn't say.

Soon a young employee named William Fuld takes over and makes it his own. When a reporter asked if he believed in it, Fuld said: "I should say not. I'm no spiritualist. I'm a Presbyterian."

Quick thing before we go on. If you're enjoying this, the board would like you to subscribe. See? It's spelling it out. Okay, fine. That's my hand on the pointer. But it really does help the channel.

## 3:32 | Knees Touching

So who was buying it? A lot of young couples.

Dating around 1900 came with rules. A young man and woman couldn't just be alone together. Parents watched. Neighbors talked.

Now look at how you play Ouija. Two people, facing each other, the board across both your laps. Knees touching. Lights low. Fingertips on the same little pointer. Then you ask questions. Does he like me? Will we get married?

It's a parlor game with a built-in excuse to touch. And if the board says yes... who's going to argue with the spirits?

By 1920, America is in a full Ouija craze. The summer before, Rockwell had watched couples at a dance hall in Potsdam, New York, sitting knee to knee over Ouija boards. That's where his cover came from.

## 4:14 | Patience Worth

But at the same time, other people were taking it dead seriously.

July 8th, 1913. St. Louis. A housewife named Pearl Curran sits at a Ouija board with a friend, and the pointer spells out:

"Many moons ago I lived. Again I come. Patience Worth my name."

Patience said she was an Englishwoman from the 1600s. Over the next twenty-four years, Pearl produced around four million words in her name. Novels, plays, thousands of poems. Most researchers think Patience came from Pearl's own mind. But to her readers, it looked like proof.

So the same board that's a flirting game in one parlor is, in St. Louis, a doorway to a voice from three hundred years ago.

## 4:52 | Empty Chairs

And then the world gave millions of people a reason to need that doorway.

World War One kills more than a hundred and sixteen thousand Americans. Then the 1918 flu kills around six hundred seventy-five thousand more. Most of the dead are young. They leave behind parents. Wives. Sweethearts.

So, just like after the Civil War, Spiritualism comes roaring back. Even Arthur Conan Doyle, the man who invented Sherlock Holmes, the most logical detective in fiction, spends his last years preaching that the dead are still with us.

And Ouija boards sell like never before. Mothers and wives buy them to reach sons and husbands who aren't coming home.

So picture one house around 1920. In the parlor, a teenager and her date, knees touching, giggling over the board. Upstairs, her mother, with the same kind of board, trying to reach the son she lost in France.

Same object. Two completely different stories.

## 5:48 | Next to Monopoly

In 1966, the Fuld family sold the Ouija board to Parker Brothers. The Monopoly company. Headquartered in Salem, Massachusetts. Yes, that Salem. I could not make that up.

Now it sits on toy-store shelves next to Monopoly and Clue. In 1967, it outsold Monopoly.

But look at who's playing now. Kids at sleepovers. Lights off, flashlight on, whispering, "Is anybody there?" They're not trying to reach Grandpa. They're trying to get scared. The spooky part isn't a side effect anymore. It's the point.

Which made it the perfect setup for what came next.

## 6:18 | Captain Howdy

In 1949, priests performed an exorcism on a teenage boy from just outside Washington, D.C. According to some accounts, it all started after his aunt, a Spiritualist, taught him to use a Ouija board. A Georgetown student named William Peter Blatty read about it in the paper, and never forgot it.

In December 1973, his story becomes a movie: The Exorcist. Regan finds a Ouija board in the basement and plays with it alone. "I make the questions," she tells her mom, "and he does the answers."

People lined up around the block. It became one of the biggest movies ever made. The Ouija historian Robert Murch compares it to Psycho: "No one was afraid of showers until that scene."

The Exorcist didn't invent the fear. Some religious leaders had warned against talking boards for decades. What the movie did was hand that fear to everyone at once. One story. And a villain.

But here's the twist. Who kept selling that fear? In 2014, a horror movie called Ouija hit theaters. One of the companies behind it was Hasbro, which owns Parker Brothers. It made over a hundred million dollars. The toy company made a horror movie about its own toy.

## 7:23 | Who Moves It?

So, back to our question. How did a game for sweethearts become the object people are afraid to touch?

Put a board from 1891 next to one you could buy today. Same alphabet. Same numbers. YES and NO in the corners. GOOD BYE at the bottom. The board barely changed.

The story did. And it depended on whose hands were on the pointer.

Remember: toy, or spirit? Who's actually moving it?

Scientists had an answer before the Ouija board even existed. In 1853, Michael Faraday tested the Spiritualists' tipping tables, and showed the people around them were pushing, without realizing it. It's called the ideomotor effect: your muscles move with an idea, before you decide to move them.

And in 2012, researchers in Canada blindfolded people at a Ouija board, then had their partner quietly let go. When players thought they were just guessing at a yes-or-no question, their spoken answers were right half the time. Pure chance. Through the board, sixty-five percent. The board knew more than they thought they knew. Because the board was them.

So who turned the Ouija board from romantic to scary? Not one person. Not even one movie. We did. Couples put love into that pointer. Grieving mothers put in grief. Kids put in fun, and then fear. And it went right where our hands took it.

That's what people do when something seems to move on its own. We reach for whatever explanation our time hands us. In 1920, romance. In 1918, a son still out there somewhere. After 1973, a demon.

Helen Peters said the board told her its name meant "good luck."

Turns out, that was up to whoever was holding it.

## Fact-check flags

- **Patent-office demonstration:** a Kennard/Bond family story, passed down through Ouija historian Robert Murch. No independent record. The narration hedges it ("the company's favorite legend," "we only have the inventors' word").
- **Helen Peters and the locket:** also from the family account via Murch (Smithsonian). Ouida as the source of the name is likely, not proven ("probably").
- **Fuld quote:** "I should say not. I'm no spiritualist. I'm a Presbyterian." Wording is consistent across sources; the original interview date wasn't confirmed.
- **Patience Worth's output:** "around four million words" follows American Heritage; other sources describe seven novels plus plays and thousands of poems.
- **1949 case:** location is given variously as Cottage City or Mount Rainier, Md., and the boy's age as 13 or 14, so the narration says "a teenage boy from just outside Washington." The aunt-and-Ouija origin is hedged with "according to some accounts."
- **Rockwell cover:** the narration only describes what's certain (knees touching, board on both laps, shared pointer). Check the painting before adding anything about who's looking where.
- **"Two priests":** the film's exorcism is performed by Fathers Merrin and Karras.
- **1918 flu:** ~675,000 U.S. deaths is the CDC figure. WWI: ~116,000 U.S. military deaths.
- **Conan Doyle** had publicly embraced Spiritualism in 1916, before his son Kingsley died in 1918, so the narration doesn't say a loss *caused* his belief.
- **"In 1967, it outsold Monopoly":** Smithsonian: two million boards sold in 1967, "outperforming Monopoly."
- **Ouija (2014):** box office ~$103.6 million (Hasbro Studios, Blumhouse, Platinum Dunes).

- **The 1891 ad's price:** the ad shown on screen (Danziger's, Pittsburg Dispatch, 1891) says "PRICE, $1 49," so the narration says "a dollar forty-nine." (Smithsonian quotes $1.50 from a different early ad.)
