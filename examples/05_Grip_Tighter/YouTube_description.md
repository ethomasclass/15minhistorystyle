Why did the American South, at the very moment it could have started letting slavery go, hold on tighter than ever? In 1831, after Nat Turner's rebellion, Virginia's lawmakers openly debated ending slavery. They voted no, and then they made it harsher. It starts with the governor's $500 reward notice for "a man named Nat," the closest thing we have to a portrait of Nat Turner.

This is slavery in the Old South explained in 15 minutes: the 1808 ban on the overseas slave trade, Eli Whitney's cotton gin, the Cotton Kingdom and Indian Removal, the domestic slave trade and the Second Middle Passage, the Southern social pyramid of planters, yeomen and poor whites, daily life under slavery, slave law, the ways enslaved people resisted and built lives of their own, Nat Turner's rebellion, and the Virginia slavery debate of 1832. Told with original documents, from the governor's proclamation to the words of Solomon Northup, Harriet Jacobs and Harriett Hill.
