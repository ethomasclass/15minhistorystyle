# Grip Tighter: Slavery and the Cotton South

**15 Minute History** · narration script · 2,760 words · about 17:26

**Driving question (asked in the cold open, answered in the last chapter):** Why did the South, at the very moment it could have started letting slavery go, hold on tighter than ever?
**Answer:** cotton. The gin turned a system many expected to fade into the engine of America's most valuable export, so enslaved people became the South's biggest store of wealth. Slavery also propped up the status of white Southerners who owned no one. When Nat Turner showed the system could be fought, the South chose to tighten its grip instead of loosening it.

## 0:00 | The Proclamation

September 17th, 1831. Richmond, Virginia.

The governor signs a notice. A reward: 500 dollars, for a man named Nat.

On the second page, someone has written down what he looks like. Between 30 and 35 years old. Five feet six or eight inches high. Broad shoulders. Large eyes. Walks brisk and active.

It's the closest thing we have to a portrait of Nat Turner. No painting of him exists. No photograph. Just this.

A month earlier, Turner, an enslaved preacher, had led a revolt in Southampton County. Between 55 and 60 white people were killed. Then white mobs and militias killed Black people across the region, many of whom had nothing to do with it.

And then Virginia did something nobody expected. Its lawmakers openly debated ending slavery.

They voted no. And then they made it harsher.

So here's the question I want to answer today: Why did the South, at the very moment it could have started letting slavery go, hold on tighter than ever?

And the answer starts with a seed.

## 1:33 | Supposed to Die

To see how we got there, rewind about forty years.

It's 1790, and the first United States census counts about 700,000 enslaved people. Most of them live in Virginia, Maryland and the Carolinas, growing tobacco and rice.

And tobacco is in trouble. It's brutal on the soil. After a few seasons, a field is worn out. A lot of Virginia planters are switching to wheat, which needs far fewer workers.

So plenty of people at the time thought slavery might slowly fade away on its own. Leaders like Thomas Jefferson and James Madison wrote that slavery was wrong. Both of them also kept enslaving more than a hundred people at a time, for the rest of their lives.

The Constitution even set a date. Starting in 1808, Congress was allowed to ban bringing enslaved people in from overseas. And on January 1st, 1808, the ban took effect. Cut off the supply, the thinking went, and slavery would slowly starve.

Hold that thought. Because that is not what happened.

## 2:34 | Fifty Pounds a Day

Meanwhile, Britain is building the first big factories in the world, full of machines that spin and weave cloth faster than anyone had ever imagined. And those machines are hungry for one thing: cotton.

And across the inland South, planters can grow a tough kind of cotton almost anywhere. There's just one problem. It's packed with sticky green seeds. Picking them out by hand, one worker might clean about a pound of cotton a day. At that speed, cotton could never be a big business.

Then, in 1793, a young Yale graduate named Eli Whitney, staying on a plantation in Georgia, builds a simple machine. You turn a crank, and a drum covered in wire teeth pulls the cotton fibers through slots too narrow for the seeds. It's called a cotton engine. {Gin}, for short.

With a gin, one worker can clean around fifty pounds a day.

Now, you'd think a machine that saves labor would mean fewer enslaved workers. *It did the exact opposite.* Cleaning got easy, so planters wanted to grow as much cotton as they possibly could. And somebody still had to plant it, hoe it, and pick every single boll by hand.

Cotton also wore out the soil, so planters were always hungry for new land. And they got it. The Louisiana Purchase opened the Mississippi Valley. And after the {Indian Removal Act} of 1830, the United States forced the Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole nations off their homelands in the Southeast. Those homelands became cotton plantations.

By 1860, about three out of every four pounds of cotton in Britain's mills came from the American South, and cotton was more than half of everything the United States sold overseas. It was the oil of the 1800s.

And it wasn't just a Southern business. Northern banks loaned the money. Northern ships carried the bales. Northern mills spun the thread.

## 4:28 | Sold South

So, remember 1808? The ban that was supposed to starve slavery? It actually made the people already enslaved in America *more valuable*.

Virginia and Maryland planters had more enslaved workers than their tired tobacco fields needed. Cotton planters in Alabama, Mississippi and Louisiana couldn't get enough.

So the Upper South started selling people to the Deep South. This is called the {domestic slave trade}. Between 1790 and 1860, roughly a million enslaved people were forced to move south and west, most of them sold. Some historians call it the {Second Middle Passage}.

Traders bought people in cities like Richmond, then marched them hundreds of miles, chained together in long lines called {coffles}. Or they packed them onto ships bound for New Orleans, which became the biggest slave market in the country.

Think about what that meant for a family. The law didn't recognize enslaved people's marriages, so nothing stopped an owner from selling a husband, a wife, or a child. Any day could be the last day you saw them.

Harriett Hill, interviewed in the 1930s, remembered being sold away from her mother in Georgia when she was three years old. She said it was like selling a calf away from a cow. "We are human beings," she said.

To the people who owned them, enslaved people weren't only workers. They were money. Planters borrowed against them, the way people borrow against a house today. When the price of cotton went up, the price of human beings went up with it.

By 1830, the census counted more than two million enslaved people. The institution that was supposed to die had nearly tripled.

## 6:04 | The Pyramid

So who ran this world? Start with who lived in it. In 1860, about *one out of every three* people in the slave states was enslaved.

Now picture the white South as a pyramid.

At the very top are the {planters}, usually defined as people who enslaved twenty or more. They're a tiny slice of the population. But they hold a huge share of the land, a huge share of the enslaved people, and far more than their share of political power. A handful enslaved more than a thousand people and built grand columned mansions.

Planters liked to describe themselves as fathers looking after one big family, Black and white. Historians call this {paternalism}. It was a story that made owning people feel acceptable. Their wives, the plantation mistresses, ran the household, directing the enslaved women who did most of the work.

Below them are the {yeomen}: small farmers who worked their own land with their own families. Most of them owned no one. Some hoped to buy an enslaved worker someday. Many never did.

And at the bottom are poor whites, who didn't even own land. They rented, or worked for wages, and lived on the edge.

Now here's the number that should surprise you. In 1860, about *three out of every four* white families in the slave states owned no enslaved people at all.

So here's the puzzle. Why would most white Southerners defend a system they didn't own a piece of?

Some hoped to climb the pyramid. Cotton money ran through every store and bank in the South. But for many, the biggest reason was status. However poor a white Southerner was, slavery guaranteed there was always someone below him. Being white meant being free. To a lot of people, that promise was worth more than money.

And after Southampton, there was one more reason. Fear.

## 7:52 | Sunup to Sundown

So what was life like for the people the whole pyramid stood on?

On a cotton plantation, most enslaved people worked in the fields, in groups called {gangs}, from sunup to sundown. At harvest, even longer.

At the end of every day, each person's cotton was weighed. Fall short, and you could be whipped. Hit your number, and your number might go up.

Women worked the same hours as men. Pregnant women were sent to the fields until close to giving birth, then sent back within weeks, bringing their babies to the field or leaving them with an older woman. Children started working at five or six years old: carrying water, pulling weeds, chasing birds away from the crops.

Food was a weekly ration, mostly cornmeal and a little pork. Clothing was a couple of rough outfits a year and one pair of shoes. Families slept in small wooden cabins, often with dirt floors, sometimes two families to a cabin, on beds of straw and rags.

Some enslaved people worked in the owner's house instead. They often got better food and hand-me-down clothes. But better is a relative word. Working in the house meant living under the owner's eye, day and night. As a boy, Frederick Douglass started learning the alphabet in a house in Baltimore, until the man of the house found out and stopped it. Reading, the man said, would make a boy unfit to be a slave. Douglass decided that was exactly why he had to learn.

And for an enslaved woman, being close to the owner could be the most dangerous place of all.

## 9:18 | No Law Above Him

Under Southern law, an enslaved person was property. They couldn't testify in court against a white person. They couldn't legally defend themselves against their owner.

In 1829, a North Carolina judge named Thomas Ruffin summed it up in one sentence: "The power of the master must be absolute, to render the submission of the slave perfect."

Absolute power. And the main tool of that power was violence. On big plantations, overseers were judged by how big a crop they could squeeze out, and the whip was how they did it. Some owners went further, with branding, iron collars, and worse.

For enslaved women, there was another kind of violence. Owners had near-total control over their bodies, and many used it to rape them. Under the law, it wasn't even a crime.

In 1850, a Missouri farmer named Robert Newsom bought a girl named Celia. She was 14. For the next five years, he raped her again and again. She had children.

In 1855, pregnant again, Celia told him to stop. He didn't. So the next time he came to her cabin, she hit him with a heavy stick, and he died.

At her trial, her lawyers made a simple argument. Missouri law made it a crime to force "any woman" into sex, and a person could use deadly force to stop a crime like that. The judge refused to let the jury consider it. In the eyes of the court, Celia wasn't a woman defending herself. She was property that had turned on its owner.

Celia was hanged in December 1855. She was about 19 years old.

## 11:01 | A World Outside Work

So far, it might sound like enslaved people only had things done to them. But that's not the whole story. Not even close.

Under all that control, enslaved people built lives of their own.

The law didn't recognize their marriages, so they married anyway. They named their children after grandparents, aunts and uncles, so family ties would survive even if people were sold apart. One historian describes what family gave enslaved people as "a world outside of the world of work." A place where a man could be a father and a woman could be a mother, not just somebody's property.

They built a faith of their own, too. White preachers on plantations loved one Bible verse in particular: "Servants, be obedient to them that are your masters." But enslaved Christians heard a different story in the Bible. Moses. Pharaoh. A God who sets his people free. They sang it: "Go down, Moses, way down in Egypt land. Tell old Pharaoh, let my people go." At night, some slipped off to secret places in the woods called {hush harbors}, and turned a big iron pot upside down to muffle the sound of their prayers.

Resistance came in everyday forms, too. Working slowly. Breaking tools. Faking sickness. Running away, for a few days or for good. Men ran more often. Women usually had children they couldn't leave behind, so many of them resisted in ways that kept them close.

Harriet Jacobs found a way to do both. In 1835, to escape the man who controlled her in North Carolina, she ran. But not far. She hid in a crawl space in her grandmother's attic, three feet high at its tallest, for almost seven years. Through a tiny hole, she watched her children play outside. In 1842, she finally escaped north, and later she told her story in a book, Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl.

And very rarely, resistance became open rebellion. Most plots were betrayed before they began, and dozens of the accused were hanged.

Nat Turner's was the deadliest one that actually happened.

## 13:03 | Southampton

So let's go back to Southampton County.

Nat Turner was born there in 1800. Even as a kid, the people around him thought he was special. He learned to read so young he couldn't remember learning. As an adult, he preached to other enslaved people, and some of them called him the Prophet.

He also had visions. He described "white spirits and black spirits engaged in battle," the sun going dark, and blood flowing in streams. He came to believe God had chosen him to fight slavery. So in February 1831, when the moon slid in front of the sun in the middle of the day and the sky went strange and dim, he took it as the signal. When the sun turned a strange bluish-green that August, he took that as the second.

On August 21st, he met his followers in the woods. That night, they began. They killed nearly every white person they found, including women and children. Then came the revenge, and the killing of Black people who'd had nothing to do with it.

Turner hid in the woods for more than two months. On October 30th, a farmer found him.

In jail, a white lawyer named Thomas Gray interviewed him and published The Confessions of Nat Turner. We have to read it carefully, because Gray chose what to print. But in it, Turner doesn't apologize. When Gray asked if he now saw he'd been mistaken, Turner answered with a question: "Was not Christ crucified?"

Nat Turner was hanged on November 11th, 1831. Virginia hanged others too, and sold still others out of the state.

He fought a system that was violent every single day. His rebellion also killed children. Historians still wrestle with how to hold both of those facts at once. You probably will too.

## 14:55 | Grip Tighter

Now the South had to decide what Southampton meant. Most white Southerners blamed outsiders: Northern abolitionist newspapers, pamphlets smuggled in on ships.

But in Virginia, some lawmakers said what others wouldn't. Maybe the problem wasn't pamphlets. Maybe it was slavery. In January 1832, Virginia's lawmakers spent two weeks debating it. Thomas Jefferson's own grandson proposed {gradual emancipation}, a plan to end slavery in Virginia slowly, over many years.

The closest vote, on whether to even start writing a law to end slavery, failed. 73 to 58.

And then the South went the other way. Virginia banned Black preachers from holding meetings. States across the South tightened their {slave codes}, the laws that controlled enslaved people's lives: curfews, passes to travel, bans on gatherings, and more laws against teaching enslaved people to read. A few years later, South Carolina's John C. Calhoun stood up in the United States Senate and called slavery not a necessary evil, but "a positive good."

So, back to our question. Why did the South, at the very moment it could have started letting slavery go, hold on tighter than ever?

Go back to that seed. In 1790, slavery looked like it might fade. Then the cotton gin made enslaved people the engine of America's most valuable export. By 1860, the four million people enslaved in America were counted as property worth *more than all the country's railroads and factories combined*. Letting go meant giving up the biggest pile of wealth in the country.

And it wasn't only money. Slavery held up the whole pyramid. It told even the poorest white farmer who he was.

So when Nat Turner showed that the system could be fought, the South had a choice. Loosen its grip, or tighten it. *It tightened.* More laws. More patrols. More fear.

In February 1831, Nat Turner looked up at a darkened sky and saw a sign. That August, the rest of the South saw one too. They just read it differently.

And the tighter the South held on, the closer the whole country came to tearing apart.

## Fact-check flags

Every claim was verified chapter by chapter; the full table, with evidence and links, is in **`FACT_CHECK.md`**. The flags a teacher is most likely to meet:

- **Death toll:** Gray lists 55 white victims; the Library of Virginia says 57; others say "about 60." The script says "between 55 and 60."
- **The eclipse** was annular, but Southampton was just outside the ring path (about 96% covered). Never say "ring" or "total."
- **73–58** was the vote on whether it was "expedient" to legislate abolition (Preston's amendment, Jan 25, 1832), not on Randolph's plan. The narration now says exactly that.
- **Anti-literacy laws:** several came *before* Turner (GA 1829, NC and LA 1830, VA April 1831), so the narration says "more laws."
- ***State v. Mann*** is dated 1829 (13 N.C. 263).
- **"Three out of four"** holds for all 15 slave states. It's about seven in ten for the 11 Confederate states.
- **Domestic slave trade:** "roughly a million… most of them sold." Estimates run from 835,000 to over a million, and the sold share is debated.
- **Value of enslaved people:** $3.06 billion (HSUS) or $3.5 billion (Blight). It's more than railroads ($1.13 billion) and factories ($1.01 billion) combined either way. The chart uses the official HSUS figure.
- **World share of cotton:** no solid primary source exists for "two-thirds of the world's cotton" (figures from two-thirds to 80% circulate). The narration now says "three out of every four pounds of cotton in Britain's mills" (Beckert: 77% in the late 1850s; about 80% of 1860 imports).
- **Slaveholder counts:** the 1860 census's own national table misprints Arkansas (1,149 for 11,481). Corrected totals are 393,967 slaveholders and 47,571 planters in the 15 slave states.
