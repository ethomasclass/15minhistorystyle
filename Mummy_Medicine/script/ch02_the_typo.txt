In Persian and Arabic, {mumiya} was a mineral. A black, sticky pitch that oozed out of the mountains of Persia. Doctors in the Islamic world prescribed it for bruises and broken bones. No one was in it.

But the ancient Egyptians had used black resins and pitch on their dead. So when outsiders saw those dark, glossy bodies, they made a connection. Black stuff in the mountain. Black stuff on the mummy. Same thing, right?

In the 1100s, a scholar named Gerard of Cremona translated Arabic medical books into Latin, the language of Europe's doctors. And he described mumia as the stuff found in embalmed bodies.

Historians still argue over exactly who got it wrong first. But the result was the same. Somebody read pitch and wrote *people.* Mumia stopped meaning a mineral. It started meaning the mummy.
